Report: John Harbaugh called Amy Adams Strunk directly in an effort to land Jeffery Simmons

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The trade that sent defensive tackle Jeffery Simmons from the Titans to the Bears has an interesting — and unusual — postscript.

Jonathan Jones of The Athletic reports that Giants coach John Harbaugh directly called Titans owner Amy Adams Strunk in an effort to get Simmons to New York.

The call reportedly came after Giants G.M. Joe Schoen had contacted Titans G.M. Mike Borgonzi about Simmons, only to be told that the Titans weren't trading him.

As Jones notes, another G.M. who learned about the effort said it "violates a code" among football's decision-makers. One source in a key football position with one of the NFL's teams dubbed the effort "shameful."

Despite Borgonzi's claim from Thursday that the Titans weren't looking to trade Simmons until the Bears called on Wednesday night, Jones reports that the Titans' "braintrust" had a meeting on Tuesday during which they decided to start listening to offers for Simmons.

It's unclear whether Harbaugh made the call to Adams before or after Bears G.M. Ryan Poles offered a package that included a pair of first-round picks. We're told the Giants were willing to offer more than what the Bears ultimately sent to the Titans. (Of course, that's easy to say at this point.)

The entire situation underscores the value of pre-existing relationships when it comes to getting deals done. It was a point we discussed on Friday's PFT Live. In 2021, the trade that sent quarterback Matthew Stafford from the Lions to the Rams had a distinct "inside job" feel to it, given that Lions G.M. Brad Holmes had recently been hired from the Rams.

Other teams that would have been interested in attempting to acquire Stafford were surprised to learn that he had been quietly and quickly dealt to the Rams, who were able to tuck quarterback Jared Goff into the deal — and to discreetly attach extra compensation to get the Lions to take on a contract that, after only two seasons, the Rams had come to regret. The outcome prompted former Commanders G.M. Martin Mayhew to call every team in the league the following offseason to inquire as to whether their starting quarterback may be available in trade.

The ultimate question, when trades like this happen, is whether the relationship angle results in the team that gives up a key player getting as much as it could. If the Giants would have indeed offered more, shouldn't the Titans have fully explored that avenue? Ultimately, the longstanding relationship between Poles and Borgonzi may have superseded the effort to get as much as possible for Simmons.

Chocolate Caterpillar or Chocolate Teapot? Top up the disk space for Windows 10 to find out

Today's bit of signage silage is either Windows 10 protesting that it is not part of London's Oxford Street pedestrianization, or someone failing to heed an alert before the public is made all too aware that things aren't going well…

Eagle-eyed Register reader Aaron Colwill spotted the sign outside the food hall entrance of a Marks & Spencer store on London's Oxford Street. It usually entices passersby through the doors with promises of treats inside, but today it appears to require a different kind of feeding. Disk space is running low.

Marks and Spencer (also known as M&S and Marks and Sparks) is no stranger to cyber whoopsies, having been the victim of an attack in 2025 in which crooks made off with customer data. Later that year, it reckoned cleanup costs would be about £136 million.

That's quite a lot, but not enough to buy a bigger hard disk for Windows 10, it seems. Storage prices have increased over the years, but surely not that much?

Colwill reckoned that Windows 10 21H2 LTSC was behind the problems. It could well be. While support for 21H2 ended for many users in 2023, Windows 10 LTSC 2021 will carry on until Jan 12, 2027, meaning engineers might have an exciting festive period ahead of them. Windows 10 IoT Enterprise LTSC 2021 will receive extended support until Jan 13, 2032.

Either way, using Windows to advertise the Food Hall's wares seems slightly overkill. Simple posters, for example, never run out of disk space or attract the bork fairy's attention.

Perhaps they might use something that lacks the voracious appetite for storage that Microsoft's finest demands. According to the Windows biz, installing Windows 10 requires at least 32 GB of disk space, ideally more. It all seems a bit excessive for a cake-based caterpillar advert. Then again, when your operating system tends to have the resilience of a chocolate teapot, using it to advertise a chocolate insect might not be such a bad idea after all. ®

8 of Jamie Carragher’s biggest beefs: Aston Villa, Salah, Messi, Ronaldo…

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Jamie Carragher doesn’t tend to hold back when giving his opinions and as a result, several high-profile stars have taken a pop at him over the years.

Whether Carragher has said something to upset a player or he’s just rubbed them up the wrong way, there are plenty of reasons why the pundit has found himself in hot water.

Now we have the surreal plot twist of Aston Villa taking legal action against Carragher. Whatever next?

We’ve taken a closer look at Carragher and have found eight of his biggest beefs over the years, and to paraphrase Kiera Knightley: “they’re all of Salah.”

Aston Villa

Villa say they will initiate legal action against Carragher over what they describe as “unacceptable insults”.

Villa made the announcement in a statement on social media – signed by chairman Nassef Sawiris – in which they accused Carragher of insulting the club and their fans.

The club said they would ‘not allow anyone to stop our ascension in English and European football’.

It all came after Carragher said Manchester United should target Unai Emery as their next manager, pointing out they’ll always be bigger than Villa in football’s food chain.

It’s quite telling that even most Villa supporters have recognised their club has taken a sh*t with their clothes on over this one.

Mohamed Salah

Carragher and Salah started taking shots at each other after the former suggested Salah only spoke when it suited him, not willing to address the media about his and the Reds’ poor form in 2025.

The pundit said: “A year ago this weekend Mo Salah wasn’t shy in coming out and speaking about his own situation, about the club not offering him a contract.

“I only ever hear Salah speak when he gets Man of the Match, or he needs a new contract. I’d like to see Mo Salah come out as one of the leaders, one of the legends of Liverpool, come out and speak for the team. It shouldn’t always be the captain.”

Just weeks later, he was proven right, with Salah unleashing on Liverpool for dropping him and throwing him “under the bus.”

In the same interview, Salah was aware that he’d receive backlash from Carragher, stating: “After what I have done for the club it really hurts. You can imagine, really.

“After going from home to the club and you don’t know if you are starting. I know the club too well, I have been here many years. Tomorrow Carragher is going to go for me again and again and that’s fine.”

While Carragher has named Salah as one of Liverpool’s best players of all time, the pair also bickered about his contract situation in 2024-25.

Carragher took issue with Salah’s interview after the Southampton game in November 2024 and the pair began firing shots at one another.

“The most important thing is Liverpool winning the Premier League,” Carragher told Sky Sports.

“That is more important than any of those players, and if he continues to put comments out or his agent keeps putting cryptic tweets out, that’s selfish, that’s thinking about themselves and not the football club.”

They had another dig at one another following Carragher’s comments on the Egyptian forward.

“I think rather than speak about Trent Alexander-Arnold or Mo Salah I’d probably like to pay tribute to Virgil van Dijk,” Carragher said.

“I think the other two could maybe look at Virgil van Dijk and take a leaf out of his book.”

Salah bit back at Carragher’s comments on social media and replied by saying: “I’m starting to think you’re obsessed with me.”

Cristiano Ronaldo

Upon Ronaldo’s return to Old Trafford, Carragher was vocal in his criticism of the Portuguese icon.

“I’ve been critical of the Ronaldo signing since he came in and I said that Ten Hag should really be moving on from that,” Carragher told Sky Sports in 2022.

When the pair came face to face on the pitch at Old Trafford, Ronaldo famously aired the Liverpool pundit while greeting Roy Keane and Gary Neville.

“Trust me, Cristiano would’ve seen those comments and would’ve been thinking walking over there I’ll just let this guy know with a little… he needs to kind of calm him down, humiliate him and show him up,” Rio Ferdinand said when commenting on the situation.

“Cristiano is very calculated, he’s very aware of what’s going on, he’s not somebody who sits at home and switches off.”

Lionel Messi

While Carragher often speaks highly of Messi, he found himself in hot water with the Argentine superstar following the comments he’d made in 2022.

Carragher was critical of PSG’s approach in Europe and was adamant that the club couldn’t win the Champions League with a front three of Messi, Neymar and Kylian Mbappe.

Messi clearly took issue with those comments as he took to Instagram to call Carragher a ‘donkey’ in the DMs.

Virgil van Dijk

During an interview with Gary Neville in 2022, Van Dijk claimed that Carragher in his prime wouldn’t even get on their bench.

“He [Carragher] played many games for Liverpool but in the modern game I think Matip and Konate would be ahead of him,” Van Dijk said.

Carragher sat on those comments for a few months and fired back at Van Dijk after Liverpool were dumped out of the Champions League by Real Madrid.

“What makes me laugh is Virgil van Dijk said I wouldn’t get in that back four about two months ago. I think I’d take his place at the moment,” Carragher told CBS Sports.

Rio Ferdinand

Since both becoming pundits, Ferdinand and Carragher have continued to fire the odd shot at one another over the years.

The beef between the pair started when Carragher referred to Ferdinand as a “clown” when appearing on CBS Sports.

Ferdinand then couldn’t resist having a pop at Carragher after the Liverpool pundit kept quiet about Steven Gerrard’s move to Saudi Arabia, despite being critical of others making the move.

“Steven Gerrard’s gone. You used to carry his boots and his bags at Anfield to games. Your boy’s gone over there, I’ve not heard a peep out of you,” Ferdinand said.

“I’ve not heard how disappointed you are. I want to hear him [Carragher to] say, ‘I can’t believe Stevie’s gone there’.”

Carragher quickly bit back at Ferdinand’s comments on social media and fired a dig at the former Manchester United star in the process.

“Never criticised Silva, [Karim] Benzema, [Ruben] Neves or SG [Gerrard], I don’t like Saudi trying to buy football like they have Golf. I knew you’d be all over it Rio, like you were in Qatar!”

Jose Mourinho

Mourinho once famously said: “Jamie Carragher talks too much for someone who isn’t even among the top 1,000 defenders in football history.

“How can someone who never won the Premier [League] talk about what it takes to win the Premier [League]?”

After Mourinho’s comments resurfaced last year, Carragher responded by inviting the Portuguese manager onto Monday Night Football.

“I’m currently trying to get him to come on MNF,” Carragher said in response to the Special One.

We’re still waiting for that episode of MNF.

Casemiro

Carragher didn’t hold back on his assessment of the Brazilian last season, insisting that the 32-year-old was finished at the top level.

“I think Casemiro should know tonight that he should only have another three games left at the top level,” Carragher said in May last year.

“I always remember the saying ‘leave the football before the football leaves you’. The football has left him. At this top level, he needs to call it a day at this level and move.”

While Casemiro didn’t specifically name Carragher, the Brazilian did hit out at the “disrespectful” criticism he’d been receiving.

“The criticism is disrespectful,” Casemiro told Sky Sports.

“So, when it’s lacking respect, then it’s worrying, and I don’t have to respect that either. But there is always going to be criticism in football, especially playing for such a long time, people want to criticise.

“But from the moment when it lacks respect, that’s when you lose my respect.”

The forest sounds empty, but I know it’s not. Earlier today, I’d walked along the lakeshore and seen hoof prints, a cougar track, and coyote scat.

The trees have all turned black beneath the night sky. The only light comes from the full moon creeping through clouds. I’m deep in Washington’s Gifford National Forest and it’s so quiet I can hear my own heartbeat.

No creature is giving its location away by making noise—but we certainly aren’t alone as we stand on the edge of a lake, its waters as dark as midnight. I look at the shadows between trees and wonder what’s hiding in the forest.

The Gifford Pinchot National Forest, which the group frequents on their hunts for Bigfoot (Photo: Emily Halnon)

Beside me, Tanner Hoskins raises a thermal scope to his eye and scans the trees to look for heat signatures. He pauses as he sweeps to the far corner of the lake.

“I think I’ve got something moving,” he says.

For the last six years, Hoskins and a group of friends have been searching the Pacific Northwest’s wilderness for an answer to that very question: What else is out there? 

And I’m tagging along for a weekend to see what happens when I go looking for evidence of the unknown.

About two miles away, as the crow flies, a dispersed campsite is tucked into a grove of pine trees.

On a July night in 2020, decades-long buddies Kelly Stolp and Mike Palagi sat around a fire with Stolp’s sons and Palagi’s fiancée. Flames licked the black sky as they laughed into the night. It was a familiar scene for the friends, who met in the Army in 2005.

Stolp retired to his tent while the fire was still crackling. Deep in the night, he heard a loud, guttural howl—it sounded like a car engine trying to turn over. Then, footsteps. Slow and steady, getting closer to his tent. They seemed too loud to be human. More stomp than step. He could feel each footfall hit the earth. But he knew, without a hair of doubt, they were coming from a two-legged creature.

Stolp grew up on a 35,000-acre ranch in Montana. He’s hunted for decades and recreated outside since he could walk. He went on his first backpacking trip at the age of 12. He’s an avid outdoorsman and familiar with all of the wildlife that roams the West. A grizzly bear once came into his camp. But in that moment, he knew he wasn’t hearing a bear, nor a cougar, elk, or coyote.

Stolp listened as the creature approached his tent, brushing up against the huckleberries just feet away. It was only 50 degrees, but he was covered in sweat. Then, he heard the footsteps disappearing up the dry creekbed that winds past the campsite.

The next morning, he told Palagi, “Something came through camp last night.” It was large and two-legged, he went on. Palagi laughed, said he didn’t hear anything, and pointed at the empty bottle of whiskey as an explanation. Stolp dropped the topic for the rest of the day.

He could feel each footfall hit the earth. But he knew, without a hair of doubt, they were coming from a two-legged creature.

But at 3 A.M. that night, Stolp heard the footsteps again. Thump. Thump. Thump. The same guttural howl was now coming from two different spots.

He sat up and said, “Mike. It’s back.”

He heard a gun racking, then: “Kelly, I’m up.”

“Two legs or four?”

Palagi didn’t hesitate: “Bipedal, man.”

“How big?”

“Really fucking big.”

They rolled out of their tents. Stolp shouted, “We’re armed, show yourself.”

Palagi and Stolp both trained as military snipers and knew how to clear a space. They moved through camp, weapons ready. They found nothing, but the two men, who don’t scare easily, passed the rest of the night in their cars.

“I’ve spent my entire life outdoors and encountered everything the wilderness has to offer,” said Stolp. “But I’d never slept in my truck, not even when a grizzly came through my camp.”

They tried to recreate the stomping sound in the morning. They were not small men. Palagi played football in Montana as a defensive lineman, and Stolp looks like he could have been on a team, too. But even when Palagi jumped with his full body weight, he couldn’t come close to what they’d heard.

Palagi watches the fire at camp (Photo: Emily Halnon)

Stolp headed to the Shriners Children’s Hospital in Portland the next Monday, where he worked as an electrician alongside Palagi, a maintenance engineer. He pulled his coworker, a security guard named Hoskins, aside.

“But you have to promise you won’t tell anyone,” he told Hoskins. He knew how ridiculous the story sounded. He didn’t want anyone to think he believed in Bigfoot.

But Hoskins was more of a believer. While he was growing up, his great-grandmother had warned him about “hidebehinds,” a large, mysterious creature that was blamed for the disappearance of loggers in Idaho. Stolp’s story, when added to ones told by his great-grandmother, made him wonder if the Pacific Northwest was home to similar creatures. Hoskins swore he wouldn’t say a thing. Then he went home and built a website for the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search, telling anyone who found it about his friend’s story—and inviting others to share their own.

Tales of large, mysterious, forest-dwelling creatures have swirled around the world for centuries. Bigfoot’s other most-used moniker, Sasquatch, is an anglicized version of “Sasq’ets”, a word from the Coast Salish People, a tribe that lives in British Columbia. Versions of this mythical being, which goes by many different Indigenous names, have traveled across generations of stories, as both a large, hairy animal and a spiritual guide.

Today, Bigfoot has grown into something else entirely—as has the hunt to prove Bigfoot exists. Its likeness has been slapped on stickers, shirts, and road crossing signs. The Bigfoot subreddit boasts nearly 300,000 members. There are Bigfoot podcasts, conferences, symposiums, and a seven-night Bigfoot cruise that features daily lectures with Bigfoot researchers. Animal Planet released 11 seasons of the popular show Finding Bigfoot, and the Travel Channel has aired 59 episodes (and counting) of the docuseries Expedition Bigfoot.

There’s no question that Bigfoot has captured the imagination of many, but whether Bigfoot has any foothold in reality remains a mystery.

There are thousands of reports alleging Bigfoot’s existence; most of them focus on one creature, though the common belief within the Bigfoot community is that Bigfoot encompasses an entire species, like wolves, bears, or cougars. The Bigfoot Field Research Organization has collected more than 5,000 sightings through its online database. Washington state leads the charge, with over 700 reports. For decades, people have circulated grainy photographs, videos, and stories. But if Bigfoot is real, he (or she) remains elusive and incredibly camera-shy.

Bigfoot hunters, like Hoskins and crew, want science to back them up. “Science wants a body” is a common refrain in Sasquatch circles. Most aren’t actually rooting for the death of Bigfoot, but if he happened to peacefully pass away in an easily findable location, that would really help them out.

Bigfoot hunters might be lacking scientific proof, but they do have some trained scientists on their side, including tenured professors like the late anthropologists Grover Krantz and Jeffrey Meldrum, who both spent years researching the creature. Even Jane Goodall said she was open-minded about the existence of Bigfoot, and believed some of the stories she heard, adding that it was compelling that every continent has stories about a Bigfoot-type creature.

The lake near camp at night (Photo: Emily Halnon)

When Hoskins launched the website for the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search, he invited people to submit tales of their own encounters. He shared the site widely through social media channels and online Bigfoot groups, as well as in more general outdoors, fishing, and hunting forums. He also used geotags on Instagram during the group’s early searches, which generated some of the first reports from people who’d experienced supposed encounters near the same areas.

Since 2021, Hoskins and the rest of the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search have received 287 reports via the website and social media, covering everything from footprints and vocalizations to alleged sightings. “You have to take a lot of them with a grain of salt,” he told me. But he finds some reports compelling, especially the ones from people who want to remain anonymous, like loggers, law enforcement, and government employees.

The Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search has grown beyond solely an online database. The crew has headed out regularly, every month or two for the last five years, looking for evidence of unknown, large, bipedal creatures across the Pacific Northwest. The four men behind the Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search include Hoskins, Stolp, and Palagi, who all once worked at Shriners together, along with Alberto Quinones, a warehouse manager who married Hoskins’ cousin. They’ve all become best friends, Hoskins says.

At 30 years old, Quinones, who usually sports a black hoodie and mustache, is the youngest of the group. Stolp, 48, is the oldest and a father of two, who commutes to Texas where he manages a multibillion-dollar construction project. He’s been out of the Army for over a decade, but his haircut could probably still pass a military inspection. His Army buddy, Palagi, likes to challenge his friends to cook-offs and lives with his three cats and dog at his home outside Portland. And Hoskins, also a father of two, is the de facto leader. He doesn’t relax until everyone’s made it to camp and always requests texts to make sure everyone got home safe after a trip. He likes to say that he “looks like a cop,” though he was denied a career in law enforcement because of severe scoliosis and an amputated right leg.

The four men don’t seem like typical Bigfoot hunters. For starters, half of them don’t think Bigfoot is real. Palagi calls himself the greatest skeptic of the group, saying, “I’ll drill holes in their theories all day long.” And Stolp is quick to clarify that he “doesn’t believe in that shit.” As for that camping trip in 2020, Palagi has an explanation: “A deer pouncing with both legs.”

And the loud, guttural howl: “A territorial bullfrog on the deer’s back.”

Even Hoskins, who spearheaded the formation of their Bigfoot search, says he’s only at 80 percent on the Bigfoot-believometer until he sees one for himself.

A washout pitches my car sideways at a precarious angle. A tunnel of overgrown bushes and trees scratches at my van. The rutted old logging road I’m following, full of creek crossings and potholes, turns my drive into a carnival ride. Hoskins warned me I’d need the right rig to get into camp to join the crew for a two-night Bigfoot search. I’m driving my 2011 Toyota Sienna, to which, Hoskins tells me, “I’ve never seen a minivan on that road.” But it’s all-wheel drive and equipped with an aftermarket lift kit and beefier tires for extra clearance. So far, it’s getting the job done, even if my heart rate is accelerating with every ditch I have to drive over. I wonder if this will be the scariest part of the weekend.

As I drive farther into the Gifford Pinchot National Forest, the trees seem to grow taller and thicker as the road narrows. I’m 47 miles from the nearest gas station, and each washout feels like a “do not enter” sign. I can’t help thinking that this densely wooded forest in the shadow of Mount Adams is where I’d come if I never wanted to be found.

I’m not a Bigfoot believer. But I’m curious about some of the stories I’ve heard that don’t have an easy explanation. Like, what exactly stomped through the guys’ campsite in 2020, howling like no creature they’d ever met.

The 1.3-million-acre Gifford Pinchot, where I’m meeting Hoskins and crew, is a hotbed for Bigfoot reports. It’s home to the aptly named Ape Canyon, where gold prospectors allegedly encountered seven-foot apelike animals in 1924. There’s also Skookum Meadows, where scientists found a body imprint left in the mud in 2000 that they attributed to an “unknown primate” because they couldn’t connect it to any animal. There were so many Bigfoot reports around here in the 1960s that Skamania County passed an ordinance to protect Bigfoot from harm, punishable by jail time or a hefty fine, which still stands today. The original ordinance acknowledged the increasing volume of “well-armed” Bigfoot hunters and expressed concern that said firearms could threaten both Bigfoot and humans traveling through the area.

Palagi, Hoskins, and Quinones at camp (Photo: Emily Halnon)

Finally, the narrow road opens to a small clearing in the trees, where two vehicles are parked a stone’s throw from a fire ring. Hoskins and Quinones are tossing wood onto a campfire. The ground is damp from 24 hours of rain. This summer’s fire ban has been lifted, and the group is taking full advantage.

At five-foot-two, Hoskins is about a foot shorter than Quinones. His work pants hang loose over his prosthetic leg, and he holds a walking stick in his left arm. He moves around the campsite with confident steps as he grabs his chainsaw and returns to the pile of firewood.

I walk over and see that a folding table is covered with their go-to supplies for Bigfoot hunts: thermal scopes, drones, casting materials, trail cameras, and audio recorders.

Hoskins has a handgun on his hip. Quinones has a shotgun in his car. They’re always armed, says Hoskins, though more for humans than Bigfoot. He mentions the Green River Killer, who murdered 49 women in the Pacific Northwest in the eighties and nineties, and left some of the bodies in the forests, which were later found by hikers.

It’s not unusual for campers to be armed in the woods this time of year. It’s hunting season in Washington. I’ve got a bright pink fleece and neon orange hat packed, thanks to Hoskins reminding me to bring colorful gear. He, on the other hand, is wearing a camouflage jacket and light brown pants. Quinones is dressed in all black.

“Bigfoot hunting is like playing the lottery, and the only way to really play is to go camping.”

As Hoskins fiddles with the thermal camera, he tells me that it took four years after that first encounter for them to find anything that could be pinned to Bigfoot. Fittingly, it was a big footprint, measuring 16 inches from heel-to-toe and 7 inches wide, that they stumbled upon in the woods. That’s roughly equivalent to a U.S. men’s size 26.

“That’s a long time to keep looking with no results,” I say.

“Well,” Hoskins starts. “There are only 2,000 Bigfoot creatures across North America.”

It’s unclear how exactly that estimate was generated, considering there isn’t definitive proof of a single Bigfoot. It’s also unclear what the plural of “Bigfoot” is. Bigfeet? Bigfoots? Bigfootuses?

“There are 30,000 bears in Oregon,” he goes on. “How often do you see one?”

I think back to the last bear I saw. It was in May. And the only one I’ve seen this year, despite spending most weekends, and plenty of weekdays, on trails around my home in Oregon.

“Bigfoot hunting is like playing the lottery, and the only way to really play is to go camping,” says Hoskins.

“I mostly said yes to my first search because I love camping,” says Quinones, who tells me that he grew up in a family that sees the outdoors as more of a threat than a fun pastime. When he moved to Sherwood, Oregon, from Los Angeles as a teenager, his main social options were hanging out in parking lots or around trees. “I chose trees,” he says and gestures at the many pine trees towering over us.

“I’d stopped getting outside until Kelly shared his encounter,” Hoskins adds.

Hoskins says he used to love driving deep into the forest and spending the night outside. But in his twenties, he fell into a different routine. Most of his friends preferred video games or dive bars to camping trips.

After Stolp told him about his 2020 encounter, Hoskins seized the opportunity to drive his rig back into the woods and set up camp. He also lost his great-grandmother in 2021, who was a parental figure through a childhood affected by addiction. He found himself even more drawn to the outdoors after her death.

“I spent weekends in the trees just screaming, crying, and staring at the fire,” he says. “I think being out here is the best thing for you when you’re going through shit; your mind actually has space to process what you’re feeling.”

The Pacific Northwest Bigfoot Search hikes into the forest (Photo: Emily Halnon)

He’s not the only one in the group to use these camping trips to process grief, stress, or other hardships. “Some weekends, we’re all just sharing the tough things we’re going through around the campfire,” he says.

“It feels like the only place we can talk about everything, down to the really, really hard stuff,” says Stolp.

“The news is garbage, social media is garbage, everything is garbage,” Stolp continues. “The only thing that feels real and meaningful, other than my family, is going into the woods with my buddies. I think a connection with the outdoors is probably what a lot of the world is missing.”

Hoskins sweeps his arm around the campsite, which, short of the thermal scope and casting materials, looks like any weekend campsite, with camp chairs, packed coolers, and plentiful BBQ fixings. This location is a favorite of the group, I’m told, both for its idyllic setting on an alpine lake and for the ideal Bigfoot terrain that surrounds it: remote wilderness, very few humans, and thick forests. Also important is what they all describe as “weird energy.”

“Something about this camp is just creepy,” says Palagi. “At night, there’s just a weird feeling, maybe it’s because you can’t see very far. But the energy just shifts after the sun goes down.”

They’ve been to this particular spot so many times that they constructed a makeshift outhouse on a tree stump downhill from camp. Though Hoskins warns me not to use it, thanks to the local fire ant population taking a liking to it.

“I think it’s incredibly important to be good stewards of the land,” Stolp tells me, adding that they often pack out more than they pack in.

Hoskins checks the battery on the audio recorder. We’ll leave it out overnight to see if it captures any “vocalizations,” like the howl heard back in 2020. Bigfoot is also rumored to be a wood knocker, screamer, and whistler, depending on whose accounts you read or hear.

“Witching hour is from 11 P.M. to 3 A.M.,” he says.

I pull my hood up as the rain picks up. The shadows are growing long on the forest floor.

Hoskins tells me he was thrilled to see rain in the forecast for the weekend—not just because it’s good for campfires, but because “rain is great for prints.”

Last month, the group found a 12-inch footprint in the forest adjacent to our campsite. That’s equivalent to a size 14, which is less outrageous than the size-26 print, but still suspicious, according to Hoskins.

“I assume they aren’t born eight-feet tall,” he says. “That would be a very painful birth experience.”

He promises to show me where they found the footprint tomorrow.

“Tonight,” he says, “We’ll go down to the lake after it gets dark.”

The Gifford Pinchot isn’t just a hub for Bigfoot activity.

In 2021, a family reached out to Hoskins because their loved one, Glenn Austin Oldfield II, had gone missing in 2015 while camping alone in the Gifford Pinchot. When they saw the group was spending so much time in the same area, they asked if the Bigfoot hunters could keep an eye out for any sign of him.

Inspired by the request, Hoskins reached out to the Washington State Patrol, and they sent him a list of 34 people who’d been reported missing in the Gifford Pinchot since 1979.

It was then that the group launched the Pacific Northwest Missing Persons Project to look for evidence of missing persons and bring closure to cold cases. They realized that the same skills they use to look for abnormal footprints and disturbed soil could help them find scraps of clothing or human bones. They search in the Gifford Pinchot and beyond—wherever their hunt for Bigfoot takes them—or wherever there’s a family in need. Hoskins compiled the names of 500 people missing across Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Montana, and California. Every time they go out, they’re armed with lists of clothing and belongings.

“I’ve been told I can’t do a lot of things. But no one can stop me from being out here.”

They see their work as picking up the trail where search and rescue leaves it, since most SAR squads lack the funding to continue the hunt after a mission moves from a search to a recovery.

“I want these families to get the closure they deserve,” says Hoskins.

Many families have gone years without answers. Some of them tell Hoskins they’ve been moved from detective to detective, while others can’t get anyone to answer their calls.

For Hoskins, searching for missing people gives him the purpose he’s been unable to find in his career. Hoskins was born with severe scoliosis and had 14 surgeries as a kid. His right leg was amputated when he was a toddler. He uses a hiking stick to get around, and has to physically lift his amputated leg over many of the logs that we crawl over throughout the weekend.

He wanted to serve in the military or go into law enforcement, but he couldn’t pass the physical fitness tests. He got within 30 seconds of completing the course Oregon requires for police candidates, but no matter how hard he trained, he couldn’t do it any faster. He was denied by every branch of the military, he told me. When he was young, doctors told him he wouldn’t be able to walk or run.

“I’ve been told I can’t do a lot of things,” he said. “But no one can stop me from being out here.”

The Pacific Northwest Missing Persons Project is currently pursuing three active searches and has a waitlist of over 100 families. They’re mostly limited by time and gas money, Hoskins says; he would take on every case if he could. Last fall, they found human bones during one of their searches. They’re still waiting on law enforcement to identify them.

A curtain of mist shrouds the lake in the early morning. The night before, we’d set up an audio recorder on the shore and searched the forest for heat and sound. I don’t know if it was the campfire stories or the “weird energy” vibes, but I wasn’t upset that Hoskins had that handgun on his hip as we slipped into the shadows of the dark forest. Quinones recounted how they once heard a voice, “clear as day,” saying “hello,” as they walked through the woods. It’s part of the legend: Bigfoot will mimic human voices or animal sounds. We didn’t find any sign of Bigfoot last night. Still, I didn’t get out of my van to pee until the sun was up.

This morning, we climb over the deadfall that guards the lake and bushwhack through its marshy edges. We walk over broken tree limbs, foot-long pinecones, and downed logs, looking for disturbances or anything that seems odd: boulders that have been moved, branches that have been woven together, or footprints that seem too big and too out-of-place to be human.

The lake in the Gifford Pinchot National Forest (Photo: Emily Halnon)

“Basically anything that looks like nature didn’t do it,” says Hoskins. The crew draws from their many years of military service, backcountry hunting, and outdoor navigation to look for clues during their searches.

“I’m constantly scanning 20 feet in front of me, looking for breaks, looking for grass pushed down, watching game trails, seeing if there’s a track,” says Stolp.

As we hike, the guys point at the foliage, which is starting to change color, and the hillside that rises above us. Quinones offers to take me up to the ridge so I can see the view. “It’s just gorgeous out here,” says Hoskins.

I get the sense that if searching for Bigfoot involved cities, smog, and skyscrapers, they’d quickly lose interest.

After a lot of off-trail hiking, we get to the shore of the lake and scan the softer, more malleable ground for prints. There are hoofprints galore. But after searching banks of mud, we spy something much bigger than a hoof.

“That could be a deer butt,” says Hoskins. “But let’s cast it to find out.”

He pours plaster into each depression in the soil. We’ll pick up the hardened casts tomorrow to see if they reveal anything promising, like toes, or a whole footprint.

Hoskins then leads us to where they found a footprint last month. When they discovered it, they had Quinones try to match it.

Hoskins sets the cast (Photo: Emily Halnon)

“He’s our go-to guy when we’re trying to gauge whether a large human could’ve left the print,” says Hoskins of Quinones’s six-foot-two height and 250-pound build. “He couldn’t come close.”

“Our first step is to try to debunk anything that we find,” he says. “I can’t say that it wasn’t a human.” Then, he gestures to the ground, which is covered in debris right up to the mud, before sweeping his arm over the lake, which is more bug bog than pristine water.

“But would you walk around here barefoot?”

Palagi shifts his gaze towards the canopy and asks, “You ever look up while you’re out here?” His tone suggests he’s gearing up to spook me, not to point out the scenery.

It’s the next morning, and we’re walking back to the lake to fetch the cast. Palagi drove into camp a few hours ago while an owl was still hooting from a nearby tree. He had to work all weekend, so he told Hoskins he’d get here before we woke up to make breakfast. I thought there was no way Palagi would show up, just to cook us some eggs. Our campsite was a two-hour drive from his home outside Portland, each way. But I saw his headlights cutting through the night while I was still in my sleeping bag. Before I was out of my van, he had his entire camp kitchen out, along with fixings for breakfast sandwiches, and more wood for the fire.

“There’s definitely a crisis going on for men right now,” says Stolp, as he talks about why he keeps coming back to these woods. “And we’ve found a brotherhood through going out in the woods and looking for something that may or may not exist.”

I look up at the canopy and ask Palagi: “Does Bigfoot like to hang out up there?”

“No,” he scoffs. “But cougars do.”

“There’s definitely mystery out here,” Palagi goes on, as he looks around the trees that surround us.

Palagi tells me he plans to stop telling the tale of that camping trip in 2020. He’s sick of talking about something that he doesn’t believe in.

But he’s still out here—and even the “greatest skeptic of all” thinks about that question when he’s walking around this forest, he says: What else is out there?

Hoskins points to tracks in the dirt (Photo: Emily Halnon)

It’s like a treasure hunt every time they come out, Stolp tells me, like being a kid again. And, minus the loaded firearms, this camping trip is reminiscent of scenes from my childhood, when I’d wander through the woods of Vermont looking for my next pirate ship or dragon’s den in the trees and boulders. The possibilities felt endless every time I stepped into the forest.

And maybe that’s a big part of why these guys come back again and again. Whether we believe in Bigfoot or not, there’s an appeal to searching for the unknown. We’re wired to stare at the ocean, the night sky—and a dark forest—and wonder what’s out there. We like thinking about that question, even if it comes with goosebumps on occasion. But it’s easy to lose touch with that in all of the other wires of life.

Hoskins grabs a toothbrush and goes to work on the cast. He gives a play-by-play while he dusts off the hardened plaster, “Indent, another indent.”

When he holds it up for its dramatic reveal, everyone stops talking so he can tell us what he found. I want it to be a large and indisputable footprint. But I know it’s hard to win the lottery on one ticket.

“It’s an unidentifiable blob,” Hoskins says. “Most of them are.” I’m disappointed, of course, but I realize that even the biggest, most undeniable footprint wouldn’t really answer anything. Not even another size 26 track would stop anyone from coming back to the forest—between deadlines, commutes, and night shifts—to walk through the trees and look for reasons to believe the world is still full of wonder.

Everest 1971: Ambition, Death, and Dispute

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In the spring of 1971, only one party attempted to climb Everest. Led by Norman Dyhrenfurth and Jimmy Roberts, this ambitious expedition planned to split into two groups. One party would go for the steep Southwest Face, while the other would attempt the West Ridge.

It was meant to be as much a test of international cooperation as an extraordinary climbing effort. But what happened turned out to be more complicated and controversial than anyone had expected.

Before the team even arrived in Nepal, the venture was heavily commercialized and advertised to the public. The BBC secured exclusive television rights and embedded a nine-person film crew directly into the expedition. At the same time, the British newspaper rights were sold to The Sunday Times, which sent along prominent journalists like Murray Sayle. The media machinery relentlessly hyped the expedition beforehand, boldly framing the climb as a mere formality and setting public expectations for a historic success.

Difficult goals

The expedition itself came from a merger of two plans. A British-Norwegian group asked Roberts to lead a Southwest Face attempt. Dyhrenfurth was already organizing the same route, so they joined forces. As the combined team was large, they decided to try two routes at once.

The Southwest Face was the main goal, specifically aiming for a direct, vertical line (a direttissima), while the other group targeted the complete, aesthetic West Ridge route. Both goals were very difficult, and climbers chose their preferred route.

Jimmy Roberts in Nepal. Photo: The Nepal Times

 

The Southwest Face team included British climbers Dougal Haston and Don Whillans, Japanese climbers Naomi Uemura and Reizo Ito, and others. The West Ridge team included Austrian Wolfgang Axt, Swiss climbers Michel and Yvette Vaucher, Norwegian climbers Odd Eliassen and Jon Teigland, American David Isles, and Indian Major Harsh Vardhan Bahuguna. Frenchman Pierre Mazeaud and Italian Carlo Mauri later joined the West Ridge side.

In total, the whole expedition had 31 foreign climbers from a dozen countries, as well as several Sherpas, including Ang Phurba and Sonam Girme. According to early planning notes, neither leader had personal summit ambitions. Both described themselves as the older men of the team.

The Southwest Face of Everest. Photo: Wikimedia

 

Setting up the routes

Base Camp was established on March 22, 1971, at 5,425m. Both teams worked through the Khumbu Icefall. Dyhrenfurth later said it was in the most difficult and dangerous state he had ever seen it. They set up Advance Base Camp on the Western Cwm, then separated.

The Southwest Face team pushed higher. The West Ridge team, with Axt as coordinator, began setting up their own camps, although supplies arrived slowly. Some of the West Ridge climbers didn’t want to carry heavy loads. A long storm in late April made conditions worse for both teams and delayed everything by two to three weeks. Dyhrenfurth’s own account in the American Alpine Journal confirms the early harmony among the team and the exceptional difficulties of the Icefall that spring.

The 1971 Everest expedition. Photo: 'Surrender to Everest'/imdb.com

 

One member dies

On April 18, Bahuguna and Axt of the West Ridge team were descending from a high camp in a storm at about 7,000m. They were not tied to each other. Axt had left his harness and climbing rope higher up to save weight, knowing fixed ropes were already in place. He crossed a long fixed-rope traverse by hand, waited for Bahuguna, saw him wave, then continued down because he was exhausted. He later heard screams and raised the alarm.

A rescue party reached Bahuguna still clipped to the fixed rope, about 25m from easier ground. He had lost a mitten and a crampon, and his face was covered by ice. Attempts to lower him failed in the storm and darkness, and he finally died of cold and exhaustion. Bahuguna’s fingers and face were crusted with ice, his eyes were wide, and he could not recognize Whillans or speak.

Major Harsh Vardhan Bahuguna on the West Ridge. Photo: Wolfgang Axt/The Himalayan Journal

 

According to Roberts’ report in the Himalayan Database, the rescuers had to leave Bahuguna because it would be completely dark in 10 minutes, and the others would freeze too. Roberts later described the death as the result of a series of mistakes by Bahuguna and Axt themselves, and noted that allowing them to go so high alone had been a factor.

Bahuguna’s body was later recovered and cremated near Base Camp.

Inquiry and blame

Dyhrenfurth held an inquiry after the fatal incident. Axt said that the fixed ropes made roping up unnecessary and that he didn’t realize how serious things were until the screams. He later explained that if he had stayed longer, he risked losing his own fingers and toes.

“What should I do? One hour later and I am frozen,” he said. He added that he couldn’t pull Bahuguna himself and had to go down for fresh men.

Mazeaud called it murder through negligence. Dyhrenfurth agreed that Axt should have gone back to look, but said that it was easy to judge after the event. Roberts noted that Bahuguna’s death was very sad but didn’t determine the expedition's final outcome.

Norman Dyhrenfurth, Carlo Mauri, Yvette Vaucher, Reizo Ito, Pierre Mazeaud, and Michale Vaucher in 'Surrender to Everest' (1971). Photo: imdb.com

 

After the death and the storm, most of the remaining West Ridge climbers (especially Mazeaud, Mauri and the Vauchers) wanted to abandon both hard routes and switch to the normal South Col route. They said that it was more practical and gave a better chance of reaching the summit.

Many others, including most of the Southwest Face group and the leading Sherpas, believed that those climbers mainly wanted personal success: first Frenchman, first Italian, or first woman. A vote that included the Sherpas went strongly in favor of putting all remaining effort into the Southwest Face. The West Ridge group said the decision was a plot to keep them off the summit, so the press would stay focused on the Southwest Face.

Camp 2. Pumori on the left, with snow-and-ice slopes leading to the West Ridge. Photo: Wolfgang Axt/The Himalayan Journal

 

More accusations

Mazeaud’s radio message said that they expected him -- a French member of parliament -- to work like a Sherpa for Anglo-Saxons and Japanese. He said France had been insulted. Yvette Vaucher called the decision monstrous and said she had lost Everest and her friends at the same time.

According to one account, Roberts told Mazeaud to leave during a loud argument. The four went home in early May, ending further work on the West Ridge. Isles and Teigland later said that the decision to cancel the West Ridge climb, made in a highly emotional moment after Bahuguna’s death, was a great mistake. Teigland said he had dreamed of the West Ridge for two years.

Sickness, and another attempt

A virus, diagnosed by some as mononucleosis, spread through the camps and forced many climbers down, including Dyhrenfurth. Bad weather continued to block supplies. With the West Ridge group gone and fewer people left, Haston and Whillans kept pushing on the Southwest Face. Japanese climbers and Sherpas carried heavy loads high on the mountain. Camps were placed up to approx. 8,290m.

Haston and Whillans fixed ropes partway into a chimney in the Rock Band but ran out of rope, oxygen, and food. According to Roberts, they placed Camp 6 at 8,290m and were making progress every day. He said they could have traversed onto the South Buttress and reached the summit that way in one day, but that was not the route they had come to climb. Extreme cold hampered them. They needed another camp higher up. They turned back 450m short of the summit on May 21.

Roberts later said weather was the basic cause of failure: Once it goes bad, it affects everything else. He described the expedition as 50 percent successful. They failed to summit, but those who stayed worked well together.

Naomi Uemura. Photo: Wikipedia

 

Different views after the climb

Roberts said that he was bitterly disappointed after three years of work, but apart from Bahuguna’s death, he had nothing to regret. He criticized Schloemmer for contributing almost nothing and said that Axt was asked to come up and help on the Southwest Face but thought it over and went down instead.

Haston called claims that the Austrians were ready to help “complete balls.” He said that when they asked for support at Camp 5, Schloemmer replied that he needed a Sherpa to carry his personal gear. Haston added that he and Whillans were the only ones who could push hard enough to get the route going, and that the Japanese were the only others with real high-Himalayan experience.

Uemura said that he and Ito helped by carrying loads because that was their obligation, and that if the weather had been good, the expedition could have succeeded. After the 1971 expedition, Uemura decided to always climb solo thereafter.

No real authority

Axt and Schloemmer complained there was no real authority above Advance Base Camp. Axt said the two British stayed high for three weeks and monopolized the lead, and that without a coordinator to rotate people, there was no room for others. He said if there had been good organization on the Face, he could have succeeded. Isles and Teigland said the international aspect had nothing to do with the failure.

The problems came from personalities and the emotional decision after the death. Mazeaud and the Vauchers maintained that the leaders had broken the original plan of two routes and prevented them from reaching the summit for publicity reasons. Dyhrenfurth said the experiment in international climbing was only partially successful, but cooperation had been good until the argument over the route. He noted that the United Nations had tried international cooperation for a long time and had not done too well, either.

Post-mortem

In their interesting and detailed post-climb article titled Post-Mortem of an International Expedition for Mountain magazine (later reprinted by The Himalayan Club), authors Ken Wilson and Mike Pearson traced exactly how the initial merger and massive team size doomed the expedition from the start. They concluded that the 1971 expedition's collapse was primarily driven by severe natural forces, specifically adverse weather and illness, rather than interpersonal friction. They also attributed systemic blame to organizers for combining distinct climbing factions into an unmanageable, oversized team.

Memorial at Everest in 1971 for Harsh Bahuguna. Photo: Wolfgang Axt/The Himalayan Journal

 

The questions remain: Was Bahuguna’s death mainly the result of mistakes by the two men involved, or something the leaders should have prevented? Did the decision to drop the West Ridge and focus on the Southwest Face save the expedition or destroy it? And was the international idea itself the problem, or simply the mix of strong personalities, bad weather, and the pressure of being on Everest?
