2027 Nissan Rogue Hybrid Has a Comfort Feature Its Biggest Rivals Lack

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A Chauffeur Without the Chauffeur

One of the notable features in the 2027 Nissan Rogue Hybrid is the e-Power system, a series hybrid that uses electric motors to drive the wheels while the engine serves as a generator. As such, the compact crossover delivers an EV-like driving experience minus the range anxiety.

But aside from the smoothness of EV-like operation, Nissan is championing a new feature called Smooth Stop. Inspired by a technique known as a “limo stop,” the feature automatically reduces braking pressure just before the vehicle comes to a complete stop, minimizing the forward-and-backward jerking sensation passengers typically experience.

Nissan

Braking Away From the Competition

With Smooth Stop, drivers can simply maintain pressure on the brake pedal without manually adjusting it. Similar comfort technology has been available in BMW models like the E90-generation 3 Series, but Nissan claims Smooth Stop is a first-in-class feature, giving the Rogue Hybrid an advantage over direct rivals like the Honda CR-V and Toyota RAV4. Perhaps Nissan can bank on Smooth Stop to distinguish the Rogue Hybrid, especially considering it has added other comfort features.

For one, Nissan made the cabin quieter by adding sound-deadening materials in noise-prone areas like the wheel wells and reducing wind noise through concealed windshield wipers, door-mounted side mirrors, and slim roof rails. The cabin also features Zero Gravity seats, soft-touch materials, and generous front headroom that contribute to comfort, while the available ventilated front seats were redesigned to minimize operating noise. The Japanese marque claims that normal conversations can be maintained between front and rear passengers, even at highway speeds.

Another comfort feature is the improved ProPilot Assist, the brand’s advanced driver-assistance system, similar in purpose to Ford’s BlueCruise. The system comes with a new front camera with a 20% wider field of view, increased object-detection range, and improved steering control for centering the vehicle in the lane. Moreover, it is available with Hands-Off Traffic Jam Support for hands-free steering at speeds below 30 mph and Auto Lane Change, which requires the driver’s hands to remain on the wheel. The system was developed and tested in California.

Nissan

A New Trick for an Old Rivalry

While the Nissan Rogue has been the brand’s best-selling model in the U.S., its sales still trail those of its main rivals by a wide margin. In 2025, Nissan sold 217,896 Rogues, significantly fewer than the 479,288 Toyota RAV4s and 403,768 Honda CR-Vs sold. But for the 2027 model year, Nissan is switching up the formula with a new series hybrid system and a more comfortable driving experience.

How that will pan out remains to be seen. The 2027 Rogue Hybrid will arrive first in the SR AWD trim with the SR Technology Package in limited quantities in late November 2026, with a $41,490 MSRP. More affordable trims and the more expensive Platinum trim will follow in early 2027.

Which Credit Card Should You Use For Marriott Hotel Stays? Here’s The Math

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When you’re staying at a Marriott property, what’s the best credit card to use to maximize your return on Marriott hotel spending? Should you use a co-branded Marriott credit card, or are you better off using another credit card offering bonus points on travel? This is a question that comes up every so often, so I’d like to address it in this post…

Best credit card to use for Marriott hotel stays

I’ve written in the past about the best credit cards to use for hotel stays. However, more specifically to Marriott, which credit card should you put all of your Marriott spending on? Let’s take a look at the various approaches that you can take.

Let me start by acknowledging that we all value points currencies differently, so you should apply your own valuation to the math. My below analysis is based on my own valuation of various points currencies.

I also want to emphasize that the credit card you use won’t impact your ability to earn elite qualifying nights, take advantage of elite perks, and accrue the standard points that you can earn with the Marriott Bonvoy program. The credit card rewards offered when paying for your Marriott hotel stay are incremental.

The value of using a co-branded Marriott credit card

Marriott has co-branded credit cards in the United States that are issued by both American Express and Chase. All of these credit cards with annual fees have the same rewards structure for Marriott spending, as they offer 6x Bonvoy points on all eligible Marriott spending.

This includes cards like the Marriott Bonvoy Bevy® American Express® Card (learn more), Marriott Bonvoy Business® American Express® Card (learn more), and Marriott Bonvoy Brilliant® American Express® Card (learn more), just to name a few. Personally, I value Bonvoy points at 0.7 cents each, so to me these cards offer an incremental 4.2% return on that spending.

I think the one thing worth pointing out is that the Marriott Bonvoy Bevy® American Express® Card offers 1,000 Bonvoy bonus points per Marriott stay when paying with the card, in addition to the rewards that you usually earn.

Technically that would be the best co-branded card for Marriott spending, since you earn 6x points, plus 1,000 bonus points per stay. That being said, I’d otherwise consider this to be one of the less compelling Marriott cards in terms of the overall value proposition.

Co-branded Marriott cards offer up to 6x Bonvoy points

The best non-Marriott credit card to use

I generally recommend putting most of your spending on cards earning transferable points currencies, given that they offer the most flexible rewards, and also have excellent bonus categories.

I’d argue that the two best cards for hotel spending are the Chase Sapphire Reserve® Card (learn more) and Sapphire Reserve for Business℠ (learn more), as they offer 4x transferable points for direct hotel purchases. Since I value Ultimate Rewards points at 1.7 cents each, that’s like a 6.8% return on that spending, which is excellent.

It’s worth noting that there are other cards earning 3x points on hotels, like the Citi Strata Premier® Card (learn more), and I’d value that at a return of 5.1%. Lastly, the Wells Fargo Autograph Journey Card (learn more) actually offers 5x points on hotel purchases, which is excellent, though I don’t value those points as much as other transferable points currencies.

Some transferable points cards offer great hotel rewards

The card I use for Marriott hotel spending

Personally, the Chase Sapphire Reserve and Sapphire Reserve Business are my go-to cards for hotel spending. Simply put, I can’t turn down 4x Ultimate Rewards points, in comparison to earning 6x Bonvoy points with the Marriott Bonvoy Brilliant Amex for my Marriott spending.

The difference in math is just too great, since I value 4x Ultimate Rewards points at way more than 6x Bonvoy points. But this also brings me to a related topic of how points valuations sometimes don’t dictate my consumer behavior. For example, back in the day, the Chase Sapphire Reserve offered 3x Ultimate Rewards points on hotel spending.

So I valued that at offering a 5.1% return, rather than a 4.2% return on a Marriott credit card. Despite that, I’d use a co-branded Marriott card for my Marriott spending. Why? Well, this gets at a consideration that’s hard to factor into my valuation of points. I’m talking about the ease of earning various points currencies.

I can earn transferable points currencies in all kinds of ways, including with my everyday spending. However, there aren’t many ways to efficiently earn Bonvoy points, short of staying at Marriott properties. As I often talk about when sharing my points strategy, there’s merit to collecting points that are harder to earn, even if they’re not the points currency that you necessarily value the most. This was one of those cases, at least for me. However, with the Chase Sapphire Reserve nowadays earning 4x points per dollar spent on hotels, my logic on that has evolved.

Bonvoy points are hard to earn efficiently

Bottom line

Everyone has their own credit card strategy, and at times it can be tough to decide which card is the best for a particular spending category.

In the case of spending at Marriott properties, I’d argue that the Chase Sapphire Reserve and Sapphire Reserve Business are nowadays the most lucrative, given that they offer 4x Ultimate Rewards points on that spending, which I value at a 6.8% return. I’d say that beats the Marriott Bonvoy Brilliant Amex, offering 6x Bonvoy points, which I value at a 4.2% return.

How artificial intelligence is changing the meaning of human creativity

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence can now create stunning artwork in seconds, producing images that once required years of training and practice.

But something important may be lost when the creative process becomes almost effortless.

A new paper argues that the greatest value of making art has never been the finished product alone. It is how the creative process changes the person behind it.

As AI increasingly separates artistic achievement from human experience, one researcher is raising questions about what creativity should mean in a world where machines can produce remarkable results.

Amit Raphael Zoran is a computer scientist at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, where he builds craft and fabrication tools in its School of Computer Science and Engineering.

His paper presents a philosophical argument rather than experimental findings, so its conclusions remain open to debate.

“For centuries, creating art meant going through a process of learning, experimenting, and changing as a person,” Zoran said.

How art changes us

Zoran’s answer to what human creativity is for starts by splitting what art does into two jobs.

One is the artwork itself: the painting, the song, the building that carries a meaning from one person to the next.

The other is what he calls artistic becoming, the change that making art, or meeting it, brings about in a person.

In his account, the change happens at three levels. A painter’s eyes and hands are trained over years. A viewer walks out of a gallery seeing differently.

And whole cultures shift: Renaissance perspective reorganized how people saw space, and Cubism unsettled assumptions about objects and viewpoint.

Zoran doesn’t claim the split itself is new. The philosopher John Dewey separated the art product from art as experience in the 1930s.

What Zoran adds is a claim about history: for most of it, the two stayed tied together only because making a sophisticated work required the maker’s years of change.

AI changes the process

Earlier computer art kept the tie. Harold Cohen’s drawing program AARON and the interactive installations that followed it supplied precision and repetition, while people supplied interpretation and meaning.

Generative models work on a different material. They are trained on the recorded traces of human culture, what Zoran calls an unevenly weighted and culturally skewed slice of the human record.

From that material, they produce images and music with qualities that once took years of training. The years are no longer required.

Zoran argues that AI has separated the quality of a creative work from the years of human experience traditionally required to produce it.

Nor does the maker’s change disappear, in his telling; it moves. Prompting, curating, and iterating with a model are real skills.

But they work on symbols instead of clay or wood, and many of them can be picked up without a long apprenticeship.

“Today, AI allows us to produce sophisticated works without necessarily undergoing that journey. But this doesn’t mean human creativity has lost its value. It means we need to rethink where that value lies,” Zoran said.

Judging the tools differently

Image and music generators are usually judged on output: realism, coherence, fluency, how closely a song fits its genre.

Zoran wants a second set of questions. Does a tool widen what a person explores or narrow it? Does it push users toward familiar forms or expose them to unfamiliar ones?

Zoran acknowledges that measuring how creativity changes a person is difficult. A new way of seeing leaves no trace in a model’s outputs, and asking people is a weak instrument.

So he proposes measuring conditions instead. An engineer can count how broad a range a system leads users to explore, and how often they explore instead of merely retrieving.

A tool’s defaults can be checked for how far they drift toward the statistically familiar. Recommendation feeds have already shown how easily software narrows what people encounter.

Experiencing art differently

Zoran describes three projects from his own creative work to illustrate how technology might expand human experience.

However, the projects do not establish whether these approaches actually produce lasting changes.

In the first, a live lecture on perception turns out to be partly prerecorded.

Several versions of the speaker start talking to one another, so the audience feels how easily confidence in a digital presence can be manipulated.

For the second, participants taste an essence reconstructed from an old oak. It was put together from connections among historical alchemy, botany, and chemistry that would have been hard to assemble without computers.

The third puts a tree at the center of a multisensory environment. Visitors smell and taste an essence from the tree and listen to sounds recorded from it.

Generative AI turns those sounds into music that a human musician performs acoustically, while projections show structures a person can’t normally see.

No one measured what any of this did to the people in the room, and Zoran wrote as much.

Zoran names a limit of his own. His examples and the thinkers he leans on are all Western.

East Asian calligraphy has long valued the brush stroke as a record of the writer’s training, and Japanese craft treats repair and imperfection as a continuation of an object’s life.

The tie between a work and its maker may run tighter there, and the thesis has not been tested against those traditions.

Creativity beyond the finished product

He closes with commitments. Artists should treat the effort a model saves as a budget and spend it on experience the model can’t supply.

Schools, museums, and funders should value process and documented engagement alongside finished pieces, since a finished piece no longer certifies them.

Tool builders should design against convergence and make provenance legible, because these systems increasingly shape what people notice and prefer.

Zoran argues that the future of creativity should not be judged solely by what artificial intelligence can produce. The more important question is what people gain, or lose, when they use it.

If technology removes much of the effort involved in making art, that freedom could open new opportunities for exploration, experimentation, and personal growth.

But whether AI ultimately expands human creativity or narrows it may depend on how people choose to use these increasingly powerful tools.

The full study was published in the journal AI & Society.

Review: Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai (DXB – B Concourse)

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I returned to the Emirates First Class Lounge in Dubai’s Concourse B during my latest trip through Dubai. Below are my updated photos and impressions, followed by my original 2019 review for your comparison.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Review (DXB – Concourse B)

With 30 minutes until boarding, I stopped by this lounge after a longer visit at the Emirates First Class Lounge in Concourse A. This Concourse B lounge is virtually unchanged since my last visit and by now, feels dated…not that I would touch it because it still serves its purpose well. I did notice one important change, though.

Hours + Access + Location

The Emirates First Class Lounge in Concourse B is located airside in Terminal 3 at Dubai International Airport, beyond security and passport control. Take the elevator or escalator to Level 4. The lounge is open 24/7.

Access is available to:

  • Emirates First Class passengers
  • Emirates Skywards Platinum members traveling on an eligible flight, with one adult guest and two children under 17 traveling on the same flight
  • Qantas Platinum and Platinum One members traveling on an eligible Qantas or Emirates flight
  • Passengers with Emirates Business Class Lounge access: upgrading to the First Class Lounge costs $150 per person for Skywards members or $175 for nonmembers, plus 5% UAE VAT. The totals are $157.50 and $183.75, respectively.
  • Other eligible paying passengers: First Class Lounge access costs $250 for Skywards members or $300 for nonmembers, plus 5% VAT. The totals are $262.50 and $315, respectively.

Paid access is subject to availability and covers one visit for up to four hours before scheduled departure. Business Class Special fares do not automatically include lounge access. Check Emirates’ current eligibility and pricing before your trip.

Seating

As you enter the lounge, you’ll note the high ceilings and the sooting sound of water fountains.  

It’s massive, though does not feel as large as the Concourse A Lounge.

Dining + Drinks

I had just had breakfast and wasn’t hungry, but one advantage to this lounge over the Concourse A lounge is that it has better barista coffee at the bar (unless I missed it in the other lounge). I enjoyed an excellent flat white.

There’s also a Costa Coffee in the lounge.

Had I been here longer, I’m sure I would have enjoyed a nice a la carte meal in the dining room.

In addition, there was a separate stand-alone bar in the smoking area with a limited section and drinks and Arabic coffee.

There was also another coffee nook with a small selection of breakfast items (it was 6:30 am). Let me commend Emirates here because you’ll notice if you scroll down to my prior review that the coffee machines were automatic prior to the pandemic and those simply cannot compare to a proper manual coffee machine. The coffee has been upgraded!

Smoking

This lounge has a large smoking section that is not separately ventilated from the rest of the lounge, though thankfully the cigarette smell was not too bad.

Showers + Restrooms

Restrooms and showers are available – I did not go inside the restroom, but there are pictures of both the restrooms and shower suites below in my prior review.

Spa

The spa is located one level above the lounge, with treatments available for purchase plus 15-minute complimentary treatments for first class passengers, though there’s a desk in this lounge from which you can reserve your treatment.

Quiet Room

A quite area with recliner chairs is available for longer layovers (there’s also an airport-operated transit hotel located one level up as well as a full gym, but that’s not included for first class passengers).

Children’s Play Area

A large children’s play room offered space for kids and even had a Pacman-style arcade video game.

CONCLUSION

While this does not feel as massive as the Concourse A lounge, I actually prefer this lounge for the superior coffee. Yes, it does look a bit dated by now, but it is quite functional and is a great place to wine and dine before your flight. I look forward to returning.

Original Emirates First Class Lounge Concourse B Review — 2019

The review below was originally published on June 4, 2019. Photos, menus, and observations in this section reflect that visit, including references to renovations and temporarily closed facilities.

The Emirates First Class Lounge in Dubai’s Concourse B is well-suited for the huge crowds that pass through it each day, but lacks the intimacy and luxury that typically characterizes a first class lounge.

I say it is huge–and it was–but not nearly as big as the Concourse A lounge, which I’ve reviewed before here. The lounge is undergoing a remodel of sorts, which is desperately needed. On the one hand, the lounge is just fine the way it in that is still functional and clean. But it is bereft of character and simply dated in appearance.

We walked down the long hall from check-in, though security and passport control, upstairs, down another concourse, and finally up another escalator to the lounge..it was quite a hike from curbside to lounge and Emirates offers no escort (or gulf cart).

The lounge is open 24/7, but was virtually empty when we arrived at 4:30 AM. I used the “calm before the storm” to capture some photos of the lounge.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Seating

Seating is spread out around the lounge and includes chairs, couches, and desks.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Spa

The spa is currently under renovation (or perhaps just closed). There was still an attended “Timeless Spa” desk in which spa treatments could be scheduled. First Class passengers were then directed to the business class lounge for treatment. I took advantage of a complimentary 15-minute back massage shortly after arrival and had no wait for a treatment. It’s a small gesture, but an appreciated one.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Dining

Actually, the plan was to eat first…both John and I were hungry. But the restaurant does not open until 5:15 AM so we ended up having to wait. A buffet is available 24/7 and included mostly breakfast selections.

Shortly after the restaurant opened, we sat down for breakfast…dinner actually. I wrote about it here. Here’s the a la carte menu:

As you can see, if you’re not in the mood in the for breakfast there are lunch/dinner items available. The nice leather-bound, multi-page menus have been replaced by oversized menu cards.

I enjoyed some berries and juice from the buffet followed by a filet mignon with French Fries and a salad. The food was excellent, although the coffee was quite disappointing. Best to stick to Arabic coffee and avoid the machine-dispensed.

Around the lounge there are a couple of additional food stations, one offering Mediterranean food and the other offering Japanese food. There’s also a full bar is self-serve and was open during Ramadan.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Shoe Shine

Complimentary shoe-shine is available. There’s a station in the middle of the lounge, though the shoe shiner approached us in the restaurant and asked if he could take our shoes. In their place, he offered us slippers.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Children’s Room

The children’s room, with video games, was roped off.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Restrooms + Showers

The restrooms reminded me a bit of the Concorde Room in London Heathrow…that’s not a good thing. I’d call the showers and restrooms still functional, but past their prime.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Smoking

Rather than a separate room for smokers, an area directly adjacent to the dining room has been designated for smoking. While there are no walls, barriers, or other partitions, I did not smell any smoke (and there were plenty of smokers there). Perhaps the super-high ceilings were helpful in that regard. It’s fairly rare to see smoking so integrated into a lounge.

Emirates First Class Lounge Dubai Concourse B Service

The staff in the lounge was all Filipino and provided friendly and attentive service. Some of the passengers did not treat them well, especially in the restaurant. They barked orders and were simply rude. As I remind myself, I remind you: let’s treat everyone with respect.

CONCLUSION

You cannot really compare an Emirates First Class Lounge to ones like Air France or Lufthansa. Emirates offers first class on such a wider scale that it has a larger and less-personal first class ground experience by necessity. I did appreciate the tasty lunch and spa treatment during my three hours in the lounges. That said, now that I have reviewed both lounges next time I will just stay a bit longer in my hotel.

U.S workers just took home their smallest share of capital since at least 1947—and the lowest ever recorded

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As corporate earnings soar and U.S. GDP balloons, the American workforce isn’t feeling the same boom. American workers are taking home less of the country’s overall income, data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics shows, and employment in the U.S. is set to continue to slow.

Labor share, or the portion of the U.S.’s economic output that workers receive through salary and wages, decreased to 52.8% in the second quarter of 2026, its lowest level since the BLS started recording this data in 1947, according to its labor productivity and costs report. In the previous quarter, labor share was at 53.6%. This decade, the labor share average was about 55.6%.

That’s despite corporate earnings skyrocketing, with profits for Fortune 500 companies hitting a record $2.1 trillion in 2025. The U.S. GDP grew 3.4% in the third quarter last year, exceeding economists’ predictions. 

That growth has not only come at the expense of how much of the pie of wealth workers are taking home, but also how many Americans are in the workforce, economists warn.

“That decline in the share of labor has got to be either falling earnings or falling numbers of people,” Raymond Robertson, a labor economist at Texas A&M’s Bush School of Government, told Fortune. “The falling share of income is having to do with the shift towards capital.”

Indeed, there are growing signs that as national income balloons, the U.S. workforce is deflating. Unemployment ticked up to 4.2% in September, and employers added just 584,000 jobs in 2025 compared to 2 million added in 2024. The U.S. added 29,000 jobs in September, about one-third of economists’ projections.

The bifurcation of corporate victories and weak labor data raises concerns among economists of jobless growth jeopardizing the U.S. workforce, as well as a K-shaped economy, where the rich get richer while the poor get poorer, becoming more exaggerated.

“Data right now is very mixed,” Robertson said. “But I think it also all consistently points to this idea that things are getting worse for workers and much better for billionaires.”

What is causing jobless growth?

Robertson attributes weakening labor share averages to the rise in automation, which he noted is displacing workers, with productivity—a metric essentially measuring worker output—continuing to rise. Second-quarter GDP data showed nonfarm productivity growth grew 1.4% last quarter, though the rate has slowed from 2.2% in the same quarter a year ago.

“All these things, bit by bit, are replacing people, and they’re concentrating income and their share of capital,” he said.

Goldman Sachs analysts Joseph Briggs and Sarah Dong estimated in a report, based on Department of Labor job numbers, that AI automation could displace 25% of all work hours. They predicted that over the course of the AI adoption period, a 15% increase in AI-driven productivity would displace 6% to 7% of jobs, and, at its peak, a 1 million increase in unemployed workers, though job displacement as a result of AI has not shown up meaningfully in any economic data.

The displacement could be substantial, the analysts said, but said the impacts of automation will be tempered by a wealth of new jobs created as a result of the technological changes.

Automation is expected to be a boon to corporate profits and GDP, projected to boost GDP by 1.5% by 2035, according to a Wharton brief published in September 2025. Early signs indicate AI could drive productivity gains, with around 80% of employees reporting AI makes them faster at specific tasks, but only 37% of companies seeing an impact of that productivity on operating profits, according to McKinsey data, and AI operating costs continue to constrain its deployment.

Robertson added that growing unemployment, which he expects to see rise over the next few months, keeps wages down, allowing margins and profits to expand.

To be sure, the recent productivity surge has been an “open question,” Morgan Stanley economists have argued, not unanimously attributed to increased adoption of AI or automation. The analysts suggested this increase would be cyclical, or vestiges of pandemic-era habits of companies making more from less.

An Oxford Economists research brief from earlier this year suggested companies are disguising overhiring-related layoffs as a result of AI, but said automation-related workforce reductions have not yet happened en masse. Additionally, while unemployment has been ticking up over the past year, it is still relatively low.

Why is an immigration crackdown backfiring on U.S. labor?

Mark Regets, senior fellow at National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP), sees a different reason for a slowing workforce. He told Fortune President Donald Trump’s immigration crackdown has not done what Trump administration officials, such as White House Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller, said it would in increasing the number of U.S.-born workers. Instead, according to Regets, Trump’s immigration policies have not only decimated the foreign-born workforce, but has also created fewer opportunities for domestic-born workers to find jobs.

NFAP data shows a 1.1 million to 1.4 million-person reduction in the foreign-born workforce since January 2025, consistent with the Congressional Budget Office’s report last year indicating shrinking U.S. population growth as a result of migrants being deported or refusing to come to the U.S. out of fear of hostile polities.

“The data is raising huge red flags that we are losing immigrants of all types that we otherwise would be advancing America’s economy,” Regets said.

Recent BLS data is counter-evidence to Miller’s argument that harsher immigration policy would grow the U.S. workforce. The labor participation rate for foreign-born workers fell from 66.3% to 66% from September 2025 to September 2026, and labor participation for native-born workers has similarly fallen from 61.6% to 60.9% over the same period. Fewer immigrant workers may actually make it harder for U.S.-born individuals to find work.

“A company unable to find the workers it needs for some roles could shut down operations rather than continuing,” Regets said.

He noted skillset diversity in a workplace could boost productivity and justify employing more people. Greater immigration can also increase consumer spending and stimulate businesses, as well as encourage businesses to take advantage of ample labor market availability and seek out their labor instead of offshoring jobs.

How does the U.S. reverse a shrinking labor force?

While friendlier immigration policies could help reverse an exodus of foreign-born workers, Robertson said addressing the workplace automation push would be key to growing the U.S. workforce.

“There are trades that are technology-assisted,” he said. “Those are going to be in higher demand, but you really still have to have a significant investment in skills.”

The young generation of workers are already prepared to adapt to a changing labor landscape. Gen Z are flocking to trade schools in hopes of a finding a job as a carpenter or welder not so easily outsourced by AI, and in 2024, enrollment in vocation-based community colleges increased 16%, according to data from the National Student Clearinghouse. 

Companies have taken it upon themselves to provide reskilling opportunities to employees. A CareerTrainer survey from June found that 72% of hiring managers intended to reskill employees. While the U.S. Department of Labor updated guidelines to encourage states to adapt workplace development systems, Robertson argued the government hasn’t done enough in several decades to imbue the workforce with necessary skillsets for future jobs.

“Democrats and Republicans have not significantly invested in training [or] the retraining or active labor market programs that you need to match workers to jobs,” Robertson said. “That’s the obvious solution.”

Without changes, economists see the pattern of an employment slowdown continuing, but with greater concern about the ability for the U.S. economy to sustain growth.

“We need job growth to have a growing economy, and I think we need job growth to pay our debts,” Regets said. “I don’t know how you have job growth with a shrinking labor force.”

Why some Amazon communities welcome the oil companies threatening their water

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Oil drilling in the Amazon is often portrayed as a battle between Indigenous communities and powerful energy companies.

But along Ecuador’s Lower Napo River, some communities facing the expansion of a major oil project were negotiating for something unexpected: clean drinking water.

They already depended on a polluted river, and they feared that more drilling would make conditions worse. Yet the company responsible for the new extraction also offered something the government had failed to provide.

Julie Dayot spent eight months in 2016 and 2017 living with Quichua communities while the Ishpingo-Tambococha-Tiputini oil project, known as ITT, expanded through their stretch of the river.

She began the work as a doctoral student in Oxford’s Department of International Development and is now at the University of Exeter‘s Environment and Sustainability Institute.

“When I first met people from Quichua communities, I was surprised that many wanted the oil company to come,” Dayot said.

“I wanted to understand why. Over time, I saw how the costs and benefits of oil extraction are spread unevenly over space and time.”

The river came polluted

The Napo is the villages’ tap. Families drink from it, cook with it, wash in it and brew chicha, the fermented yucca drink, from it.

By the time the project reached them, residents already considered the river polluted by sewage, agricultural runoff and earlier oil drilling upstream.

Older residents still talked about a 1987 spill from the Texaco era, when, as two of them put it, “no one would give us even a gallon of water.”

Fish were getting scarcer, and so was bush meat.

In Vicente Salazar, Dayot found that monthly household cash income ranged from $95 to $140. Residents struggled to afford improvements such as metal roofs and motorized canoes.

These pressures made the company’s financial support attractive long before drilling reached the communities.

Oil became the provider

The state operator, Petroamazonas, committed about $5.5 million in social compensation across the six communities inside the project’s zone of influence, on top of cash payments and jobs.

The funding was intended to support water systems, electricity, transport, schooling, training and agricultural projects.

One village of 480 people negotiated about $1.25 million in projects, while another community of 95 people secured $155,000.

Most of those are things a government normally provides. The company also paid for the health center in Tiputini and donated medicine and fuel for emergencies.

Several people told Dayot that local officials steered communities toward asking the company rather than the municipality.

An anthropologist in the company’s community relations office summed it up for her. “We (also) represent the state, which has been absent here,” he said.

“One particularly striking example is drinking water,” Dayot said.

“Several communities therefore asked the oil company for drinking water systems, both to address an existing problem and because they expected the new extraction to further contaminate the river.”

Oil extraction threatened the communities’ water supply, yet the industry also offered a way to protect it. That contradiction challenges the usual narrative of Indigenous resistance to drilling.

Harm travels, money stays

Only six communities were entitled to compensation, even though pollution could spread beyond the project’s boundaries and affect villages that received nothing.

In Vicente Salazar, just outside the project line, residents called themselves “affected” months before the company arrived. They meant affected by exclusion.

“We are affected,” one man said, “because they don’t give us jobs.”

When the company turned up in January 2017 to announce a new seismic survey, it did so during the monthly village work day, between the beer and the volleyball. There was no outcry.

One woman told Dayot the company needed to hurry up because she had no money for trousers. A man she asked about a vote shook his head.

“It’s not like we are going to oppose,” he said. “What it’s all about is negotiating what we wanna ask.”

When Dayot returned in 2023, she found that all six communities still supported oil extraction, despite the environmental risks.

The cost of acceptance

Dayot’s research focused on a small number of communities along one stretch of the Lower Napo River. Whether the same patterns exist elsewhere remains unclear.

The future of the ITT oil field remains uncertain. Ecuadorians voted in August 2023 to end extraction in Yasuní National Park, but production continued into September 2026, despite the referendum.

The study raises questions that extend beyond the fate of one oil field.

For communities along the Lower Napo River, accepting drilling was not simply a choice between environmental protection and economic development.

It was also a way to secure clean water, jobs, and essential services that had long been out of reach.

“We need to think beyond the decisions themselves to the uneven conditions under which acceptance happens,” Dayot said.

Her findings expose a troubling contradiction: Communities facing the environmental risks of oil extraction may depend on the same industry to provide the basic necessities they need.

The full study was published in the journal Geoforum.
