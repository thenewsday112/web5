The NYU Brennan Center for Justice recently published a valuable report entitled "Seven Solutions to Rein In Abuses of Executive Power." The Brennan Center is well to the left of me. But I agree with the vast majority of their recommendations here. We do indeed have an overgrown executive, and it needs to be pared back. I particularly agree with the excellent section on constraining emergency powers:

With just a signature, presidents can declare a national emergency and unlock special powers contained in more than 130 statutory provisions. These run the gamut from the seemingly trivial to the near-dictatorial, including powers to shut down or take over communications infrastructure, freeze Americans' bank accounts, and control domestic transportation. They are intended for sudden, fast-moving crises that Congress can't address quickly or flexibly enough. But presidents of both parties have stretched the meaning of "emergency" far beyond reason, enabling them to draw from a growing stockpile of statutory authorities with few real safeguards.

There are currently 51 active states of emergency, and the longest-running has lasted 47 years.10 Any emergency that persists for generations is, by definition, not an emergency; it's a problem that can be addressed through ordinary lawmaking. Yet presidents have used emergency powers to bypass Congress and unilaterally advance their policy goals.11

The National Emergencies Act once allowed Congress to terminate emergencies with a simple majority vote and without the president's signature. But the Supreme Court struck down that procedure, known as a legislative veto, in 1983. Today, ending an emergency effectively requires a two-thirds majority in both chambers to override a presidential veto.

Congress should amend the National Emergencies Act to constrain executive overreach.12 Emergency declarations should expire automatically after 30 days unless Congress approves a one-year extension, renewable annually with Congress's assent. And presidents should be required to share detailed reports on their uses of emergency powers. Proposals along these lines have received widespread bipartisan support, with versions of these reforms overwhelmingly passing out of the relevant House and Senate committees in 2024.13

Additionally, Congress should amend specific emergency statutes whose extensive delegations of power and inadequate safeguards make them susceptible to misuse. Of particular concern is the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the most frequently invoked presidential emergency authority.14 Sanctions imposed under this law can equate to a "financial death sentence" lasting years or decades.15 Although the Supreme Court blocked Trump's effort to use the law to set global tariffs, the potential for other abuses remains.

I completely agree, and have previously advocated similar reforms, myself. Brennan is right that IEEPA should be tightened up. While the Supreme Court did rule (in a case I helped litigate) that IEEPA cannot be used to impose tariffs, it still includes a lot of other dangerous powers.

I would add, as I did in this article, that invocations of emergency powers should be subject to  nondeferential judicial review, to ensure that they are used only in the event of genuine emergencies, not bogus ones. Congress would do well to amend relevant emergency statutes to make clear that such nondeferential judicial review is available.

I also strongly endorse Brennan's recommendations on constraining laws governing the domestic use of the military. The Supreme Court did strike down Trump's attempts to use bogus pretexts for federalizing the National Guard; the Ninth Circuit recently reinforced that ruling by rejecting the administration's broad definition of what qualifies as a "rebellion" justifying federalization. But, as the Brennan report indicates, there is still a lot of potential for abuse, especially when it comes to the Insurrection Act.

I also agree with the Brennan report's advocacy of constitutional amendments eliminating presidential immunity from criminal liability for acts undertaken in office, and barring him from pardoning crimes committed by" members of their inner circle, including immediate family members and senior administration officials" and those "convicted of crimes ordered by the president or committed on the president's behalf."

I doubt that these amendments have much chance of being enacted, given the extreme difficulty of passing any constitutional amendment. On the other hand, the reforms to emergency powers and domestic use of the military can be adopted through ordinary legislation. In some cases, they can also be bolstered by stronger judicial review under existing laws and provisions of the Constitution.

I do have a few disagreements with the report. For example, it is not clear to me that the Supreme Court's decision in Trump v. Slaughter goes as far as the authors suggest. The decision's scope remains uncertain. Among other things, it's not clear how wide a range of federal agencies it applies to and how to reconcile it with Trump v. Cook, decided on the same day. I also think they may overestimate Slaughter's impact, since most federal agencies are already not independent in the sense that there are legal constraints on the presidents' power to fire their heads.

I would also have liked to see more recognition that curbing excessive executive power requires curbing the scope of federal power generally. It is virtually impossible to have a federal government that regulates almost every aspect of human activity without simultaneously delegating vast - and often dangerous - discretion to the executive.

That said, the vast majority of the Brennan report's recommendations are well-taken. I hope future Democratic administrations and members of Congress act on them.

For those keeping score, it's worth noting that Brennan Center emergency powers expert Elizabeth Goitein, one of the authors of the report, is a long-time critic of abuses of emergency powers under Democratic administrations, as well as Republican ones. For example, she condemned President Biden's attempt to use emergency powers to effectuate massive student loan forgiveness. And so did I.

17 Easy Holiday Cocktail Recipes That'll Make You Feel Merry & Bright

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Family Fresh Meals

Whether you’re hosting a holiday party or just want to sip on a special beverage alone by the fire, now is the time when much of the world goes searching for easy holiday cocktail recipes. We all deserve a little treat after the year that was 2025. Well, rest assured, there’s a fun and festive drink out there for every palate (including if mocktails are more your speed). From spiked hot chocolate and eggnog concoctions for sweets lovers to wintry old-fashioneds and martinis, here are some of the best easy holiday cocktails on the internet.

Santa’s Magic Potion

Averie Cooks

If you’ve never put Bailey’s in your eggnog, now’s the time. Averie Cooks’ recipe for Santa’s magic potion will help you get the ratio just right, and hit it with a little cinnamon and nutmeg for that perfect holiday flavor.

Cranberry Mimosas

Damn Delicious

Mimosas are a crowd-pleasing cocktail, so why not just put a holiday spin on them for your winter party? Damn Delicious’ cranberry mimosas blend orange, pineapple, and cranberry juice with sparkling white wine. Just top with a sprig of rosemary and a couple cranberries and serve.

Bourbon-Spiked Hot Chocolate

A Cookie Named Desire

A Cookie Named Desire’s spiked hot chocolate is probably the most work of any recipe in this list, simply because it does involve melting some semi-sweet chocolate — but I promise that’s the extent of it. And let’s be real, the results look absolutely worth that effort.

Drunken Rudolphs

Princess Pinky Girl

If you’re not a bourbon fan, might I recommend Princess Pinky Girl’s drunken Rudolph, a hot chocolate cocktail spiked with Fireball instead? This recipe is even less work and is sort of the everyman’s version, if you will.

Starry Night Holiday Punch

Family Fresh Meals

Family Fresh Meals’ starry night punch recipe is nonalcoholic, but obviously you can just swap the sparkling grape juice for the real thing. It will yield a classic Christmas red sangria, which you can top with star-shaped apple cutouts for the perfect finishing touch.

Maple Old-Fashioned

A Cookie Named Desire

If you like a true classic cocktail, maybe A Cookie Named Desire’s maple old-fashioned is as festive as you want to get — and that’s OK. This blend of bourbon, cherry liqueur, maple syrup, and toasted pecan bitters has all the seasonal flavor you need without veering too far.

Peppermint Cream Martini

Dude That Cookz

If you want a cocktail that absolutely screams Christmas, then Dude That Cookz’ peppermint cream martini is the drink for you. It’s a delicious mix of peppermint schnapps, white cream liqueur, and vanilla vodka, with the cutest crushed-candy-cane rim.

Sparkling Apple Cider Sangria

Averie Cooks

Why haven’t I thought of apple cider sangria before? Averie Cooks explains everything you’ll need and how to get the perfect balance of flavors. Then just load ‘er up with apples, cinnamon sticks, oranges, pomegranate, and whatever else your holiday heart desires.

Jack Frost Holiday Cocktail

Princess Pinky Girl

Personally, I swore off blue drinks after one particularly rough hangover in college, but Princess Pinky Girl’s Jack Frost cocktail is admittedly very fun-looking. Just blend your ingredients together, rim your glass, and clink it with a friend’s.

Boozy Eggnog Egg Cream

A Cookie Named Desire

What if you took ye olde egg cream, which is kind of like an ice cream float, and made it with eggnog and champagne? It certainly doesn’t look half bad. A Cookie Named Desire’s recipe explains how (it’s super easy), and there’s a bonus holiday mimosa recipe higher up in the post, too.

Apple Cider Mimosas

Budget Bytes

One last mimosa idea before you go, courtesy of Budget Bytes: simply swap out orange juice for apple cider, pour it into your champagne flute until the color is just right, and bada bing bada boom. It doesn’t get any easier than that, and you know it’s going to be delish.

Candy Cane Jello Shots

Princess Pinky Girl

Oh, so you’re having a holiday party party — love that. Then you have to try Princess Pinky Girl’s Instagram-worthy candy cane shots, which only take about five minutes to whip up. Adding a little sweetened condensed milk to the gelatin mix gives it a sweet, creamy base that makes the candy cane flavor really come alive.

Spiced Pear Cocktail With Bourbon & Chai

Biscuits & Burlap

Biscuits & Burlap’s spiced pear and chai bourbon drink is the perfect reminder that apples are not the only seasonal fruit worth celebrating. It’s a bit more involved (you’ll need to make a fresh pear simple syrup), but the end result looks so worth that little bit of time and effort.

Pear Gin Fizz

Dinners Done Quick

Speaking of pears, you’ve gotta try Dinners Done Quick’s pear gin fizz. When’s the last time you actually ordered a gin fizz out? They’re so good but they’re never really on cocktail menus anymore, and they give me an old-timey celebratory feel every time.

Holiday Moscow Mule

Fresh Apron

Moscow mules are the perfect drink to serve to guests if you’re not much of a bartender and you’re just out here doing your best as a host. Fresh Apron’s recipe is a matter of a few simple steps (complete with exact measurements); it’s the festive garnishes and copper mugs that really make this beverage shine.

Chai Cranberry Fizz

savory spice

Savory Spice’s recipe will teach you how to make a chai simple syrup (very useful if you’re an at-home coffee enthusiast, by the way) and how to balance it perfectly within a cranberry fizz. It’ll add a seasonal, cozy depth of flavor that’s unexpected yet oddly perfect.

Easy Apple Pie Cocktail

Dinners Done Quick

White russian drinkers, you’ll love this easy apple pie cocktail, made with Crown and RumChata, from Dinners Done Quick. You can easily customize it to make it sweeter, heavier on the cinnamon, extra apple-y, however you take your cocktails.

Be honest: How many gallons of apple cider did you just add to your grocery list?

Atmos Rewards has Alaska and Hawaiian Airlines in its DNA, but its points can take you all over the world. Better yet, its two personal credit cards make it easier than ever to earn points and unlock travel perks.

Both the Atmos™ Rewards Ascent Visa Signature® credit card and Atmos™ Rewards Summit Visa Infinite® credit card earn the same valuable Atmos Rewards points but with different earning rates, benefits and extras.

The $95 Ascent includes competitive airline perks for Alaska and Hawaiian flyers, while the $395 Summit adds lounge passes, faster progress toward elite status and a Global Companion Award. Both are genuine solutions for travelers, so the question boils down to whether the extra benefits justify an added upcharge in Summit's annual fee.

For frequent Alaska and Hawaiian flyers, the answer is yes.

Companion perks

Atmos Summit cardholders receive an annual 25,000-point Global Companion Award each account anniversary and can earn a 100,000-point Global Companion Award after spending at least $60,000 on the card in the prior anniversary year.

Atmos Ascent cardholders instead receive an annual $99 Companion Fare (plus taxes and fees from $23) each account anniversary after spending at least $6,000 on the card in the prior anniversary year.

Main cabin on a Hawaiian Airlines Airbus A330. SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

The Summit's Global Companion Award effectively serves as a discount on award tickets on Alaska, Hawaiian and participating partner airlines worldwide. It can be used in any cabin, subject to award availability.

By contrast, the Ascent benefit only applies to paid Main Cabin travel within North America on flights operated by Alaska or Hawaiian, but it can still deliver significant savings. TPG director Carly Helfand saved over $400 by using the Ascent's Companion Fare for a last-minute round trip from Portland, Oregon, to Chicago with her husband.

Lounge benefits

The Atmos Summit grants two Alaska Lounge passes per quarter. If purchased separately at $65 each, this is a $520 value. To use the pass, you must be flying on a same-day ticketed flight on an Alaska, Hawaiian, or eligible partner airline.

The Alaska Lounge in the N Concourse at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (SEA). SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

Meanwhile, Atmos Ascent cardholders receive $100 off an annual Alaska Lounge+ membership, which costs $795 per year and includes access to all Alaska Lounges and nearly 90 partner lounges.

These benefits feel somewhat mismatched: The Atmos Ascent is aimed at less frequent travelers who likely have no need for an annual Lounge+ membership. Meanwhile, someone flying often enough to want (and pay for) a Lounge+ membership is probably a better fit for the full premium perks package on the Summit card.

Regardless of which card you're leaning toward, keep in mind that the Ascent cardholder discount is not stackable with discounts available to elite members. If you already receive the same discount through Atmos status or a milestone benefit, the Ascent perk is redundant.

Summit-exclusive perks

The Atmos Summit comes with several additional benefits that may look like fine print but can make a meaningful difference. For example, Summit cardholders receive a $50 voucher for Alaska Airlines flights canceled or delayed by 2 or more hours.

While that won't rescue your trip's logistics, at least it takes some of the sting out of the disruption.

SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

The partner award fee waiver can be especially valuable for frequent Atmos redemptions. TPG contributing editor Matt Moffitt saved $365 in partner award booking fees over the past year with the Summit, showing how quickly that benefit can add up.

Lastly, the points-sharing benefit is understated but potentially useful: Instead of paying to move points to another member account, Summit cardholders can designate up to 10 Atmos members to transfer points back and forth, with no transfer fees. That's a helpful way to reach the necessary points balance for an award faster.

Winner: Atmos Summit. Its companion award, lounge passes and faster path to status make it the clear winner, while smaller perks like fee-free points sharing add even more value.

Earning points

The Atmos Ascent and Atmos Summit both earn Atmos Rewards points. These cards share an earning rate of 3 points per dollar spent on eligible Alaska Airlines and Hawaiian Airlines purchases.

Plus, both cards earn a 10% rewards bonus on all points earned from card purchases if you have an eligible Bank of America account.

HINTERHAUS PRODUCTIONS/GETTY IMAGES

The cards earn drastically differently in non-airline spending, though. The Summit favors dining and spending abroad, while the Ascent rewards several everyday categories, especially transportation and streaming.

The Atmos Summit's bonus earnings on foreign transactions are unique in the rewards card space, making the product a fantastic option if you make many purchases outside the U.S. Meanwhile, the Ascent may be more rewarding for commuters and households with significant gas, transit, cable or streaming expenses.

Status points

In addition to redeemable award points, both cards earn points toward Atmos status with no spending caps. Atmos Summit cardholders earn 1 status point for every $2 spent on the card, while Ascent cardholders earn 1 status point for every $3 spent.

The Summit card also provides a lump sum of 10,000 status points each year on their account anniversary, putting Summit cardholders halfway to Atmos Silver before accounting for any additional flying or card spending.

Winner: Atmos Ascent. It's easy to get sidetracked by the Summit's higher earning rates, but most cardholders won't spend enough to justify an extra $300 in fees to earn a few extra points.

Redeeming points

You can redeem Atmos Rewards points earned on the Atmos Summit or Atmos Ascent in the same ways. Our favorite redemption options are for flights on Alaska, Hawaiian, Oneworld or a nonalliance partner airline.

Atmos Rewards offers outsize value thanks to its popular award chart, which is filled with redemption sweet spots to maximize. For instance, TPG lead loyalty programs writer Katie Genter recently redeemed 55,000 points to fly Condor business class from Frankfurt to Punta Cana, a ticket that ordinarily costs more than $1,500 one-way.

That represents a value of about 2.36 cents per point, even after accounting for $208 in taxes — a big step up from TPG's estimated value of Atmos Rewards points.

ERIC ROSEN/THE POINTS GUY

However, you can find great values with economy redemptions, too. Katie also redeemed just 30,000 Atmos Rewards points to fly on Alaska Airlines' inaugural flight to Europe. This route, from Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (SEA) to Rome Fiumicino Leonardo da Vinci Airport (FCO), often sells for well over $700 one-way in high season. Another popular redemption is using Atmos Rewards points to book short-haul American Airlines flights for as few as 4,500 points.

Winner: Summit. Both cards share the same redemption options, but the Summit gets a slight edge because it waives partner award booking fees.

Winner: Atmos Summit, since it provides access to transfer partners while the Ascent does not.

Can I have both the Atmos Ascent and Atmos Summit?

There is no restriction on holding more than one Atmos Rewards card, so if you choose, you could hold both of these credit cards. Bank of America's application and approval rules still apply.

Holding both could make sense if you value the Ascent's $99 Companion Fare and everyday bonus categories alongside the Summit's Global Companion Award, lounge passes and status boost. However, given the cost of the annual fees, most travelers will find one card or the other adequate for their needs.

Not everyone needs an Atmos credit card, though. Even though Atmos Rewards are fabulous for partner travel as well, a big reason to get one of these cards is for the perks when flying Alaska and Hawaiian. If they're not part of your regular travel, a general travel rewards card will offer more flexibility.

Should I get the Atmos Ascent or the Atmos Summit?

Get the Atmos Summit if you fly Alaska or Hawaiian regularly and will use its Global Companion Award, lounge passes and faster path to status. Its premium perks make it the clear choice for travelers who are deeply invested in Atmos Rewards.

Get the Atmos Ascent if you mainly want a free checked bag, preferred boarding and a lower annual fee. It delivers the basics most occasional Alaska and Hawaiian flyers need without the premium annual fee upfront.

Related: Are premium credit cards worth the annual fee?

Upgrading or downgrading between the Atmos Summit and Ascent

If you're hoping to upgrade from the Atmos Ascent to the more premium Atmos Summit, you can reach out to Bank of America and request a product change.

However, if you opt for an upgrade instead of applying, you may miss out on the Atmos Summit's welcome bonus. So, consider your options before you make a decision.

If you hold the Atmos Summit and want to downgrade to the Atmos Ascent, you can also contact Bank of America to make that product change.

Related: 4 major considerations before upgrading your credit card

Bottom line

There's no question the Summit has a better all-around package, but there's no reason to pay extra if you'll leave perks untouched.

Because both cards earn the same 3 points per dollar spent on Alaska and Hawaiian purchases, the Atmos Summit doesn't improve your return when booking flights. The extra cost in annual fees buys access, flexibility and a head start on status.

For travelers who simply fly Alaska and Hawaiian from time to time, the Atmos Ascent is a smarter fit.

Coinbase (COIN) Misses Revenue Estimates as Users Fall, Armstrong Sets Up New Share Sales

Coinbase had a mixed third quarter. Revenue came in at $1.20 billion, below the $1.233 billion analysts expected, according to company filings. Subscription and services revenue did better, reaching $570 million. The company is still heavily tied to trading activity, but its recurring income is growing.

Monthly Transacting Users fell to 7.4 million, down from 9.3 million a year earlier. During its earnings call, Coinbase blamed overall market conditions. Its share of crypto trading volume did rise to 10.7%, the fourth straight quarter of gains, which suggests it is winning business even as the broader market quiets. 

Adjusted EBITDA was $332.9 million, extending a run of 15 straight profitable quarters on that measure. The prediction markets business passed $200 million in annualized revenue, and Coinbase One hit a record number of subscribers.

Coinbase CEO preps share sales

Separately, filings reported on October 30 show CEO Brian Armstrong has set up a plan to sell up to 8.17 million Class A shares. He adopted the plan in August under SEC Rule 10b5-1, which lets executives schedule sales ahead of time. Sales could run from November 16 through October 2027, subject to stock price conditions. Co-founder Fred Ehrsam and President Emilie Choi have similar plans, bringing the total to about 13.6 million shares.

This is not new territory for Armstrong. In the past, VanEck analyst Matthew Sigel tracked about $545.7 million in sales over nine months, starting in April 2025. Armstrong has said these sales are part of his risk management, not a signal about the company.

Coinbase stock slumps nearly 15% 

Coinbase shares (COIN) fell about 15% today, but not necessarily due to this earnings report. 

Binance disclosed a security breach on October 29 that it estimates at $6.3 billion, making it the largest crypto theft on record. Around $3 billion in assets has been frozen so far, with industry partners assisting in the freezes. 

The hackers are allegedly linked to North Korea, though Binance has not attributed the attack to any group. The exchange says it has found no evidence of a leaked wallet private key.

Binance says it has $6.34 billion in reserves available to cover losses. Deposits were unaffected, and the company expects to resume withdrawals within 48 hours. Figures remain preliminary, and estimates vary slightly across outlets.

The market reacted quickly. Bitcoin fell roughly 4.5% to below $77,000. Shares of Strategy (MSTR) fell nearly 10% on the day. Strive (ASST) shares fell over 12% at times. Coinbase’s stock is down over 55% on the year.
