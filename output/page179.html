Nick Viall first appeared on “The Bachelorette” 12 years ago and ever since, he’s had one serious reality TV run. You lose strength much more slowly than you probably think.

A few days off changes basically nothing, measurable losses take roughly two to four weeks depending on the quality (muscle endurance fades first, maximal strength last).

Muscle memory makes rebuilding faster than building the first time, and maintaining takes a fraction of the training that building did.

Here’s the timeline, and how it can inform your training.

Has this question ever crossed your mind:

How fast do I LOSE strength if I miss my workouts?

This was an awesome question that Charles sent my way. (Thanks Charles! )

When life gets crazy and you find yourself skipping workouts, there’s a voice that says:

“Is this undoing everything I just worked hard for?”

I had that exact thought the first time I had to take significant time away for a back injury.

And heck, just recently my kiddos brought home the “crud” from school and I missed a full week of workouts.

So, let’s look at what actually happens, and what it means for your training.

Strength is stickier than you think

Short version: a few days off doesn’t impact your maximum strength or muscle size basically at all.

Your muscles may look or feel “flatter”, but that’s because stored water and glycogen decrease, not because you are weaker.

And this “pump” comes back quickly once you start training again.

The first image that popped into my head when I thought: “the pump”

Here’s a rough timeline of when we start to see the effects of detraining:

  • Muscular Endurance 1.5 to 2 weeks
  • VO2 Max 2 to 3 weeks
  • Muscle Size 2 to 3 weeks
  • Maximal Strength 3 to 4 weeks

Highly trained folks and older adults will notice detraining faster than new or younger lifters (so they’ll sit towards the beginning of these timelines.)

But you don’t lose your progress in any meaningful way after a week or 2 away from the gym.

Your muscle remembers

And if you DO have to spend significant time away for whatever reason, I have good news for you, too:

“Muscle memory” is a real thing.

1) Your nervous system remembers how to do the movement.

The first session or two in the gym after time away may feel a bit clunky, but you’ll quickly regain the technique.

And a big component of strength is neuromuscular coordination (i.e. your brain and muscles firing together more efficiently, which improves rapidly.)

2) The muscle itself seems to hold onto some of the machinery it built, so it rebuilds faster the second time.

A general rule of thumb is about 50% as long as you were away (so if you were away for 12 weeks, it may take ~6 weeks to rebuild your strength.) Take this with a grain of salt as there is a LOT of variation here. This isn’t a hard and fast rule.

(And you don’t want to jump right back in at 100%. Use the 50/50 rule to get yourself going again.)

The point is, it’s generally easier to rebuild than to build the first time.

The work you did before still counts.

A little goes a long way

Building muscle and strength? It takes time and effort.

No one wakes up a day after a workout and has just magically put on 5 pounds of muscle.

But maintaining what you’ve built?

That takes only a fraction of the volume.

When you keep intensity the same (i.e. how hard the exercise is), doing 1/3 the number of sets maintains your strength for a loooong time.
​
The point being: doing ANYTHING during a period where you aren’t able to consistently work out is still really really helpful!

Learning how to maintain progress is half the battle.

So what do you do with this?

Two things:

1) Keep a toe in the water.

When life throws you off track with your fitness goals, you’re not starting back from square zero.

And even a single session each week with a couple challenging sets is enough to hold what you’ve built until things calm down.

2) Get the work in now.

There’s that old saying that: “The best time to invest was 20 years ago. The next best time to invest is today.”

I think the same holds true for building up your fitness.

Every session you put in now is strength you get to keep later.

The effort is worth it.

The Death Of The Button: Why The Best Interface Is No Interface

📅 Sep 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ever since the commercialisation of the Graphical User Interface pioneered by systems like the Xerox Star and popularised by the original Apple Macintosh, software has relied heavily on point-and-click interactions. If you wanted to book a trip, buy a pair of shoes, or research a health symptom, you were expected to navigate a labyrinth of user interfaces. You click menus, adjust range sliders, fill out multi-step forms, deal with cookie pop-ups, and open dozens of browser tabs just to cross-reference basic information. We have become accustomed to spending more time managing the software rather than actually achieving our goals.

That contract is officially changing. Driven by advances in artificial intelligence and large language models, a new generation of web tools is pioneering a radical philosophy: Intent-Driven Design. Rather than expecting our users to learn complex menus and click through elaborate sales funnels, these platforms operate by capturing high-level human goals and silently executing the grunt work in the background.

This vision builds on long-standing HCI concepts like Golden Krishna’s “The Best Interface Is No Interface” and Don Norman’s principles of human-centered design.

The ultimate goal of modern web design is no longer to build prettier buttons or flashy animations; it is to eliminate the interface entirely.

Well, at least what we understand based on our current experience.

It is essential for UX designers to understand the changes we’re witnessing and even re-evaluate our role, shifting our focus from designing visible interfaces to guiding transparent, intent-driven AI experiences.

The Death Of The “10-click” Process

To understand where web design is going, we first have to look at the friction we have accepted as “normal” for decades.

Consider the traditional workflow of buying a flight online. The user experience is intentionally hyper-interactive:

  1. Navigate to a travel aggregator or airline website.
  2. Select “Round Trip” from a dropdown menu.
  3. Type the origin city and wait for auto-complete.
  4. Type the destination city and wait for auto-complete.
  5. Click a calendar modal, toggle through months, and select departure and return dates.
  6. Choose the number of passengers and cabin class.
  7. Click “Search” and wait for the results page to load.
  8. Filter by price, layover duration, departure time, and airline.
  9. Sort the results and scan through dozens of individual options.
  10. Click through a three-page checkout funnel dodging upsells for rental cars and travel insurance.

This is a classic point-and-click UI paradigm. The computer acts as a passive container of data, and the human acts as the orchestrator, manually inputting parameters, interpreting raw outputs, and executing each micro-step along the way.

Intent-driven design flips this dynamic entirely. Instead of forcing you to navigate the mechanical steps of how to find a flight, the interface asks a simple question: What are you trying to accomplish?

When you express a goal, such as “Find me a non-stop flight to Chicago next weekend under $300 that arrives before 5 PM”, the software reads your intent, executes the multi-step search query behind the scenes, compares the options, and presents a single actionable resolution. The ten clicks dissolve into one clear intent outcome.

Three Web Platform Examples Replacing Buttons With Intent

This shift isn’t a theoretical vision of the distant future. Some of the world’s most accessible websites allow you to experience intent-capturing systems already.

1. Perplexity AI: Search Without The Open Tabs

Traditional search engines like Google were built as directories. You typed a keyword, and the interface rewarded you with ten blue links. The actual work of opening five tabs, skimming long articles, dodging ads, and manually assembling an answer was left to you. Although this is now changing, with AI content appearing first when you search on Google.

Perplexity AI allows anyone to perform instant intent-based searches. Instead of forcing you to click into multiple tabs, the interface synthesises the perfect result for your context.

  • The interaction: You ask a complex question in plain English: “Compare the top three budget laptops for a computer science student, focusing on battery life and keyboard quality.”
  • The invisible work: In under two seconds, the platform searches the web, reads dozens of articles, evaluates hardware specifications, and cross-references user reviews.

  • The result: (See screen above) Instead of throwing web pages at you, Perplexity dynamically generates a single custom answer complete with formatted comparison tables, pros and cons lists, and concise inline citations. You get the outcome of thirty clicks in just one.

2. Vercel v0: Web Design Without Dragging And Dropping

For years, creating a website meant using visual builders like Figma or drag-and-drop web editors. As designers, we spent hours manually drawing rectangles, picking hexadecimal colour codes, adjusting padding values, and sometimes writing CSS code.

Vercel v0 lets anyone test its generative interface builder right from the home page without signing in.

  • The Interaction: A user types a descriptive goal into the input prompt: “Create a modern dark-mode dashboard for a subscription SaaS app showing monthly revenue, active users, and a customer churn chart.”
  • The invisible work: The underlying AI understands layout principles, accessibility guidelines, UI component libraries, and responsiveness. It writes clean HTML, Tailwind CSS, and React code in real time.
  • The result: Rather than spending an afternoon manipulating elements, the user receives a fully functional, pixel-perfect user interface in seconds! The traditional visual design tool disappears, replaced by raw human intent.

3. Goblin.tools: Breaking Down Overwhelming Tasks

Many traditional productivity tools require you to manually construct task lists, drag items across Kanban boards, and assign micro-deadlines. Goblin.tools is a free, single-page suite of single-task AI tools designed specifically for neurodivergent users or anyone feeling overwhelmed, requiring zero sign-ups or configuration.

  • The interaction: You enter a vague, intimidating goal into the Magic Todo tool, such as “Prepare for a job interview”.
  • The invisible work: Instead of forcing you to plan every step, the system analyses the cognitive load of the goal and automatically breaks it down into small, actionable sub-tasks adjusted to your preferred level of detail.
  • The result: The interface eliminates the stressful task-planning phase and presents a clean checklist tailored to your exact goal.

The Core Pillars Of Intent-driven Web Design

When you remove traditional menus, sidebars, and forms, how do you keep a website usable? Designers pioneering this space rely on three core pillars:

Pillar Traditional UX approach Intent-driven UX approach User input Micro-actions (Clicks, dropdowns, toggles) 1. High-level goals (Natural language, context, habits) Interface state Static layouts (Everyone sees the same page) 2. Generative UI (Layouts created dynamically on the fly) Task execution Manual execution by the user 3. Autonomous execution by background AI agents

1. High-level Goals

An invisible interface doesn’t always wait for you to type a command — it leverages context to anticipate user needs. Guided by industry standards like Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines on Contextual & Ambient Design, modern systems read environmental metadata, such as device state, location, and past interaction patterns, to trigger proactive actions.

For example:

  • Time and location: A food delivery web app prioritising your saved home address and dinner items automatically at 6:30 PM on a weekday.
  • Cross-app history: A calendar application noticing an email about an upcoming appointment and surfacing a one-click “Add to Schedule” card without forcing you to copy and paste event details manually.

2. Generative UI

In traditional web design, every user sees the exact same layout. An e-commerce store shows the same navigation bar whether you are a first-time visitor looking for customer support or a returning customer tracking a package.

As highlighted in the Nielsen Norman Group’s analysis on AI as a new UX paradigm, computing is shifting from command-based interaction to outcome-specification. This enables Generative UI, where interfaces are rendered dynamically on the fly based on what you are trying to do in that exact moment. For example, platforms like Vercel v0 demonstrate how raw intent can instantly render functional code components without manual layout building. If you express a desire to compute complex financial data, the interface generates a dynamic calculator widget on demand, receding once your goal is completed.

3. Execution by AI Agents

Traditional interfaces are obsessed with prevention, constantly peppering users with confirmation pop-ups (“Are you sure you want to delete this file?”).

Intent-driven interfaces adapt Jakob Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics on User Control and Error Recovery by shifting the safety net from prevention to easy reversibility. Because autonomous agents take actions on your behalf, products prioritise frictionless rollback mechanisms. A prominent “Undo” button, simple revision prompts (“Make this summary shorter”), and transparent audit logs replace intrusive warning modals.

The Hidden Risks

While removing interface friction is liberating, stripping away visual controls introduces significant product design challenges. When software acts on inferred intent rather than direct point-and-click commands, designers must navigate critical ethical and technical pitfalls.

The Illusion Of Control

When a website makes decisions for you, it can quickly feel patronising or invasive. As explored by the Stanford Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) Group, automation must maintain clear boundaries to prevent user frustration. Furthermore, ethical frameworks from the Center for Humane Technology emphasise that product designers must guard against manipulative defaults.

Designers must strike a delicate balance: automate routine execution, but explicitly prompt the user for high-stakes confirmations (such as financial transactions, publishing public content, or altering privacy settings).

The Black Box Problem

In a traditional UI, if you get an unexpected result, you can usually diagnose the issue — perhaps you checked the wrong filter box or selected an incorrect date.

With an intent-driven interface, troubleshooting becomes harder. To address this “black box” challenge, the Google PAIR (People + AI Research) Guidebook advocates for transparent feedback loops. The system must explicitly state its interpretation (“Searching for non-stop flights to Chicago under $300...”) so users can calibrate trust and correct misinterpretations instantly.

From A UX Perspective

As a UX designer, always being curious to understand how our design approach is changing in the world of AI, I find it essential that we re-evaluate the foundational elements of our craft.

Rather than designing static buttons and rigid forms, we need to learn to map and present dynamic, generative UI components that adapt on the fly to user intent.

By mastering these evolving patterns, like ambient feedback, inline prompts, and transparent AI status indicators, we can ensure that screenless, agent-driven tools remain intuitive, ethical, and deeply human-centered.

Conclusion
The ultimate goal: The best design is no design.

For decades, the digital industry has evaluated software success through engagement metrics like time spent in app, click-through rates, and page views per session. Websites were deliberately engineered to maximise visual engagement and interaction volume.

The philosophy behind Golden Krishna’s seminal book The Best Interface Is No Interface, aligned with the broader Center for Humane Technology’s Time Well Spent movement, turns these metrics on their head. The ultimate test of a modern digital product is no longer how visually captivating its buttons are, but rather: How effectively did this software solve the problem and get out of the user’s way?,

We are entering an era where web applications will no longer be measured by the beauty of their interfaces, but by their ability to render those complex interfaces completely obsolete. The future of the web isn’t more interaction — it is seamless, invisible completion.

Image: Shutterstock.com, Kirill Makarov.

This month’s patch bundle obliterates the software giant’s previous record set in July, when it released updates for at least 570 security vulnerabilities. September’s Patch Tuesday brings this year’s total to more than 2,600, more than twice Microsoft’s previous record-setting patch year in 2020 (1,245) and with three more months to go.

There are two “zero-day” flaws fixed this month that are being actively exploited: both CVE-2026-81963 and CVE-2026-85880 allow an attacker to elevate their privileges on Windows system.

Fully 113 of the bugs addressed today earned Microsoft’s “critical” rating, meaning they could be abused by malware or miscreants to seize control over a vulnerable Windows machine with little or no help from the user.

Among the more serious critical flaws this month is CVE-2026-69730, a DNS weakness present in Windows Server 2012 onward and on Windows 10. Microsoft warns that an unauthenticated attacker could leverage this weakness simply by sending a specially crafted packet to an affected system, and that it is likely to be exploited.

Also scary is CVE-2026-69829, a critical, remote code execution flaw in the Windows Shell. This vulnerability has a CVSS base score of 9.8 (10 is the most severe), and can be exploited with low attack complexity, no privileges, and no user interaction.

Microsoft’s summary of the security updates released today. Image: msrc.microsoft.com.

Microsoft is hardly alone in shipping monster patch bundles lately. Tyler Reguly, associate director of security research and development at Fortra, said one core challenge with deploying Windows updates is that they need to be tested before being installed across an organization because not all third-party software works seamlessly in the face of changes to the underlying operating system.

“It’s time to put our CISOs and CSOs on notice,” Reguly said. “How are you helping your teams through these difficult times? Do you have your teams deploy after hours and on weekends to avoid disruption to the business environment? Do you reward them for that effort? Time to dig into your budget and buy dinner for your teams that are working on Saturday to get patches rolled out before users return to work on Monday.”

Satnam Narang is senior staff research engineer at Tenable. Narang said it’s important to recognize that while the number of vulnerabilities being patched by Microsoft is rising, the number of flaws that can and will affect most organizations remains quite low.

“AI-assisted vulnerability discovery in 2026 is creating larger haystacks, but it isn’t finding more needles,” he said. “It’s critical that organizations understand which vulnerabilities actually apply to them, whether they pose a threat by being reachable and exploitable, and prioritize remediation based on this risk context.”

Of course, regular Windows users don’t need to test patches before deploying them, but they still need to open Windows Update periodically or else assent to the program’s nag notices about pending updates. And at the rate these Windows patch releases are ballooning in size, it’s probably best not to let them pile up month after month.

Soreness is not an indicator of a good workout, and not being sore doesn’t mean your workout didn’t count.

That day-after ache (DOMS) mostly tells you that you did something new, not something effective.

Progress is more reliably measured in your training log: more weight, more reps, more control over time, sore or not.

Rachael (a reader and all-around amazing person) sent me a question about this so well-organized it came with multiple choice answers.

Is severe soreness in the following day or two [after a workout]:
​
A) A sign that I did a really great workout, good job pushing myself to the limit?
​
B) A sign that I really overdid it and should have stopped earlier?
​
C) Neither, just a matter of personal preference. If it bothers me, I can try to avoid it next time. If it doesn’t bother me, grin and bear it, but no extra fitness points for getting to a point of soreness?

I LOVE this question, because almost everyone who’s getting started (or starting again) wonders the same thing:

“If I’m not sore the next day, did that workout even count?”

What soreness actually tells you

Quick definition first. That achy, stiff, “why does it hurt to sit on the toilet” feeling that shows up a day or two after a workout has a name: DOMS, or Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness.

It usually peaks somewhere around 24-72 hours after you train.

Meet General DOMs. We made him a boss in our bodyweight workout series. He’s a jerk.

Severe soreness is NOT an indicator of a good workout.

It’s a sign that you did something new that your body isn’t used to yet:

  • a new exercise program
  • a bigger range of motion than you’re used to
  • coming back after time off (that’s when I personally get the MOST sore)
  • more emphasis on the eccentric portion of a lift (the down phase, which is known to produce more soreness)

So it’s not something you need to chase.

But it’s also not something to be afraid of, especially if you switch up your routine and are mildly sore for a day or two.

Your body is adapting quickly! And as you adjust to the new exercises, you’ll be less and less sore from it.

Are there any benefits to soreness?

So when is soreness helpful, if ever?
​
Soreness can give us a useful signal that we worked a muscle we were trying to target and hadn’t felt before.

But if you’re SUPER sore? Like, can’t get out of bed the next day and have to waddle around like a newborn giraffe sore? That’s a sign the jump was bigger than your body needed.

And the real cost (beyond the discomfort) is that it gets in the way of your next couple of workouts. Either because your technique gets sloppy because everything hurts, or you’re so wrecked that you don’t want to show up at all.

The best way to lower your odds of severe DOMS is to ease into a new program slowly.

Start with fewer sets and lighter weights than you think you need, and build from there.

Wait, don’t I have to push myself?

Yes! You DO need to push yourself over time in order to make progress:

  • lift a little more weight
  • do another rep or set
  • run a little faster or a little longer

That’s the law of progressive overload, and there’s no getting around it.

Sometimes adding that extra bit will make you sore. Other times, it won’t.

But it’s the underlying principle that matters: are you asking a little more of yourself over time? Not: did it hurt afterward?

The Minty Toothpaste Effect

So why do we care so much about soreness?

Here’s what I think we’ve fallen into with fitness in general.

We as humans operate really well with feedback. If we touch a hot stove, we get burned and know not to do that again.

The same goes for fitness.

We want to know, immediately after doing something, whether it worked or it didn’t.

And with strength training, you don’t feel stronger the next day. It takes time to build up.

So we reach for proxies that close that feedback loop.

“Did I get sore? OK, I did something.”
​ “Am I really sweaty? Great, that counted.”

It’s exactly like the story of minty toothpaste.

As the story goes, toothpaste manufacturers had a hard time selling their product until they added mint.

Suddenly, there was a tingly clean feeling you could latch onto. “Oh, this IS working.”

The mint doesn’t clean your teeth. But it’s associated with the thing that does, and it gave people something to feel.

That’s where soreness sits for me.

It’s not the REASON you’re getting stronger.

But it can be associated with the work you do, especially as a beginner who is new to exercise, so we’ve learned to chase the tingle.

What to measure instead

Measure your output:

How much weight, how many reps, how many sets are you doing?

Is it going up over time?

Do you feel more stable and controlled with the exercise than you did last month?

Then great! It’s working. Doesn’t matter if you never get sore.

And if you do end up super sore after trying something new, it’s not that you “failed”.

Just take it as a cue to introduce things more gently next time.

So, back to Rachael’s multiple choice question.

C is really close. But I’ll add an fourth option:

D) Soreness tells you what was NEW. Your training log tells you what’s WORKING.

No extra fitness points for being sore. No points off if you do end up sore, either.

All the points gently ramping things up over time, and showing up again next time.

-Matt

Quick recap

Is it bad if I’m never sore? No. If your weights, reps, or control are improving over time, your training is working, sore or not.

How long does DOMS last? It usually peaks 24 to 72 hours after the workout and fades over a few days. If you’re regularly wrecked for longer, ease the jumps in your program.

Should I work out while sore? Mild soreness is fine to train through, and easy movement often helps. If you’re severely sore, target a different part of the body, scale down, or take another rest day; what we don’t want to do is have sloppy technique because we’re SO sore we can’t perform the exercise normally.

Some aspects of coaching are intuitive (like, be friendly), and some aspects are paradoxical and upside-down.

For example, in Motivational Interviewing, an effective communication style used in coaching and counseling, there is something called the “righting reflex.”

The righting reflex is the instinct to help or “fix” a client, as well as give them directions on how best to change. Often, the righting reflex is particularly strong in people who really, really care.

Sounds like a great instinct to follow if you’re a coach, right?

WRONG.

We are now entering upside-down-land.

Motivational Interviewing describes how this (beautiful, well-intentioned) instinct to help can actually hinder a client’s progress.

That’s because when we swoop in to fix, correct, or steer, clients often do the exact opposite of what we’re hoping for: They dig in and get defensive.

Push someone the “right” way and, paradoxically, they’ll start advocating for the very habits they came to you to change. You end up being the one arguing for change—which leaves them to argue against it. Everyone walks away a little more frustrated, and the client feels more stuck than ever.

Instead, great, experienced coaches learn to get curious instead of corrective. (They might even encourage the client to explore not changing!)

That’s just one example of an upside-down skill that separates a good coach from a masterful one. But it’s certainly not the only example.

Which brings us here.

We asked some of Precision Nutrition’s most seasoned PN Level 2 Master Health Coach Certified coaches a simple question:

What’s something you were sure about early in your career that you later had to unlearn?

Their (honest, and sometimes humbling) answers are below, plus the wiser way forward they adopted, and are now sharing with you.

Lesson to unlearn #1: Going into robot-detective mode before getting to know a client

Our first lesson to unlearn comes from PN in-house coach Jeremy Fernandes, PN2-MHC.

With over 14 years of coaching experience, Coach Jeremy is known for his friendly, comfortable, “talk to me like I’m your buddy” coaching style. Which is ironic, because as you’ll see, he started off his career leaning heavily on a rigid structure that was actually pushing clients away.

Here’s what Coach Jeremy has to say:

“I used to think being a coach meant I had all this information and I was just meant to spew it at someone.”

At the beginning of my career, when I’d been a personal trainer for a year or two, I had a friend ask if I offered nutrition coaching. And my first move was: Here are all of the forms. And when I say “all of the forms,” I mean all of them—I probably sent this person 20 pages to fill out.

They didn’t.

Three weeks later I had to hunt them down, and we ended up filling them out together—which took two and a half grueling hours.

If not for those forms, we could have started actually coaching, like, 20 days earlier.

Not long after, another friend showed up at my door with this white-hot intensity: “Tell me what to do, I’m ready,” they said. And I immediately dumped a metaphorical bucket of ice water on them with those darn forms. It was radio silence after that.

The problem was that I was operating like my sole job was to extract information from a person and then solve the mystery—as if I had some advanced pattern recognition about you that you didn’t have about yourself.

And unfortunately, I kept on that terrible path for a while.

What finally prompted a change was going through the Level 2 Master Health Coaching Certification.

Going through that course, I saw there was a better framework—one that doesn’t rely on me robotically extracting information.

I learned to let the client take the lead, and then just listen and maybe ask a few good questions. And it works so much better—for everyone.

Now I work from the assumption that my client knows themselves better than anyone. One of my most valuable questions is simply:

“What have you tried before, what did you like about it, and what didn’t you like?”

When I let people take the floor, they nail the really big stuff more often than not—in a way my paperwork and my detective work never facilitated.

Honestly, the forms were me projecting professionalism, because back then I didn’t feel like a professional. My identity as a coach never felt natural until after L2. Now, just letting someone talk feels like coaching. We start with a connection, and then I just let them fill in the gaps.

Lesson to unlearn #2: Treating a health goal like a destination you arrive at

The next lesson to unlearn comes from PN Director of Community Engagement and in-house coach Kate Solovieva, PN2-MHC.

With over 16 years of coaching experience, Coach Kate is known for her honesty and incisiveness. She has a way of dropping realness on her clients that may first feel like a shock, but ultimately ends up getting her clients to the heart of the matter much faster. But one hard truth she had to face was her own.

Here’s what Coach Kate has to say:

“I used to think that a goal is a destination. You pick a timeline, you do all the things, you arrive, and then it all becomes effortless.”

You know the story: You reach the healthy weight, the habits lock in, and the weight just stays stable on its own. You hit “save” and move on with your happily ever after.

And that is not the case at all.

At least most of the time. It’s true, some goals really are like that. If you want to run a marathon, you can pick a race date, follow a training program, show up to the start line, run it—and then you never have to run again and you will still have achieved that goal. That’s a real destination; you cross the finish line once and check the goal off.

But weight loss, muscle gain, body recomposition, building a habit of movement or eating well—those aren’t like that. Especially when the goal means working against a chronic tendency, like a predisposition to high blood pressure, or to hating exercise, or to living in a larger body than you’d prefer.

Those goals come with a pretty good amount of ongoing and intentional maintenance—daily, weekly, monthly. There’s no save button.

This realization didn’t come from one single client or one moment for me. It came through a collection of observations, including observations of myself. But once I saw it, it changed how I coach.

I realized that if there’s no real finish line, then my job is to manufacture finish lines along the way. That way, my clients continually feel like they’re accomplishing something satisfying.

I take the big goal and break it into something specific and daily—and then I insert smaller outcome goals into the journey.

For instance, someone going from sedentary to wanting to lose 100 pounds is facing a huge goal, full of behaviors and identities they’ll have to grow into. So along the way, we might set a goal of running a single mile without stopping. Strictly speaking, that doesn’t have much to do with the bigger goal—but it also has everything to do with it. It’s checkable. Achievable. Ideally it’s also something they’re a little excited about: fun, interesting, maybe even a little scary, but still within reach.

Because here’s the thing: It’s very hard to motivate the human brain to work out three times a week for the rest of their life to “get healthier.” “Being healthy” isn’t tangible, it’s not immediate—you can’t feel that far-off outcome in your body.

But striving towards a fun, achievable goal in the next three to six months? That’s real. It also builds in structure, and provides those little spikes of motivation—the hit of dopamine you get from working hard at something and actually accomplishing it.

Health isn’t something we ever fully “accomplish” or check off. So we have to give ourselves these little concrete goals along the way that we can feel and see and be delighted by.

Lesson to unlearn #3: Applying more structure and discipline when clients aren’t compliant

The next lesson to unlearn comes from PN content creator Azul Corajoria, CPT, PN2-MHC.

With over nine years of coaching experience, Coach Azul’s coaching fingerprint is empathetic, relatable, and endlessly encouraging.

While these traits always came naturally to her, for a long time, she thought she had to adopt an opposite style to help certain clients (and herself) to “get back in line” when habits didn’t go as planned.

Here’s what Coach Azul has to say:

“Most people don’t need more discipline; they need more self-compassion.”

Early in my coaching career, I thought that if someone wasn’t following through on what they said they would do, it’s because they needed more structure or just to get their “head in the game.”

I couldn’t understand why someone would invest in coaching and then not do the things we planned.

It was especially confusing because most of my clients had “discipline.” They were successful, responsible, hardworking people who showed up for their jobs, families, and everyone else in their lives.

Meanwhile, I was also “disciplined” in many areas of my life—yet I also struggled with eating well and exercising consistently.

At this time, I felt this lack of consistency was my fault, and went straight to being really angry at myself. I thought I was just missing the “perfect something” (the perfect workout, the perfect diet) to help me change, so I’d just move from thing to thing, but each one would bring fresh disappointment.

I was incredibly frustrated by this cycle, and my response was to get stricter and harsher with myself.

It’s hard to say when it all changed. It wasn’t an overnight epiphany; it was a culmination of years of getting curious, being open to different methods, like the Body Positivity Movement and Intuitive Eating. I dove into self-compassion and positive psychology research, and I went through the PN Level 2 Master Health Coaching Certification.

I applied everything I learned, and I noticed that the more open and curious I was, the better my clients responded. Slowly, that turned into the realization that the more open and curious I was with myself, the better I would respond.

It changed my world.

I stopped binge eating. I started enjoying exercise more. I took more career risks.

I learned that when we can respond to a setback with curiosity instead of punishment, we’re much more likely to stay engaged. When I allow myself to be disappointed by an action, take responsibility for it, and remain on my own side, I feel safer reflecting and taking risks. The more I do that, the more likely I am to achieve the things I want.

Sometimes the most helpful thing we can teach a client (and ourselves) is not how to push harder or “just be more disciplined.” It’s how to make a mistake, remain on our own side, and keep going.

Because self-compassion is not the opposite of accountability; it’s often what makes accountability possible. (I call it “compassionate accountability.”)

None of us will ever be the “perfect” coach, and we don’t have to be. The more human I’ve allowed myself to be, the better relationships I’ve had with my clients. My biggest goal as a coach is to create an environment my client feels safe to come back to with not only their wins, but their losses. The safer they feel being honest and open with me, the safer they’ll feel to try new things—and over time they’ll build the confidence to do it themselves.

Lesson to unlearn #4: Thinking I get to define a client’s “progress”

The next lesson to unlearn comes from PN in-house coach and Level 2 Master Health Coaching Certification instructor Dom Matteo, PN2-MHC, NBC-HWC.

With over 16 years of coaching experience, Coach Dom stands out for his non-judgemental attitude and excellent listening skills. For Dom, these traits are relatively inherent, but they’ve also been carefully developed and honed, in part by letting go of what he calls the “coach’s ego”—the assumption that he already knew what a client’s success should look like, and that he was the one who got to keep score.

Here’s what Coach Dom has to say:

“I used to think the client’s ultimate goal was the one they named—’lose 50 pounds’, ‘lose 20 pounds.'”

I took that stated goal at face value, and treated whatever number they gave me as the measuring stick, as the most important thing to work towards.

But then, I’d see a client struggling to do their actions even 50 percent of the time, and I’d think: ‘This person is never going to be happy, because this won’t get them to the goal weight they told me they wanted.’

Remember, I assumed the number was the real goal.

However, again and again, those same clients would turn out to be genuinely happy with their progress, not because they hit their exact goal, but because they felt so much better about themselves and their lives.

What I learned is that the stated outcome was almost never the true goal for many of them. When they said ‘lose 50 pounds,’ what they really meant was: I want to feel better. And being consistent even half the time, over a year, netted results they were thrilled with. Because they felt better. They felt different.

So the thing I had to let go of was my own scorecard. Who am I to tell a client that what they’re doing isn’t good enough, if they’re happy with how things are going?

My new definition of progress became less about approaching a specific final number and more about another question: “Am I a little better today than I was yesterday?”

These days, I spend time up front helping a client build what we call a “wellness vision.” We clarify their values, and what success actually looks and feels like to them.

Then, instead of assuming I know how to measure their progress, I’ll ask: “How will you know if this is helping? How do you want to measure success?”

The result is that even a client who’s fixated on the scale can see other signs of progress long before the scale actually changes. And often, those other signs are the things they really wanted to change in the first place.

It turns out the most useful thing I can provide isn’t a judgement about whether a client is succeeding. Instead, it’s to help them determine and notice their own success, by their own measures.

Lesson to unlearn #5: Focusing on client results instead of the whole person

The next lesson to unlearn comes from PN in-house coach and Level 2 Master Health Coaching Certification instructor Jason Bonn, PN2-MHC, NBC-HWC.

With over 18 years of coaching experience, Coach Jason’s clients describe him as patient, observant, and deeply dedicated. He has a knack for spotting a client’s potential—though as you’ll read in this story, Coach Jason learned that getting too focused on a specific version of that potential sometimes kept him from seeing other ways his clients could grow and thrive.

Here’s what Coach Jason has to say:

“I used to believe that if we kept tweaking exercise or nutrition, we’d eventually find the combination that’d get a stalled client moving.”

For me, progress felt like a puzzle to solve: I’d play with adjustments to exercise volume or intensity, calorie or protein intake, eating more slowly, eating to 80 percent fullness, all the fundamental stuff.

But then, because of one client I still think about, I began to shift how I thought about “progress.”

He was in our PN coaching program. He lost 40 to 50 pounds in his first six months—which was great—but then everything stalled.

We tried adjustment after adjustment for the next two and a half years. We added more protein, more cardio, and worked through his particular peccadillo, eating corn chips at night. But none of it moved his numbers.

What got me was that he kept showing up anyway: three straight years, re-enrolling each time, even though the scale had plateaued.

That told me something was working for him—it just wasn’t something I recognized yet.

In his third year, we stopped looking only at the gym and his diet, and started looking at his whole life. He was a landscape painter, so we built his practices around that instead: make a sketch or work on a painting daily, anything that sparked some joy or interest. Within a couple of weeks, he was getting on his bike and riding out to further places for different scenery to paint.

In part because of this client, I’m now a lot more cognizant of coaching the whole person, and considering their Deep Health. Weight and performance numbers are just one piece of a much bigger picture that includes relationships, emotional wellbeing, and what someone finds meaningful.

I’ve also stopped zeroing in on “problem” behavior.

I try to understand a person’s life overall—not just the behavior I’ve been hired to help with—because there’s a connectedness to it all, and behavior tends to emerge within the context of someone’s life.

I’ve also stopped treating the coaching relationship as a means to an outcome and started treating it as the main thing—the sun that everything orbits, the glue that holds it all together.

That client kept coming back for three years because the relationship mattered to him. By the end, I’d stopped thinking I was failing him. The weight loss was such a small part of the success. The relationship was the bigger piece.

Becoming an excellent coach isn’t about having all the “right” answers.

Coaching mastery isn’t about stockpiling more information. (Your clients can Google information or ask a chatbot, and so can you.)

It’s about developing the judgment to know when to use what you know, when to set it aside, and when the most helpful thing you can do is nothing at all, except be there.

But that kind of judgment is hard to build alone; it takes practice, expert feedback, and a few humbling moments with real clients—which is exactly what the PN Level 2 Master Health Coach Certification is designed to offer.

With enough practice, self-awareness, and the right training, you can gain the wisdom and skill of the above coaches.

But the best coaches, it turns out, are defined less by what they know than by what they’ve been willing to unlearn.

If you can’t get yourself to start a workout after a long day, the problem might not be the workout itself. It’s the gear shift from “couch” to “go.”

A bridge task is a small, neutral action (not fun, not hard, just…meh) that you put in between the two so the jump is smaller.

Below is the 5-step version one of our coaching clients uses with her own students, plus a free worksheet to map your own.

A few weeks ago I was talking with Tabitha, a Nerd Fitness coaching client, about a challenge a lot of people face.
​
Some quick context: Tabitha is an executive function coach. She helps neurodivergent students and their families build the skills and systems that support their academic and personal goals.
​
And she noticed something:
​
The challenge of getting started on a workout is almost identical to the challenge her students face when trying to start homework.
​
This might sound familiar. You get home after a long day, mentally fried, and all you want to do is veg out on the couch.
​
In that state, getting up to cook a healthy dinner or go do a workout can feel really freaking hard.

Why starting a workout is harder than the workout itself

Here’s Tabitha’s insight:

Switching from a “YEAH!” task (chilling on the couch) to a “NAH!” task (workout/homework), is often harder than the task itself.

If we can make that gear shift less dramatic, it can make a big difference!

What is a bridge task?

That’s where bridge tasks come in.

Instead of going from YEAH! to NAH!, you insert at least one task that’s just…MEH.

I’ll let Tabitha walk you through it.

How to use bridge tasks: the 5 step YEAH -> MEH -> NAH sequence

I’ve been working with Nerd Fitness for 3 years now and have had so many successes! Coach Sarah and I have been working on my self-trust and mindset, along with cross training to support my aerial silks goals (inversions, I’m going to own you one day!) ​

If you are also struggling with the executive function skill of task initiation, whether it’s getting to the gym, or hitting the books, here’s the process that we use to help parents build the bridge from preferred to non-preferred tasks – by adding some “meh” into the mix. ​ 1. What’s the yeah?
 Identify the preferred activity and postpone the decision that requires gear switching. ​
Example: I’m sitting in my cozy spot on the couch and I’m tired and it’s time to workout. ​
​2. Delay and take one step away. Delay the decision. Rather than fixating on the NAH task, instead focus on movingawayfrom the preferred activity. ​

​4. Revisit and micro-commit ​Now is the time to remind yourself of the NAH task and make a micro-commitment. What is the tiniest step you can take? 2 minutes or less. ​

​Nerd Fitness has been fabulous at making sure I don’t forget this step. Shoutout to Coach Sarah! <3 ​

Traversing the Saiga Silk Road

📅 Aug 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

As we skidded off the main road and onto a mud path maintained only by tire treads, lines began to blur.

Flanking our two-track trail were a series of parallel lanes no more than a foot wide, which forked, merged, and crisscrossed our own. In the distance, I saw what looked like a ghost train, faintly visible and thrust forward by howling Siberian winds.

Through borrowed binoculars, I soon got my first glimpse of an ancient tableau, seemingly untouched since Neanderthals roamed the earth. On the far horizon, hundreds of nomadic travelers pierced the frigid air with their ringed horns, inhaling the brittle shards through Snuffleupagus snouts as they sprinted across the desolate steppe.

Watching them charge through the treeless plain at full bore, I was reminded of a saying I’d heard shortly before arriving in this remote corner of Kazakhstan: “The saiga is a freedom-loving animal.”

Since time immemorial, saiga antelope have blazed trails across Central Asia while other civilizations rose and fell around them. They once inhabited a world dominated by woolly mammoths, saber-toothed cats, and giant deer with antlers 12 feet across—and outlived them all. When pioneering human caravans first attempted to navigate this unforgiving land, they considered the saigas to be their guides.

Ahead of my arrival, seasonal rains transformed once-green grasslands into sludge punctuated by tufts of withering brush. Driving through such muck is, I would soon discover, akin to controlled hydroplaning. The steering wheel bucked wildly as our tires swerved, shredding deep into the gunk and desperately gripping anything remotely solid enough to push off from.

In the front seats were Alexandr and Albert, two team members from the Association for the Conservation of Biodiversity of Kazakhstan, or ACBK. Between them, they have more than twenty-five years of experience following saigas across the steppe, and I’d arranged to join them for the western leg of one of their field audits.

Our visit was timed to coincide with the rutting season, a critical part of the year for saigas. This is a gathering time, when antelope from across the region sprint together in massive single-file lines, before congregating for a series of competitive battles and subsequent mating rituals.

For males, it’s a time for strutting and headbutting to demonstrate one’s physical prowess to female onlookers—an activity so energy-intensive that many will forget to eat and keel over from extreme physical exhaustion. For females, this is a time of choosing. In addition to assessing fitness, they pay particular attention to one distinctive feature: a potential mate’s floppy, elongated nose. The saigas’ sniffers are essential for filtering out dust in the semidesert plains and for warming frigid winter air before it reaches their lungs. A large nose is the ultimate sign of virility, so as the males marched into these annual gatherings, they were doing everything they could to accentuate their schnozzes.

Because of the saigas’ preference for large-scale migrations across a landscape almost entirely devoid of trees, they have long been a favorite of global wildlife trackers. The isolation and starkness of the saiga world is a strength when it comes to GPS collars, which deliver a reliable stream of daily pings without risk of visual obstruction. Prior to such breakthroughs, conservationists would sometimes spend weeks roaming the boundless steppe unsuccessfully trying to locate the nomadic herds.

The wildlife sanctuary we were traveling through was created only a year prior, with its boundaries informed by tracking data that Albert and his team personally collected. He seemed pleased that the animals were continuing to use the newly designated space as intended. Standing along the roadside, Alexandr spotted a stream of the famously skittish ungulates wrapping around the contours of the earth. As he did, ripping wind and snow started blowing in sideways—the outer ring of an encroaching storm.

These were no fluffy flakes. This was the harsh, crunchy ice of a walk-in freezer, which instantly hardened our truck’s caked-on mud like liquid nitrogen.

Seemingly unperturbed by the dropping barometer, Albert chirped, “Who’s hungry?” Setting up an impromptu picnic, he and Alexandr dug into a prepared meal of fresh bread, tea, and dried meats of unknown provenance. In a muted attempt to thaw my face, I opted to guzzle spicy instant noodles.

A tradition of nomadism has loomed large in this corner of the world since at least the Early Iron Age. The very word kazakh is thought to be derived from the Turkic word qaz, which means “wanderer” or “vagabond.” For millennia, small human groups routinely migrated to higher altitudes in the hot summer months, where melting snow made for abundant water and greenery, before returning to sheltered valleys ahead of winter’s whipping winds. At various times of the year, disparate nomads would also congregate for formal meetings and large cultural festivals, complete with physical challenges like wrestling and tug-of-war. When the gatherings were finished, the nomads dispersed once again.

Historical analysis has shown that nomads’ seasonal migrations and time-honed transit corridors may have helped pave the way for portions of the Great Silk Road, which was less a singular route than an ever-evolving web of pathways and bypasses that linked together reliable stopovers. At the Silk Road’s peak, this corner of western Kazakhstan along the fertile banks of the Ural River became a vital transcontinental crossroads. To this day, the river still serves as a natural boundary between Asia and Europe, meaning this part of Kazakhstan is, quite literally, where East meets West.

The land above the Caspian Sea also served as a main artery that linked together key transit points. Strategic settlements along the riverbank allowed weary caravans to rest their heads, unwind in large public baths, exchange knowledge, stock up on essential supplies, and regroup before moving onward.

Among overland voyagers traversing the continental divide, saigas have long been spoken of with an almost mythical reverence. Cave paintings of the antelope created as far back as the seventh century BCE depict them as resilient, elusive, and charismatic. Expert navigators who embody the true spirit of a most inhospitable terrain.

In human society, traditional nomadic practices persisted even as the land itself was variously claimed and reclaimed by Turkic tribes, Genghis Khan, and the Russian Empire. Only with the rise of the Soviet Union in the twentieth century did mandatory sedentarization efforts successfully manage to lock nomadic people in place, force them to farm, and extinguish a cultural flame that had burned brightly for millennia. Denied the ability to live in perpetual motion, people’s navigational muscles began to atrophy.

This transition was viewed by many as an inevitable shift toward “modernization,” but it also represented a significant diversion from our own history as a species. Of the roughly 300,000 years that Homo sapiens have existed, we spent the first 288,000 of them living in small, mobile groups that followed the migratory patterns of other animals. Forcing nomads into agricultural plots was also a colossal failure because much of the steppe soil was unsuitable for growing crops, which is why so many people were nomadic in the first place. Iconic filmmaker Werner Herzog once remarked that “whatever went wrong and makes our civilization something doomed is the departure from the nomadic life.”

Whether by choice or necessity, most Kazakh people now lead stationary lives in urban centers. The world that nomads once freely roamed has also been feverishly transformed, not only by agriculture but through the construction of new roads and railways.

For the saigas, whose nomadic days never ceased, these new layers now represent an existential threat.

With the weather taking a turn for the worse, Albert and Alexandr ultimately decided to call it a day and start fresh in the morning. After a blistering drive through whiteout conditions, we hunkered down in a tiny village less than 10 miles from the Russian border, where Albert pulled out his laptop and showed me how saiga movements have changed over the years.

“They would routinely trek hundreds of miles as part of an expansive migratory route across the Eurasian steppe,” he said. “Saigas could be found from the Caucasus Mountains to Mongolia, and everywhere in between.”

For millennia, they lived with few barriers and obstructions on the landscape. Then, seemingly overnight, rattling railways and roaring roads began to crisscross the region, fragmenting the saigas into smaller, more isolated groups. As the interior steppe got more accessible to vehicles and passengers, development begat more development.

On his screen, Albert clicked open a recent map that showed how a population of GPS-collared saigas once routinely traveled deep into Uzbekistan before being unceremoniously clipped off by a new rail line in 2014. The data from the years prior to the railway’s construction showed a flurry of squiggly lines representing individual animals zipping back and forth with the seasons. The post-construction slide showed those same animals spinning around despondently on the northern side of the tracks, while the fresh grasses and water they had come to rely on withered away to the south.

Such interruptions can lead to a complete collapse in spatial knowledge—a loss that science writer Ben Goldfarb once wrote is “as thorough as the erasure of a language.” Just as human nomads forced to stay in one place eventually lost connection to the physical landmarks of their ancestors’ journeys, young saigas learn where they can reliably find food and shelter, as well as places to stay away from, through active participation and observation. This cultural exchange keeps their world in orbit and is one of the reasons they have been successful at navigating a terrain fatal to so many before them. As the land they know is cleaved and partitioned, whole stretches of the Saiga Silk Road begin to fade from memory.

To unwind this damage, Albert’s team has partnered with the Kazakh government to construct dozens of saiga-friendly crossing points, where slightly sloping dirt ramps rise up to match the height of the train tracks, making it appear from a distance as though there’s a natural gap in the rail line. Cattle-guard fences positioned far away from the crossings ensure that trains and saigas never actually run into each other at these intersections, which would shatter the illusion of safety.

ACBK has also worked with the Uzbek government to reshape its border fences from a barbed-wire wall to an X-shaped barricade that blocks vehicles from driving through but leaves just enough space for saigas to pass. Over time, Albert hopes such efforts can rebuild the trust necessary for them to confidently return to a plateau they once relied on.

The next morning, I was astonished by how austere the steppe looked compared with the day before. Gone was the slippery muck and browning grasses of shifting seasons, replaced by a blanket of crunchy snow. Against this stark black-and-white backdrop, the fresh crystals functioned like fingerprint dust, illuminating the only remaining pops of color: the rust-red streaks of human and nonhuman pathways in the dirt.

Parked in an otherwise empty tract of prairie land, we watched in stunned silence as thousands of saigas seized the day, flying like banshees across the periphery, their hooves gouging the frozen crust and stamping pigment into an earthen canvas.

As I gazed east toward the colorless horizon, I tried to envision how it might have looked in the thirteenth century, when this corner of the world boasted a vast network of caravanserai, or road inns, where exhausted travelers could rest and recover before continuing onward into the great unknown.

I pondered what it must have felt like for an intrepid merchant to know that they would not see another human being for many days, maybe weeks. To be fearful of what you may soon encounter, but take solace that the space between is not wholly empty. For there is life beyond humanity’s margins, and among nonhuman communities, that in-between space is the center of everything. That, I imagined, must have offered some measure of assurance that the road ahead, perilous though it may be, is well traveled.
