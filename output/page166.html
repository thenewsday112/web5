4 Quick Activities to Try If Your Kid Is Wired at Bedtime

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As a parent, you probably know that bedtime can go two ways: one, your kid was so busy all day they’re exhausted and can’t wait to get that story and hit the pillow. Or two, they haven’t had a chance to expel the endless amounts of energy they store in their little bodies. The latter is where a normal nighttime routine can get tricky because that second wind is no joke. They’ll be jumping, singing, fidgeting, and doing everything they can to delay the inevitable “lights out” moment, meaning there’s even less time for you to binge a few episodes of your new favorite show.

If you’re dealing with a kid who’s wired at bedtime, there are a few things you can do to make the transition to dreamland easier. Mom and pediatric occupational therapist Courtney English recently shared a few of her favorite sensory activities that help kids calm down before bed, and they’re simple but effective. Her TikTok video shows what parents can do:

Easy Calming Bedtime Activities for Kids

1. Crawl tag lets them organize proprioceptive input in their body.

Dr. Megan Anna Neff writes for Neurodivergent Insights that “Proprioception plays a role in body awareness, self-regulation, coordination, posture, and the ability to focus.” Playing a short game of tag in their bedroom will allow kids to realize where they are at that very moment in time, helping them to center themselves and be present.

2. Sensory sandwich provides passive proprioceptive input to the body to lower little’s arousal level.

Massage is known to be a great way to receive proprioceptive input, and what English calls a sensory sandwich (putting your child between two pillows and pressing down) is a low-energy activity that’ll give their body what it needs to relax.

3. A combo of singing and rocking offers calming and organizing vestibular input for your kid (and you).

Vestibular input is another important factor for kids who are wired at bedtime. As Laura Petrix writes for the OT Butterfly, it’s “a network of parts in your inner ear that contribute to your sense of balance and coordination.” It allows our bodies to know when we’re falling, move in coordinated ways (like riding a bike), and know the right speed to walk around other people. The combination of singing and rocking helps to calm the vestibular system by providing auditory rhythm and linear movement.

4. Body squeezes in bed help their body feel organized and relaxed, so your kiddo feels connected and safe while falling asleep.

This is another great type of massage or touch activity that provides proprioceptive input to the nervous system. Plus, it’s a great chance to get a few snuggles in there before they close their eyes.

These activities are no secret to occupational therapists, who use them often. In fact, English has offered other sensory activities that are appropriate for bedtime, including tossing around a weighted animal and doing the “clock move.” But they’re something we should all have in our back pockets next time we’ve got monkeys jumping on the bed.

‘Should I let my kid have ChatGPT?’ and all your biggest questions about AI, answered

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It feels like artificial intelligence is taking over every facet of life. Adults encounter AI daily. It recommends products for us to buy, shares content we’ll like on streaming platforms, filters spam in our email accounts, and even tracks our health through our smart watches. 

It’s increasingly becoming a part of our kids’ daily lives, too. They are accessing AI on smartphones, using it in school and some are even developing relationships with AI chatbots. Fearing a repeat of the consequences that came from unfettered teen access to smartphones and social media in the early 2010s, many parents are justifiably concerned about this new technological frontier.

Experts are sounding alarm bells, as well. Former Google trust and safety executive Tom Siegel recently told Reuters that AI poses a greater harm to children than social media did, highlighting risks such as suicide, psychosis, and reduced critical thinking.

While banning your kids from using AI may sound appealing, it’s not feasible. Unlike social media, which is contained in standalone apps, AI is threaded into technology our kids use everyday, from search engines to video games. That’s why it’s important for parents to focus on AI education to prepare their kids to safely interact with the technology in its many forms.

Whether your kids are just starting to use AI or are already AI connoisseurs, we tapped experts to help answer some of parents’ most pressing questions about teens and AI.

How are teens using AI?

According to a Pew Research Center study, more than half of teens say they have used AI to search for information or get help with their schoolwork. They’re using generative AI, which creates content from prompts, to create images, direct them to research on topics for school and even solve math problems. 

Teens are also increasingly interacting with chatbots, which are computer programs that are designed to simulate human conversation. You’ve probably heard of or even used some of the most popular chatbots, like ChatGPT, Claude, Google Gemini and Perplexity. Roughly two-thirds of teens report using chatbots; 3 in 10 do so daily. 

What are the biggest risks AI poses for kids academically?

While schools are entrusted with educating young minds to become critical thinkers, AI often takes the thinking away from them. According to the Pew Center study, 59% of teens think students regularly use AI to cheat at school. Of the 15% of teens who said they think AI will negatively impact them personally over the next 20 years, one-third cited overreliance and loss of critical thinking or creativity as major concerns. 

While many students think AI provides an easy way to finish their work quickly, doing so has consequences. Kids who heavily rely on AI in high school can struggle in college, where professors are developing methods to identify unacceptable AI use, then handing out failing grades on tests and papers.

In an assignment asking students to compare the Industrial Revolution to today’s digital age, Alcorn State University professor Jason Gibson hid a prompt in invisible white text that would force generative AI to drop the word “Madagascar” into the response in a way that made no sense. He knew students had used AI when he saw “Madagascar” in the completed assignment, and 32 of the 35 students failed a portion of the midterm accordingly.

What are the emotional risks AI poses for kids?

In 2025, Common Sense Media and Stanford Medicine’s Brainstorm Lab for Mental Health released research showing that three in four teens are using AI chatbots for companionship, including emotional and mental health support. But these programs aren’t providing reliable help and consistently fail to recognize and appropriately respond to mental health conditions that affect young people, according to the report. 

And it can have tragic consequences. In August 2025, Matthew and Maria Raine sued OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman after their 16-year-old son Adam died by suicide. The parents allege that ChatGPT coached their son on how to die by suicide, even offering to write a draft of a suicide letter. Adam first started using ChatGPT for homework help, but the chatbot soon became his “closest confidant,” according to the lawsuit.

“Because chatbots show relative competence for homework help and general questions, teens and parents unconsciously assume they’re equally reliable for mental health guidance—but they’re not,” says Common Sense Media. “The empathetic tone and professional presentation mask fundamental limitations that make these systems inappropriate for a use case that affects millions of teens.”

Does AI have any benefits for my teen?

Yes, AI can have positive impacts. “For learning, AI can assist in brainstorming, creating, organizing, summarizing, and synthesizing information, and offering resources and solutions for challenging problems,” according to a statement from the American Psychological Association on AI and adolescent well-being. When used as a tool for guided learning rather than a replacement for critical thinking, AI can ask questions to help challenge a user to analytically approach a topic.

How are schools using AI?

There’s no one-size-fits-all solution for AI use in schools. Depending on where you live, your kids may encounter widely differing expectations. Some school districts are attempting to take a hard pause on AI in the classroom. New York City Public Schools announced that for the 2026-27 school year it has enacted a moratorium on student-facing generative artificial intelligence (AI) through eighth grade, with limited use in high schools.

But it’s hard to ban something that in many ways just can’t be escaped. According to a Common Sense Media report on teens and AI, 44% of teens who use AI for schoolwork attend schools where AI tools are blocked—many of them simply switch to personal devices to access AI.

And when schools ban access, educators aren’t able to teach their students how to use AI tools responsibly. Ash Brandin, EdS, a teacher in Colorado’s Boulder Valley School District who shares content to help parents and educators navigate screen time, video games, and digital literacy under the Instagram handle @thegamereducator, says their school district prohibits student access to generative AI platforms on school property. “In some ways, that makes it more difficult [for educators] because it’s very difficult for me to talk about something that is taken further and further away from us,” says Brandin, who uses they/them pronouns. 

A staggering 70% of teens say they have never had a teacher discuss how to use AI safely with them, and only 27% have discussed what AI is or how it works with a teacher. 

Should I ban my teens from using AI?

The short answer: No. 

AI is like toothpaste that’s been let out of its tube; trying to get it back in is a fruitless endeavor. Just like adults, “kids are seeing AI woven in and imbued in their everyday lives,” Brandin says. 

What teens need is AI education so they can learn what it is, the risks it poses, and how they can use it wisely. 

If your teen is using AI for potentially harmful activities, like cheating or for companionship, help them find alternatives. “Think about what your child is getting from the chatbot or AI and what you would like them to do instead,” Brandin advises. Then collaborate with your teen to come up with a plan.

How can I help my child learn how to use AI responsibly?

Teaching your kids how to use AI requires more exposure, not less. “I love to explain readiness with a pool analogy,” Brandin says, “I didn’t introduce my child to a pool at the age I thought that they could independently swim. I introduced my child to pools and bodies of water as early as possible.” As babies, you hold them close while they dip their toes. Later, they’re just an arm’s length away while using a floating device. Eventually, they’re diving in the deep end alone while you sit poolside in a lounge chair. 

“With each step I’m focused on what skills they need to build up so they have the ability to navigate the pool,” Brandin says. AI is no different. Parents should begin explaining what AI is and how it shows up in our lives as early as possible. Common Sense Media has a free Digital Literacy and Well-Being Curriculum that can help guide age-appropriate conversations about AI, social media, and more.

On their Instagram account, Brandin shares a couple of digital literacy lessons parents can do with their teens, tweens and even younger kids to teach critical thinking skills. The first is to bring up a chatbot and ask it to give a random number between 1 and 100. “If you do that with ChatGPT, 7 out of 10 times it will answer 42,” Brandin says. Ask your kids to react to the results with questions like, “We asked for something random, does this seem random?” 

In another experiment, you can ask ChatGPT to explain how to do something that your child already knows how to do really well. “Since our kids are going to AI for things they don’t know, we want to ask it something they know really well so we can turn around and say, ‘It looks like this chatbot made a lot of mistakes on this thing that you know really well. So what do you think is happening when we ask it things we don’t know?’”

I can confirm that my 9-year-old gymnastics-loving daughter had a lot of corrections when we asked ChatGPT how to do a back walkover. 

Brandin suggests modeling how to properly and safely use AI. When you are searching, speak out loud and explain to your kids how you are verifying the results AI spits out. What sources did AI use? Do I trust those sources or do I need to look further?

Perhaps most importantly, if your kids are using AI, you need to use it, too. The more you tap into the tools your teens are using, the more you will understand them and be able to communicate with your kids about them.

 When you feel fear and anxiety about AI creeping in, “interrogate it for yourself,” Brandin advises. “Are you worried about [your teens] using a chatbot because the information it gives them may be unsafe? Or because they won’t listen to you afterward?” Use your fear to locate the skillset you need to teach your teen.

“It’s very easy to get freaked out about the dangers of AI,” Brandin says. Instead, while we wait for systemic support from governments and tech companies, parents can focus on what they can control at home—open communication, exposure and digital education.

The Eggs Scene in &#8216;You Can See Everything&#8217; Scrambles Reality

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This review contains spoilers for the film You Can See Everything.

A version of this post originally appeared in Eater Today, which spotlights the freshest news and stories from across the food world every day. Subscribe now.

I saw You Can See Everything — the new documentary about disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim — on Thursday, and I still feel like my brain is trying to punch its way out of a bag, or like I’ve just awakened from an Inception-esque dream within a dream. It’s difficult to describe the winding, twisting, nearly three-hour film to someone unfamiliar with its many layers of context, from Fielder’s Dadaist, boundaryless approach to his subjects to Holmes’s hypnotic affect, which is either highly effective gaslighting or blinding, self-deluded earnestness. (Which is it? I still don’t know.)

What happens when you take two unreliable narrators — make that three, if you include Holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, whose on-camera persona morphs from protective, naive tech bro to unscrupulous grifter — put them in a jar, and shake them? The result is that the more questions are asked, the further the truth drifts out of reach. It’s like staring at a Magic Eye image, slightly crossing your eyes, desperate to make out the hidden 3D subject but never quite managing to see it. Eventually, you start worrying your eyes might get stuck that way.

The discourse surrounding the film is already abundant, but we’re here to talk about the eggs.

If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. In You Can See Everything, one such detail is observed in an exchange between Holmes and Evans as they make eggs. How much can be gleaned from watching someone make breakfast? A lot, apparently.

In the first half of the film, Fielder is fully embedded in the rental home of Holmes and Evans, who have invited the filmmakers to document the final weeks before Holmes reports to federal prison. (How and why they agreed to this arrangement is incomprehensible if they knew anything about Fielder’s work.) During this time, the narrative takes a surprisingly sympathetic turn for Holmes, who seems intent on convincing the filmmakers — and Fielder specifically — that she is actually a sweet, misunderstood victim of circumstance, not a Machiavellian con woman who defrauded investors. Frankly, aspects of this claim feel convincing at times. Viewers are invited to witness her ostensibly less guarded moments, including a scene in which she makes breakfast with Evans. As Fielder interviews her, Holmes somewhat unenthusiastically and limply beats a bowl of eggs with a fork. The camera lingers on her feeble whisking technique. Then, after a few seconds, another set of hands enters the frame: those of Evans. Without a word, he takes the bowl and fork from Holmes and begins whipping the eggs with considerably greater fervor.

Now, backseat driving in the kitchen is hardly uncommon among couples, though it feels like a fast track to bickering. (Evans tells Fielder that he and Holmes “never fight”; Holmes is also emphatic that she’s never lied in her life. Again, unreliable narrators.) What’s less common — or at least generally considered pretty rude and aggressive — is grabbing something out of your partner’s hands mid-task to take over. Fielder points this out, asking Evans why he felt the need to grab the eggs; Holmes explains that Evans doesn’t like the way she mixes them and thinks he can do it better. 

It’s a tiny interaction, but a remarkably revealing one. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Holmes, meanwhile, appears accustomed to the intrusion, offering an explanation rather than an objection. (Granted, unlike sustained and unblinking eye contact, beating eggs does not appear to be one of Ms. Holmes’s strong suits. Typically, one does so vigorously to aerate them and make them fluffier for an omelet or scramble.)

But with this being Elizabeth Holmes, we must also ask: Could it be a case of weaponized incompetence? Is it possible that, not wanting the task of beating the eggs, she intentionally does so insufficiently to make Evans take over? Is she being controlled, or is she getting someone else to do her work for her? Or is this evidence of her “different” way of thinking, that she truly doesn’t know the “right” way to beat them? 

In interviews in the film, Holmes portrays the Theranos years as a period during which she was manipulated by her former boyfriend and business partner, Sunny Balwani, and maintains that her intentions were ultimately good for humanity, regardless of the consequences for investors and patients who received unreliable test results. Whether she actually believes this account of events, or simply knows how to sell it, remains one of the film’s central mysteries — as does the extent to which her romantic relationships lead her to take the fall for the actions of bad worse men.

These questions reverberate in the second half of the film, when — with Holmes now in prison — we learn some unsettling things about Evans. He has power of attorney over Holmes, and has been using her X account to post odd, profanity-laced remarks that seem entirely incongruent with her own manner of speaking. Suddenly, the eggs scene looks like an early indication that Evans is far more domineering than the protective-partner persona he’s been presenting. His ostensible vigilance toward Holmes may be genuine on some level, but it’s difficult to reconcile with his willingness to license her image for billboards promoting another (bizarre-seeming) entrepreneur’s Theranos obsession, or his apparent acceptance of the possibility that her AI-generated likeness could be used to create pornography or other content antithetical to her interests. 

And yet, even as Evans emerges as an increasingly unsavory character, Holmes remains inscrutable. Does she know what he’s doing? Does she approve? Is he exploiting her circumstances, or are they working toward a shared objective — reputational, monetary, or otherwise? The film offers kaleidoscopic evidence for competing interpretations, including that even if Holmes is being dominated, she may be content with that dynamic. 

What’s extraordinary about Fielder and Oppenheim’s documentary is its optical-illusion-like layers of “truth.” Despite the film’s many harrowing close-ups of microexpressions, there is what Holmes and Evans say, then there is what they do, or have done, or plan to do (launch a Theranos 2.0 called Haemanthus, apparently). There is also Fielder’s own history as a provocateur, a faux-earnest magician who will go to virtually any lengths to actualize a concept, and who seems remarkably capable of keeping a stone-cold straight face through any scenario. 

We know enough about Fielder to question his sincerity, too. There are several moments in the film where I — and the several friends I went to see the film with — wondered if there was palpable sexual chemistry between him and Holmes, and whether Fielder would act on it. If he did, we couldn’t agree on whether it would be genuine or an act of performance art, or whether he has any barriers between those things. 

The film’s most revealing moments are the ones that feel off-script for everyone involved: a fleeting facial expression, an odd word choice in an otherwise emphatic statement, a series of gestures exchanged over a bowl of scrambled eggs. The subjects, who have invited themselves to be watched, have few moments when they seem to forget that; those instances are when we ostensibly have the clearest insight into their reality. Frankly, it’s still murky, and that seems to be both the point and the irony in the film’s title.

You Can See Everything elevates Nathan Fielder’s reputation far beyond simply a master of cringe humor; there is, here, a glimmer of genius. This story feels almost fateful in its ability to showcase his gifts, illuminating the bottomless strangeness, the infinite complexities, of how humans interact with one another. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. And in a documentary that promises you can see everything, we learn that it’s up to us to look closely — and even then, what we see might look different in another light.

How to Stay Informed But Not Overwhelmed by Election News

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You pick up your phone to check one thing—perhaps the weather, a text message, or the score of a game. A few minutes later, you have read three election headlines, watched a clip from a debate, skimmed dozens of comments, and somehow learned that democracy, the economy, your health care, and the future of the planet may all be in danger before breakfast.

If this sounds familiar, you are not alone.

Election season can make the news feel unusually personal. The issues on the ballot can affect our finances, health, schools, communities, and rights. At the same time, the way we now encounter political information—constantly, emotionally, and often in fragments—can leave us feeling overloaded rather than informed.

There is even a name for this experience: election stress. The Mayo Clinic describes it as the overwhelming anxiety some people feel during an election cycle. And surveys from the American Psychological Association suggest that elections have become a significant source of stress for many Americans. In the lead-up to the 2024 presidential election, for example, 72% of adults said they were worried that the results could lead to violence, and 56% said they feared the election could mark the end of democracy in the United States.

It is understandable to feel unsettled when an election seems to carry serious consequences. But anxiety is not always a problem to get rid of; in small doses, it can direct our attention toward what matters. It may motivate us to learn, vote, volunteer, have difficult conversations, or support people affected by political decisions.

The problem begins when information meant to help us engage instead keeps our nervous systems in a constant state of alarm.

Why elections can feel so stressful

Part of election stress comes from the uncertainty it elicits. We do not know who will win, what policies will change, or how the people around us will respond. Research suggests that uncertainty itself can be stressful because people naturally want to understand and predict the environment around them. When we feel unable to do that, we may feel less safe and less in control.

News can intensify that uncertainty. Negative news is often more attention-grabbing than positive news, and conflict, danger, and outrage make especially compelling headlines. That does not mean journalists are intentionally trying to harm us. Reporting on threats and problems is an essential part of journalism. But a steady diet of alarming information can shape how we feel about the world, especially when it leaves little room for context, solutions, or signs of human cooperation.

This can create a difficult cycle. When people feel anxious, they often seek out more information in an effort to regain a sense of control. Yet the more election news they encounter—particularly news marked by uncertainty, conflict, and emotional intensity—the more anxious they may feel. Then they check again.

In other words, we may turn to the news to calm ourselves down, only to leave feeling more activated than before.

Why today’s media environment makes it harder

Political stress is not new. But the way we receive political information has changed dramatically.

When John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon debated on television in 1960, viewers watched in a far more contained media environment. There was no endless stream of clips, reactions, memes, push notifications, and commentary arriving at every hour of the day. People could discuss the debate afterward, but they were not also carrying the debate around in their pockets.

Today, election news can follow us almost everywhere: to the grocery store line, the waiting room, the car, the dinner table, and bed. Social media makes it easier for people to participate politically and amplify voices that were once excluded from public conversation. Those are meaningful benefits. But the same technology can make political information feel impossible to put down.

It also encourages us to consume news in small, emotionally potent pieces. A headline while brushing our teeth. A video clip while waiting for coffee. A dramatic post at a stoplight. These snippets can provoke strong feelings without giving us enough context to understand the story fully.

That matters because headlines often tell us what to feel before they tell us what happened. Pew research found that five out of eight Americans (63%) said they only read news headlines rather than full news articles, specifically when scrolling through social media. When people repeatedly take in emotionally charged fragments of news, they may feel a growing sense of threat while having less detailed information to guide their judgments.

When stress turns into news avoidance

Faced with this constant emotional demand, some people keep checking the news compulsively. Others decide they have had enough and stop paying attention altogether.

News avoidance can offer temporary relief. If every update makes you tense, angry, or helpless, closing the app may feel like self-protection. In fact, a report from the American Press Institute found that a third of Americans want to disengage from news because it feels stressful and overwhelming.

But avoiding news altogether comes with costs, especially during an election year. It can make it harder to participate in democratic life, understand issues that affect our communities, or distinguish reliable reporting from misinformation passed along by friends, influencers, or highly partisan accounts.

The goal, then, is not to consume more news or no news. It is to develop a healthier relationship with news: one that helps us stay informed without asking our nervous systems to be on call all day.

Three ways to stay informed—and calm

1. Choose a news source that includes solutions, not only problems. During stressful times, it can help to seek out reporting that does more than identify what is going wrong. Constructive journalism still covers serious problems, but it also provides context, examines solutions, and asks what people and communities can do next. This approach is not about pretending everything is fine. It is about giving readers a more complete picture: What is the problem? Who is affected? What is being tried? What can citizens realistically do? Solution-oriented reporting may be especially helpful during elections because it can replace helplessness with a more grounded sense of agency and hope for the future.

When you choose a news source, consider asking: Does this reporting leave me better informed? Does it help me understand the issue, or does it only make me feel alarmed? Does it explain possible solutions as well as the threat?

2. Balance distressing news with inspiring media. We do not need to force ourselves to be positive about difficult realities. But we can balance the emotional weight of election coverage with media that remind us of human goodness, beauty, courage, and connection.

Research suggests that stories of kindness, moral courage, and collective care can evoke feelings such as elevation, gratitude, and hope. These emotions can broaden our view of what is possible and help us feel more connected to other people. And new research from the University of Santa Barbara suggests that these emotions can even reduce stress.

This might mean watching a short video about neighbors helping one another after a disaster, reading a profile of local organizers working across differences, listening to music that helps you settle, or returning to a film or show that leaves you feeling more hopeful about people, or nostalgic about the past.

Think of this as building a more balanced media diet. Election news can be an important part of that diet, but it should not be the only thing we feed our minds. Just as eating one healthy meal a week does not transform our physical health, one inspiring story will not undo chronic stress. Over time, though, regularly choosing media that restore us, physically and mentally, can help cultivate resilience, hope, and perspective.

3. Create boundaries around when you check the news. Finally, consider treating news less like a snack and more like a meal.

Snacking on news all day can mean consuming a lot without ever feeling nourished. You absorb a headline here, an alert there, and a few comments in between—but never get enough context to make sense of what you are seeing. The result can be a lot of emotional calories and very little understanding.

Instead, choose one or two intentional times to engage with the news. You might read a full article over breakfast, listen to a trusted news program on your commute, or set aside 15 minutes at lunch to review the day’s major stories. If you are prone to checking headlines compulsively, digital wellness strategies such as app limits or notification settings can make those boundaries easier to keep.

It may also help to avoid making late-night news “snacking” part of your bedtime routine. By the end of the day, our emotional and cognitive resources are often already depleted. A stream of alarming headlines can quickly turn into doomscrolling: the repeated consumption of negative online content that leaves us more agitated and less able to sleep.

Giving news its own place in the day does not mean becoming indifferent. It means making room to take in information carefully, in context, and with enough emotional space to respond thoughtfully.

Staying engaged without staying alarmed

Election stress is real because elections matter. Feeling concerned about the future does not mean that something is wrong with you; it may mean you are paying attention.

But paying attention does not require constant exposure to distress. We can choose reporting that informs and empowers us, balance difficult coverage with media that reminds us of human possibility, and set boundaries that give our minds a chance to rest.

The aim is not emotional numbness. It is becoming the kind of informed citizen who can face difficult news, stay connected to what matters, and still have enough calm and hope left to act.

Your Agent Runs in a Docker Sandbox. Now It Can Join a Team.

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your sandboxed agents just got a team. The official BAND AI kit for Docker Sandboxes is now available at docker.io/bandhq/band-python-kit:latest.

Docker Sandbox kits package everything an agent needs to run in a Docker Sandbox, so a single sbx create command gives you a ready-to-use agent environment. But an isolated agent is only half the story: real work means talking to people and to other agents. The BAND AI kit connects a sandboxed agent to the world. The agent runs on your machine with access to your repository, and joins a BAND room where it collaborates with humans and with agents running anywhere: locally, in the cloud, or on your own infrastructure. The same kit also connects sandboxes to each other, so multiple agents can work together in one room while each keeps its own isolated environment. In this post, we’ll show how a sandboxed agent connects to the world through BAND, using a local agent called Repo Doctor.

BAND provides the collaboration layer for these agents. With BAND, developers can give agents persistent identities, bring them together in shared rooms, route work through @mentions, maintain message history, and surface tool execution events in the room. Each agent brings its own model, framework, tools, workspace, and infrastructure, and joins the room as a participant, wherever it runs.

Docker Sandboxes give that agent an isolated microVM with its own kernel, filesystem, Docker daemon, and network. Inside it, the agent works with local source files, runs commands and tests, and makes changes to a mounted workspace. The BAND kit connects the two with one sbx create command.

Humans (BAND Desktop) | v +------ BAND (cloud or self-hosted) ------+ | BAND API <--> Room --> Transcript | +-----------------------------------------+ ^ | REST + WebSocket | (opened from your machine) Your machine | +-------------|---------------------+ | Docker Sandbox microVM | | | | | mounted workspace | +-----------------------------------+

All connectivity is outbound. The agent opens a WebSocket to BAND and posts its replies through the BAND API, so nothing needs to reach into the sandbox. From the room’s point of view, it behaves like any other participant.

Repo Doctor in a BAND room

Here’s a neat example of how it all works together. Repo Doctor is a small agent that repairs a deliberately broken Python project. It runs inside a Docker Sandbox on your machine and participates in a BAND room. Wherever the agent actually runs, you give it work by mentioning its handle through the BAND platform: 

This example repo includes a band.yaml file that tells the BAND sbx kit which agent to launch. In this example, it points to main.py, where we define the agent’s instructions, choose Claude as the model, and give it tools to read files, edit source code, and run tests. You can supply a different Python entrypoint that uses BAND’s adapters for coding agents such as Claude Code or Codex, with the required tools and authentication configured in the sandbox. 

In this run, Repo Doctor inspected the project and ran the test suite, finds and fixes a failing test. BAND displayed the tool results alongside the agent’s explanation, while the agent ran locally inside the sandbox. 

Connect agents to BAND from your machine

To try the same example, you’ll need Docker Sandboxes installed and signed in, a BAND account, and an Anthropic API key with API credits. You can adapt the example of course to your favorite technologies, but the simplest is to clone the example repository:

git clone https://github.com/dockersamples/band-ai-sbx-demo.git cd band-ai-sbx-demo

In BAND, create a remote agent or use an existing one. Note its handle, which you’ll use to mention it in a BAND room. Set agent.id in band.yaml to its agent UUID, and have its BAND API key ready for the next step.

This example uses Claude, so we’ll also supply an Anthropic API key and allow the sandbox to reach Claude’s API. These settings follow from the agent implementation in main.py in the repo; the BAND kit itself does not require a particular model provider. The BAND agent key authenticates the connection to the platform. 

Store both keys before creating the sandbox. Each command prompts for a secret: enter the BAND agent key first, then your Anthropic API key.

sbx secret set-custom \ --sandbox band-repo-doctor \ --host '**.band.ai' \ --env BAND_API_KEY \ --placeholder proxy-managed sbx secret set-custom \ --sandbox band-repo-doctor \ --host api.anthropic.com \ --env ANTHROPIC_PROXY_KEY \ --placeholder sk-ant-proxy-managed

Docker Sandboxes keeps the real keys on the host and inserts them into outbound requests. The agent sees only placeholder values, and both credentials are scoped to this sandbox.

Now create the sandbox with the BAND kit, allow the Claude API connection, and start the agent:

sbx create --name band-repo-doctor \ docker.io/bandhq/band-python-kit:6.0.0 "$PWD"

The kit already permits access to BAND and the Python package hosts. Add access to Claude’s API for this example: 

sbx policy allow network --sandbox band-repo-doctor api.anthropic.com

Then start the agent in the prepared sandbox. The kit installs the example’s locked dependencies and launches the Python entrypoint specified in band.yaml. The agent connects to BAND, where you or other agents can communicate with it.  

sbx run --name band-repo-doctor

Keep the terminal open while the agent runs. Once the log shows Connected to platform, add Repo Doctor to a BAND room and mention its handle like we did above. 

Create a room and add its participants:

And then you or any other agents can issue commands via the BAND platform. 

A local agent in a larger BAND team

Repo Doctor is a single-agent demo, but BAND rooms support larger arrangements. A room can contain agents running locally, in the cloud, or on your own infrastructure. They can mention each other, add specialists to a room, work sequentially or in parallel, and return the result to a human participant.

For example, a planner agent can plan a change and ask a research agent to check the relevant API. When the work needs your repository, it mentions Repo Doctor. Only Repo Doctor needs access to the local workspace, so only that agent runs in the Docker Sandbox on your machine. After running the tests, Repo Doctor can @mention a reviewer with the result; the full execution events remain visible to the human in the room. The same pattern works across sandboxes: agents that each need their own isolated environment can run in separate sandboxes and still collaborate in the same room.

BAND room |-- Planner agent (hosted) |-- Research agent (hosted) `-- @<your-user>/repo-doctor | v local Docker Sandbox | your workspace

Humans can see the whole conversation. An agent’s context stays narrower because BAND delivers only messages that mention it. Hosted agents can collaborate broadly, with local execution attached to the one participant that actually needs it.

BAND is particularly good at this composition. The local agent is one participant in a larger room, and its peers address it by handle. That saves you from building agent identity, routing, a worker connection back to the laptop, credential handling, and an audit trail before getting to the actual task. The seam between BAND and the sandbox is a WebSocket connection and a room mention, which is about as much integration code as I would like this problem to have.

Getting started with BAND’s SBX Kit

The BAND Python SDK repository contains the SDK and kit sources. The complete Repo Doctor example in the Docker Samples repository includes the agent, fixture, and scripts used to reproduce the screenshots.

Create a sandbox from the published kit, connect the remote agent to a BAND room, and check sbx policy log to confirm its network requests match the policy you intended.

For sandbox kit configuration, credentials, and network rules, see the Docker Sandboxes kit documentation.

Over the past year, while Git traffic grew exponentially, 99.99999% of the Git repositories on GitHub began living in two places at once: our own data centers and Azure. The rest are among the busiest on the platform, and they’re held back by just one thing: a 25ms round trip.

In an earlier blog post, we laid out the two principles behind our next-generation storage architecture:

  • Minimize coordination
  • Decouple storage from compute

In this post, we’ll look at the immediate bridge between our old design and our new one. We’ll talk about how a change to the way our quorum serializes writes gets the busiest repositories into Azure, and how that same step opens the door to replicas that keep no persistent state of their own.

How we got here

The timing and shape of the move were driven by the agentic shift: rapidly growing traffic, a hard ceiling on the power capacity of our data centers and the surrounding geographical region, and physical supply chain constraints. At the beginning of our migration, the remaining capacity with all possible expansion gave us enough time to migrate safely, but not enough to do it slowly.

While we had prototyped new architectural approaches for scaling Git on cloud infrastructure, changing the storage architecture and the hosting platform at the same time, under that growth, carried too much risk. Given our scale and how critical the service is to the community, we needed to sequence the work carefully: move the data first, then evolve the architecture once it arrived.

Git operations are a Tier 0 service: they’re a core function of GitHub, and nearly everything in GitHub depends on the repository data. That meant the data, services, and every dependency had to move first. The first copy of a repository landed in Azure’s Central US region in August 2025. Today, most repositories have two of their five replicas in Azure, and Azure serves about half of Git traffic. For most of that time, Azure absorbed our growth while our own data centers held steady.

During the migration, traffic follows the data. Most repositories have five replicas to balance durability, availability, and scale. To minimize read latency, Spokes uses preferential routing to direct traffic to a replica in the same region and availability zone as the request. As replicas move to Azure, the share of the traffic that can be served from Azure grows with each one, until the last replica arrives and the old region stops receiving requests entirely. Every repository goes through this process:

No repository left behind

So, what about the remaining 0.00001% of repositories?

In our current architecture, a repository hosted in more than one region has a lower write throughput ceiling. Spokes’ quorum only needs a majority to commit a write, but it waits for every healthy replica to respond (up to a timeout) before moving on, so slow replicas delay every write rather than falling behind. In practice, that means the slowest healthy replica sets the latency of every write, and because each write holds a repository-wide lock until it commits, it also sets the throughput ceiling. Once a replica lives in the target Azure region, it’s about 1,000 miles from our data centers, making each network round trip around 25ms, and a write requires multiple round trips. A repository already near its throughput ceiling can’t absorb that on every serialized write; it would fall further behind with every push.

This is where our first principle, minimizing coordination, comes into play. Specifically for this case: don’t serialize what doesn’t need to be. Git’s consistency requirements apply to each reference individually. Once the server accepts an update to a reference, no client should see an older value afterward, only that value or a newer one. That holds whether the update is a fast-forward, a force push, or a delete. But in most cases, updates to different references don’t depend on each other and can be applied independently.

So, we’re changing the scope of the quorum from the whole repository to individual references.

Holding a repository-wide lock for the entire reference update, round trips included, turns the quorum into a single, unordered request queue with one write in flight at a time. Each write in that queue takes as long as the slowest healthy replica. As the write rate grows, so does the queue, and each write waits longer than the one before it.

With per-reference serialization, only writes that contend on a shared reference must wait for each other. In practice, most writes to a repository touch different references—for example, agents and developers pushing to their own branches—so they no longer wait on each other. Each write still experiences the cross-region latency but writes to different references can be in flight at the same time. That removes the single bottleneck on the repository. We can visualize how queueing and timeouts build up by comparing the two approaches side by side under similar load:

These changes raise a repository’s write throughput ceiling roughly in proportion to how many references are being written at once, and get the last remaining repositories into Azure. In internal testing with realistic traffic, writes to different references consistently saw 3–4x higher throughput at lower latency.

Scaling reads on their own

Most repositories are heavily read-skewed, with somewhere between 100 and 1,000 reads for every write. Today, reads and writes hit the same replicas, so they compete for the same resources and can’t be scaled independently.

Decoupling storage from compute changes that. Once repository data is durably stored in Azure Blob Storage and updated with each incoming write, we can begin to decouple read traffic from the write quorum, and independently scale the compute needed for each. For example, reads would benefit from far more in-memory cache than we give them today, but that memory is also needed for compaction. Once they’re separated, each gets its own configuration.

The first step is making object storage a member of the quorum, but a special one that we call a virtual replica. The compute that represents it is our first fully ephemeral node. It serves as a bridge to object storage, streaming incoming writes and serving no reads. Because it’s a member of the quorum, a write isn’t acknowledged until it’s durable in object storage.

That adds a little latency to each write, but just like the cross-region round trip, per-reference serialization keeps it from becoming a throughput bottleneck. The virtual write replica also coalesces transactions on disjoint references into a single set of network I/Os to object storage, so a busy repository pays for one round trip across many concurrent writes instead of one per write. For now, the virtual write replica also handles compaction, and read replicas inherit the compacted state.

With the virtual write replica in place, we can scale out as many virtual read replicas as a repository needs, on demand. Each read replica warms its cache from Azure Blob Storage and starts taking read traffic without joining the quorum, using object storage as the source of truth and reconciling its local state against it in the background.

This step unlocks significant read scale. In internal testing, our distributed load testing harness routinely found ceilings in the current architecture. Against virtual read replicas, the harness hit its own limits first. It also comes with a few challenges that are solved in subsequent steps:

  1. Virtual read replicas start out reconciling with object storage in the background, which makes them a good fit for some read workloads but not all of them. Serving every kind of read requires moving that reconciliation in-line, fetching only the reference state and data that changed since the replica last caught up.
  2. While we’ve incrementally increased write throughput by shifting to a per-reference model, the quorum remains the bounding factor for increasing further.

How can we push write throughput further?

What comes next

The GitHub of today was built on the Spokes quorum, and over the years Spokes has become much more than a quorum coordinator. It’s also an intelligent routing and load balancing layer, a unified API that internal clients use to access and transact repository data at well over 100 billion operations per day, and a collection of background systems we call “sweepers” that handle repository placement, repair, and rebalancing between storage nodes.

The vast majority of what lives under the Spokes service umbrella applies just as well to the new architecture as it does to the existing one. But we also recognize that the commit protocol that has taken GitHub to a billion repositories won’t take it to the next billion and beyond.

We’re not done yet. With these steps in place, the next limit is write throughput. Every write still waits on replicas that each hold a full copy of the repository, yet very little of a write actually needs to be ordered. Git objects are immutable and named by their contents, so they can be stored without anyone agreeing on them. What’s left is small, and the hard part is ordering it as fast as possible without moving the bottleneck somewhere else. Designing for that on a whiteboard is one thing. Doing it underneath a billion repositories that developers and agents are writing to around the clock is another. Getting there forced us to rethink the second-order effects too, like where durable reference state lives and how compaction and garbage collection scale.

Agent traffic isn’t slowing down, and each of these steps gives repositories more headroom as it lands, without waiting for the end state.

Coming next week: At GitHub Universe and in the final post in this series, we’ll take a tour through that end state, what it does for write throughput, the second-order problems we had to solve to get there, and why there’s still a (small) consensus group somewhere in the stack.

From baseline to Secure Software Factory: securing the systems that build your software

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In July, Docker, Snyk and Keycard published Agent Baseline, an open, vendor-neutral reference architecture that defines security outcomes for running AI agents in the enterprise. It grew out of conversations with industry leaders who want to adopt agents more broadly but need answers to three questions:

  • What is operating, with what capabilities?
  • Is it staying inside approved boundaries?
  • Can you prove what happened and stop it?

Agent Baseline gave the industry a shared frame of reference for secure agent adoption by defining the security outcomes for safely deploying agents in organizations. Last week, we published the Secure Software Factory reference architecture, which applies those outcomes to one of the most common agentic workflows: the AI-powered software factory.

Enter the Secure Software Factory

The Secure Software Factory was written by practitioners from Keycard, Socket, Docker, Tessl, Tracebit, Browserbase and Tailscale and shaped by conversations with security leaders at large enterprises in regulated environments such as finance and insurance.

Over the past year, many engineering teams have started building software factories: systems where agents take a request, change code, run tests and hand the result to a human for review. Teams report faster delivery, but also agents doing things nobody asked for, like escaping sandboxes, dropping databases and pushing to production without approval.

Teams need controls that limit what agents can access and do, and check their work at each step. The Secure Software Factory reference architecture shows how to apply Agent Baseline’s security outcomes across this workflow, covering:

  • The factory workflow, from inputs through design, assembly, build and verification, with controls at each stage.
  • Architectural primitives for identity and access, supply chain, network controls, runtime isolation and orchestration.
  • A maturity model with three levels, so teams can start where they are and improve over time.

The paper’s central idea is that the more autonomy you give an agent, the less you can rely on it to police itself. An agent can be redirected at runtime by a prompt, a poisoned dependency or a malicious tool description. Controls have to work regardless of what the agent decides, which means they need to live in the environment it runs in. That means boundaries around credentials, tools, dependencies and network access, backed by evidence of what happened and a human accountable for the result.

This is the part of the problem Docker solves. Containers isolate applications; sandboxes isolate actors. Docker Sandboxes are isolated MicroVMs that constrain agents, deterministically. 

Docker Sandboxes lets coding agents work autonomously in isolated microVM environments, locally or in the cloud, with explicit boundaries around the files, network destinations, and credentials they can access. This supports Agent Baseline’s Constrain outcome by confining agent execution and limiting its reach to what the approved task requires.

Agent Baseline is only useful if the people building the agent stack agree on it. Which is why we are proud to be among leading companies such as Snyk, Keycard, Browserbase, Tessl, Socket and Tracebit, in shaping the baseline and the reference architectures built on it.

More companies are joining Agent Baseline

Widespread PHP Flaw May Cause Many Site Outages Today

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Expect to see quite a few websites serving error pages today: Nearly 70 percent of websites need to update their core software to fix a pair of vulnerabilities that allow an attacker to seize full control over affected servers. Experts say there are working exploits for the flaws, and that these critical bugs are already being exploited in the wild.

Image: Shutterstock.com, Sashkin.

The flaws at issue are present in PHP, the programming language that powers sites running on a large number of web publishing systems, including Drupal, Magento, Joomla and WordPress. Earlier today, PHP released an emergency security update to fix two critical bugs that can allow an attacker to execute code of their choosing on a vulnerable server.

Reuters reports that government websites in Europe and elsewhere went offline today after the PHP vulnerabilities were disclosed. Cloudflare said it has already detected some exploitation of the flaw, and that at 3:02 am (UTC) it implemented web application firewall rules to protect websites from attack traffic seeking to exploit the bugs, which could cause a denial of service condition, remote code execution, and data exposure.

OpenAI, which was involved in the vulnerability research and outreach, said “given the severity of these flaws and PHP’s widespread use, we consider this one of the most impactful security issues in recent memory.”

Censys, a company that tracks the technology used by Internet hosts, said a recent scan identified approximately 2.58 million Internet facing hosts and 54.9 million web properties running PHP.

Security experts at Wiz said every PHP version released in the last decade is affected by at least one of these vulnerabilities [full disclosure: Wiz is an advertiser on this blog].

“These findings rank among the most severe vulnerabilities discovered in PHP, and arguably in software history,” reads a blog post published today by a team of researchers at Wiz, which was one of several organizations credited with discovering the vulnerabilities. “The underlying flaws had been present for decades, but demonstrating their exploitability required an exceptionally complex chain of steps, at the edge of what human researchers could practically achieve. Frontier AI models substantially reduced that barrier, turning a longstanding flaw into an exploit.”

The Wiz researchers say it is likely the PHP vulnerabilities will be mass-exploited to install web-based backdoors or “web shells,” which give attackers a web-based panel that allows them to remotely control infected sites. Wiz said that prior to the PHP bugs being publicly disclosed, the company worked with the PHP team, Calif and OpenAI to measure the size of the problem and coordinate patch verification and disclosure.

The PHP zero-day vulnerabilities are being tracked by their CVE numbers, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, and they affect all web servers running PHP 5.4 or later. The bugs are fixed in PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36, and 8.2.35.

According to Wiz, PHP is so deeply embedded in many web applications that we are likely to see organizations struggling to patch these vulnerabilities for some time to come, comparing this PHP vulnerability pair with the Log4Shell weakness that emerged in 2021 — a widespread, severe-as-it-gets security flaw in Log4j, a popular Java logging framework.

“A vulnerability in core PHP reaches far beyond a single application,” the Wiz researchers wrote. “PHP runs across clouds, hosting providers, content-delivery networks and millions of independently managed servers. Reducing exposure before the technical details became public required coordinated work across that entire ecosystem.”

The SANS Internet Storm Center is keeping an eye on major software platforms that are shipping updates to deal with these PHP flaws. For example, Ubuntu just released updated PHP packages. For a technical deep-dive on these vulnerabilities that tells the timeline of the discovery of these bugs in a comic book style, check out this post from Calif, another technology company credited with finding the PHP flaws.
