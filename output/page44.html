WOLOLOW REINTERPRETS 1968 FUTURO HOUSE AS TABLETOP LAMP

 

Designed by Arthur Koshatahyan and Kostya Trunov, WOLOLOW Night Lamp Ver 2.0 is a compact luminaire that translates the Space Age architectural language of Matti Suuronen’s 1968 Futuro House into an everyday tabletop object. Directly scaling down the iconic portable chalet typology, the second version of the lamp features an elliptical disc-shaped hull punctuated by a ring of oval portholes, elevated on a slender, angled steel tripod base.

all images ©Arthur Koshatahyan, Kostya Trunov

 

 

ELLIPTICAL HOUSING LAUNCHES ACROSS FIVE CHROMED FINISHES

 

Currently launched on Kickstarter, the updated 2.0 edition retains the original fiberglass-inspired silhouette while upgrading the internal illumination hardware. Replacing the previous generation’s integrated battery, the revised model incorporates a hardwired power connection and a standard, user-replaceable 12V MR16 LED bulb with a GU5.3 base. The fixture accommodates standard warm-white modules as well as aftermarket smart LED bulbs, allowing integration with wireless home automation protocols for dimming and RGB color adjustments. The lamp is manufactured in five distinct shell finishes: white, yellow, orange, sky blue, and mirror chrome.

Emergency Seawalls Are Making California’s Beaches Disappear, and El Niño Is Just Getting Started

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

For many of us, beaches aren’t just a place we go for fun or vacation. The more time we spend at any particular spot, or along the coast in general, the more they become a part of our identity. They’re not expendable or replaceable. They’re an integral part of our existence.

The thought of losing one, let alone several, is beyond words.

Yet that is exactly what is happening to an extreme degree in Southern California and around the country. While plausible solutions exist, the powers that be show little concern for serious solutions. It is widely known that you can’t solve a problem with the same thinking that created it. Yet, here we are, dredging sand, dumping boulders, and hoping seawalls will keep everything from falling into the sea.

It begs the question, if data centers can be approved and built in what feels like the blink of the eye, why can’t we restore dunes, build jetties, and deploy other logical approaches to saving our beaches? A typical data center costs roughly $1 billion and takes about two years to build and they’re popping up all over the US. Restoring beaches is far less expensive and controversial. People hate data centers. We clearly need to rethink our priorities.

View this post on Instagram

This El Niño is on track to be the strongest recorded, and its destructive effects are all too obvious. Buildings have been red-tagged, communities are flooding, a nearly 400-foot crack has opened in the bluff under PCH in Orange County, waves are slamming the railroad, San Onofre’s facilities have been damaged and closed, and so many beaches are gone or barely there. With another round of king tides forecast for November 24 to 26 and December 23 to 25, right in the window for powerful winter swells, there’s likely a lot more (expensive) damage on the way.

View this post on Instagram

After storms like these, the response is almost always the same: rock, sandbags, and concrete go onto the beach under emergency permits. The effects of these reactions are detailed in Surfrider’s 2026 State of the Beach Report which graded 30 coastal and Great Lakes states on their policies for preventing unnecessary permanent armoring. No state earned an A, 27 of the 30 earned a C or lower, and 40% earned a D or F. The report describes an “Emergency to Permanent Permit Pipeline”: an emergency is declared, armoring goes in with weak limits, enforcement lags, and the “temporary” structure stays, pinning the beach in place and accelerating erosion on neighboring properties.

San Clemente may be the clearest example. According to Surfrider, a series of emergency permits since 2021 has placed roughly 27,000 tons of riprap along the rail corridor, turning the beach that used to connect the Trestles zone to the rest of town into an impassable pile of boulders.

At a recent Coastal Commission meeting in Oceanside, residents demanded answers over the continued emergency permitting, according to Courthouse News. Save Our Beaches San Clemente Chairman Gary Walsh said the community was told four years ago that officials were studying how to get sand down. “Now, in that length of time, SpaceX has managed to build a rocket and send astronauts around the moon,” he told commissioners. Surfrider’s Mitch Silverstein said the group is “dismayed and alarmed to see over 30 emergency permits for armoring across Orange County,” adding up to around 30,000 tons of boulders on public beaches.

View this post on Instagram

Commissioner Raymond Jackson of Hermosa Beach appeared to agree. “At what point does the word emergency become an indefinite permitting mechanism for something that’s foreseeable, and has been studied for years?” he asked.

The latest permit allows the Orange County Transportation Authority (OCTA) and its partners to add up to 15,000 tons of new riprap and ballast along nearly a mile of track just south of San Clemente State Beach through next July. An OCTA spokesman said the agency understands the frustration and is working to expedite plans to place up to 690,000 cubic yards of sand along San Clemente’s beaches. In the meantime, he said, “in locations where waves are directly threatening the railroad and little or no beach remains, immediate stabilization is necessary.” The line is the only passenger rail between Los Angeles and San Diego.

“So many people are sick of all the rock dumping here in San Clemente,” says SURFER Editor in Chief Jake Howard, a longtime San Clemente resident. “It feels like it’s gotten really out of control, like they’re just panicking and have no idea what to do. And other beach communities appear to be following suit.”

Silverstein, who has been attending Coastal Commission meetings, says armoring the beach to protect the railroad destroys the beach without protecting the railroad. “Everybody loses.” The commission is expected to take up sand placement and vote next month, with a hearing Silverstein says is set for Thursday, November 5. It’s in San Francisco, but anyone can testify by Zoom.

It doesn’t have to be this way. At Surfers’ Point in Ventura, a managed retreat-restoration project that has been decades in the making was completed this March. Rather than armor the beach in place, the final phase moved the remaining bike path and parking lot inland onto Fairgrounds property and rebuilt the shoreline with a cobble berm and sand dunes, giving the beach room to move with the waves. The California Coastal Conservancy approved $16.2 million for the work, and it stands as a model for the long-term planning Surfrider says an emergency permit should lead to.

View this post on Instagram

Surfrider recommends that every coastal state redefine emergencies to exclude forecasted hazards, require written authorization first, limit emergency structures to two years, and follow up with long-term fixes like dune restoration and planned relocation. “An emergency permit should buy time for meaningful adaptation, not become a poor substitute for it,” says Emma Haydocy of Surfrider.

El Niño isn’t a surprise. Neither is sea level rise and coastal flooding. The question is whether we respond with a plan or keep our heads in the (disappearing) sand.

Congo’s Ebola deaths top 5,000 as fear of continent-wide spread mounts

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

BUNIA, Congo (AP) — Deaths in Congo’s Ebola outbreak have topped 5,000 as the country grapples with the relentless spread of the disease and cases are recorded in other African countries, the latest government data on Saturday shows.

Data from the Congolese Ministry of Health showed that the disease has killed 5,026 people out of 10,351 infected, at a nearly 50% lethality rate. The surge is sparking concerns among experts regarding the authorities’ ability to contain the deadly virus.

The latest outbreak, caused by the Bundibugyo virus, was declared in mid-May, catching authorities by surprise. They have since struggled to manage it.

It is already the second deadliest Ebola outbreak in history, second only to a two-year-long West Africa outbreak between 2014 and 2016, which killed 11,000 people across multiple countries.

“It is a very tragic milestone because it is just not a number,” said Dr. Patrick Otim, a programme area manager with the World Health Organization. “It is families and communities that have been devastated by this outbreak. The number also shows the severity of this outbreak.”

2,788 people have recovered from the virus so far. The Bundibugyo virus has no known vaccine or treatment, but health workers treat patients based on symptoms.

One-third of people who have died did not get to receive any care

Since May, the disease has spread to 67 out of the country’s 178 health zones in eight of 26 provinces. Fifty of the affected 67 health zones have active cases.

The virus has been concentrated in the eastern region of Congo, especially in Ituri and North Kivu, which have emerged as the epicenters with 9,782 total confirmed cases and 4,792 total deaths.

Authorities are still struggling with tracing contacts and testing, key components in the race to curb the outbreak.

According to the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, about 80% of new cases are coming from outside the chain of monitored contacts, meaning an overwhelming share of infections are coming from unknown chains of transmission to health authorities.

Also, one-third of recorded deaths are victims who have died outside of treatment centers, before being tested for Ebola, WHO’s Otim said. These issues, health officials say, contribute to community transmission outside of the immediate purview of health workers who can effectively manage infection in accordance with medical protocols.

“These are people we never had opportunity to try to save them and it is one of the biggest concerns for us in this outbreak. How do we try to reach people early to get them to come to health facilities to get a fighting chance to survive?” Otim said.

‘The outbreak is stretching everyone responding to it’

The explosive number of infections in Congo comes on the heels of a long list of issues that have hamstrung efforts to contain the outbreak.

The eastern region of Congo, where the outbreak is concentrated, plays host to a conflict between the government and an array of armed groups, most prominently the Rwanda-backed M23 rebel group, and there is also an Islamist insurgency in the border areas with Uganda, where the Allied Democratic Forces carry out attacks, limiting the reach of healthcare workers.

Also, there is widespread disbelief among the population, which has led to a series of attacks on health workers and health facilities. A nurse was killed while responding to an Ebola alert last week, and a politician from the ruling party was killed in September after advocating for health measures on the radio.

Health workers have gone on multiple strikes, shutting down facilities where they treat infected people, citing long delays in payments by the government.

On Friday, workers at an Ebola treatment center in Tchomia, in Ituri, downed tools, demanding four months of unpaid wages. At the center, there are currently five confirmed Ebola patients and 10 suspected cases under care.

“We are tired of requesting our salaries. If they want to pay us, they will. … Today, being able to feed our families has become a real problem,” Moise Mugenyi, one of the striking workers, told the AP.

Dr. Justin Mukonkole, acting head of response operations in Ituri, said the workers should be patient while the government responds to their demands.

Similar situations have played out in other health zones like Beni in North Kivu, where services in some treatment centers have ground to a halt. Alain Siwako, an elected public official in Beni, said cases are spiralling in Beni because of a lack of fuel and financial resources from the central government.

“Across Ituri and North Kivu, this outbreak is stretching everyone responding to it, including the communities themselves,” Rose Tchwenko, Country Director for the DRC, Prosper Global (formerly Mercy Corps), said.

In October, both Kenya and Nigeria confirmed cases of Ebola. Kenya has confirmed eight cases and 2 deaths, while Nigeria’s sole confirmed case had been evacuated to Switzerland.

The confirmation of cases in both countries, and earlier in Uganda, made the latest outbreak the first recorded in three African regions and the continent’s most geographically widespread outbreak.

James O’Keefe election sting ends in his own felony voter fraud charges

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Conservative activist James O’Keefe set out to expose alleged vulnerabilities in Minnesota’s election system. Now, he faces felony charges stemming from the undercover operation.

O’Keefe, formerly of Project Veritas and current CEO of the O’Keefe Media Group, and conservative influencer Cameron Higby were charged Wednesday with unlawful voting and identity theft after allegedly impersonating registered voters at Minneapolis polling places during the state’s Aug. 11 primary.

According to Hennepin County prosecutors, the men wore disguises, used fake accents and provided election workers with the names of registered voters without their knowledge or permission. They filmed the encounters and later posted videos claiming to expose weaknesses in Minnesota’s voter registration procedures.

The operation targeted the state’s *vouching* system, which allows registered voters to verify the residency of people seeking to register on Election Day. The process requires sworn statements and identifying information from prospective voters.

Neither O’Keefe nor Higby cast a ballot or signed the required vouching oath. Prosecutors contend that misrepresenting their identities while beginning the voting process was itself unlawful.

O’Keefe defended the operation as investigative journalism, calling the prosecution an attack on First Amendment rights. Higby also denied breaking the law, saying the pair intended to expose election vulnerabilities.

The charges are the latest controversy involving O’Keefe’s undercover tactics. In 2017, reporters at the Washington Post caught him trying to sell them a false story. In 2009, he collaborated on hidden-camera investigations targeting Planned Parenthood and gained national attention for videos involving the community organization ACORN. He later settled a lawsuit brought by a former ACORN employee for $100,000 without admitting wrongdoing.

But Minnesota officials say the latest operation demonstrated something different from what O’Keefe intended.

Election workers noticed suspicious behavior and reported it, prompting an investigation. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty said the state’s system for identifying and responding to potential misconduct functioned as intended. This vouching process itself accounted for fewer than 0.6% of votes cast in Minnesota’s 2024 general election, according to state election officials.

O’Keefe and Higby are scheduled to appear in court Nov. 16. Neither has been convicted.

Mark Zuckerberg’s $170 million Miami mansion is just one piece of his sprawling real estate empire that includes a 196-year-old castle

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With a net worth of $222 billion, Mark Zuckerberg is one of the richest people in the world and the owner of a massive real-estate portfolio that spans the entire U.S.

His latest purchase from August is a 440-acre estate and accompanying 196-year-old castle in County Waterford, Ireland, a purchase that reportedly set him back 51.17 million euros, or about $57.1 million.

Yet, his priciest purchases dates back to earlier this year, when the Meta CEO and his wife, Dr. Priscilla Chan, reportedly purchased a luxury mansion on Indian Creek Island in Florida near the in-progress home of Amazon founder Jeff Bezos.

The 300-acre, man-made Indian Creek Island is near Miami and Miami Beach and contains a mere 41 lots with approximately 84 residents, the Wall Street Journal reported. Home prices on the island start at about $60 million, and records show the billionaire paid $170 million for his waterfront home with nine bedrooms and 11 full bathrooms, the Miami Herald reported. Miami-Dade County property records note the owner as a land trust.

Even an undeveloped property on the exclusive island can fetch big bucks: One vacant lot roughly the same size as Zuckerberg’s sold for a reported $105 million in 2025.

The property Zuckerberg reportedly purchased at 2 Indian Creek Island Road puts him in the most coveted area of the already exclusive island, said Mitchell Duchon, a Miami Beach–based real estate agent with the Corcoran Group who specializes in high-end waterfront properties. On the western side of the island, where Zuckerberg’s property is reportedly located, lots are about 80,000 square feet, compared with about 50,000 square feet on the east side, according to Duchon. On this side, where Amazon founder and fellow billionaire Jeff Bezos also owns two lots, residents have better access to the open water of Biscayne Bay and a more expansive view with great sunsets, he added.  

“That side of the island is perceived to be the most appealing,” Duchon said.

Bezos has been buying up properties on Indian Creek Island since he announced in 2023 he was leaving Seattle for Florida. The Amazon founder first purchased a $68 million home that would end up being just a few doors down from Zuckerberg’s in 2023. Near the end of that year, he paid another $79 million for a neighboring property with the intention of combining the lots into a single compound—a trend Duchon said is increasingly common among wealthy buyers because of the scarcity of truly expansive waterfront properties in the area. Meanwhile, Bezos is reportedly living in a third Mediterranean-style property also on Indian Creek Island, on the east side, which he snapped up for $87 million in 2024. Last year, Bezos sold one of his Seattle homes overlooking Lake Washington for a record $63 million, the Puget Sound Business Journal reported.

Indian Creek Island operates as an independent municipality with its own government and private police who patrol by air, water, and sky. Access to the island is controlled by a single gated bridge, making safety and privacy a defining feature. Most of the island’s interior comprises an 18-hole golf course and the Indian Creek Country Club, with a limited number of members owing, in part, to a difficult admissions process and a $500,000 initiation fee.

Zuckerberg is one of the latest billionaires to pick up a Florida property, especially in light of a proposed ballot initiative to be voted on in California next week that would impose a one-time 5% “billionaires tax” for any individual worth at least $1 billion dollars retroactive to Jan. 1, 2026. (To be sure, Zuckerberg still very much calls California home, having invested $50 million through Meta for a Sacramento downtown revitalization and AI-focused project earlier this year.) Google cofounder Larry Page also snagged a $71.9 million home in Miami’s Coconut Grove neighborhood in January that he plans to combine with another property to form a total $173 million waterfront compound.

Indian Creek Island, in particular, is home to a number of high-profile names, who, besides Bezos, include the financier Carl Icahn and former NFL quarterback Tom Brady. 

Zuckerberg’s reported newest property is just the latest addition to the tech CEO’s growing portfolio of luxury homes across the U.S.
