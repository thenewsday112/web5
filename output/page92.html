Meta’s new app lets AI plan your outfit of the day

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Generative AI can be an automated agent, a creative strategist, and a research assistant. Now, Meta has figured out another occupation for its arsenal of LLMs: Personal stylist.

That’s the basic idea behind Style Sketch, a new app that Meta has quietly launched. Though the tech giant provided no official announcement pertaining to its new platform, Style Sketch is already helping its users pick out their outfits and match the items in their wardrobes.

The news of Style Sketch’s launch came from a familiar herald: Matt Navarra. The notable tech leaker posted about Meta’s fashion app on X. He noted several of the app’s features, including integrations with weather apps and a travel planner that chooses an itinerary of outfits for a chosen destination.

Style Sketch is arriving at a time when creators are gaining more influence and credibility in the world of haute couture. Kai Cenat recently took New York Fashion Week by storm with an event that promoted his apparel line Vivet. Meanwhile, Theorist Media’s Creators in Fashion shows have given YouTube stars the chance to strut their stuff in a runway setting.

Many creators express their fashion sense with “outfit of the day” posts, which are typically tagged with #OOTD on social media. Some tech companies, like ShopMy, have developed products that support #OOTD posts.

Style Sketch’s links to the #OOTD phenomenon make it an intriguing addition to the glut of fashion apps. Users can link their profiles to their accounts on Facebook and Instagram accounts — where #OOTD is a popular hashtag — allowing the app’s AI engine to detect the apparel they have already featured in posts.

The potential is there, but users in the West will have to wait before they can try out Style Sketch themselves. For now, the app’s rollout is limited to three Southeast Asian nations: Singapore, Indonesia, and Thailand.

Chile Ski Resort Could Set a New Record With December Closing Date

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It’s beginning to become absurd. 

At Valle Nevado, a popular Chilean ski resort, the austral winter tends to last from June through September or, if conditions line up, October. November’s a stretch.

But this year, Valle Nevado met that watermark after a truly snowy season that allowed it to extend lift operations from October 18 to November 8, marking its latest closing date in history.

Now, Valle Nevado is announcing that it’ll keep the party going all the way until December 1. The November record shouldn’t last too long, weather permitting.

View this post on Instagram

Here’s some context: currently, Valle Nevado is open at the same time as Colorado 2026-27 frontrunners Keystone and Arapahoe Basin. When December rolls around, it should be offering skiing alongside countless North American destinations, from Alta Ski Area to Killington and Palisades Tahoe.

The final stretch comes with tweaked hours and fewer open dates, according to a social media post breaking the news. Following November 8, Valle Nevado will be open Friday through Sunday, with a Monday and Tuesday tacked on at the end. The lifts will also start spinning earlier but close earlier—a common practice at resorts during the warmer months.

Valle Nevado Extended Season Operating Dates
  • Friday, November 13 – Sunday, November 15
  • Friday, November 20 – Sunday, November 22
  • Friday, November 27 – Sunday, November 29
  • Monday, November 30 – Tuesday, December 1

While the sun will start melting out the slopes at Valle Nevado eventually, the resort’s coverage still looks excellent given the time of year. Late season storms have certainly helped. In October, one system brought more than 40 inches of snow. 

Sure doesn’t look like spring.

Valle Nevado

“Over a meter in mid spring and an image that looks like mid winter, but what we are living now, in October,” a Valle Nevado post about the storm read.

The strong sendoff follows a Chilean ski winter that’s been nothing short of memorable. Storms pounded parts of the country in July, bringing feet and feet of snow and shutting down the access road to another hotspot, Ski Portillo, for weeks. One member of the crew there at the time described to POWDER near-endless shoveling between board game sessions.

According to Chile’s Meteorological Directorate, Reuters reported, an intense El Niño episode has driven the surge in snow. 

The ongoing El Niño—a recurring climate pattern tied to ocean temperatures, atmospheric patterns, and their knock-on effects—is still strengthening. Historically, the pattern tends to bring more moisture than usual to parts of South America. For Valle Nevado and others this austral winter, that’s translated to heavy snow.

That might bode well for certain ski states in North America this winter. Long-term outlooks shared by the Climate Prediction Center show raised odds of above-normal precipitation across the southern half of the U.S., including California, New Mexico, Nevada, and parts of Utah and Colorado. 

Earlier this month, the agency said El Niño “looms large” over the winter outlook. We would, of course, prepare—whatever that means for your region—but don’t start counting the flakes before they fall.

#760: Q&A: $1 Million Invested, $100K a Year to Live On. Do We Ever Need to Save Again?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“Oh God, yes.” That’s how fast Joe answered when I asked whether our first caller, Beth, and her husband have already reached coast FIRE.

They’re in their late 30s and early 40s, with two young kids, a paid-off house, and about $1 million invested. They expect to need roughly $100,000 a year once daycare ends, and they want to know: are they coast FIRE now, and will they be in four years, when they plan to downshift?

Joe and I define coast FIRE, agree the math is the easy part, and spend most of our time on the harder question: where flexible, part-time work with health benefits actually exists for two knowledge workers.

Then we help a newly retired dad plan for his high school sophomore’s Ivy League ambitions, including why the CSS Profile matters more than his 1099 income. We close with a listener in his 30s in New York City who wonders if he’ll ever own a home, and my answer comes down to this: rent in Manhattan, own in the Midwest.

Listener Questions

The questions we answered this episode

+

Beth asks: My husband and I are in our late 30s and early 40s. He’s in tech and I’m an accountant. We have two kids—one in preschool and one in elementary school—and our day-to-day support is limited. We’re both working full-time, but we’d love more time to rest, recharge, and spend quality time with our kids while they’re little.

My husband is burned out, and work stress has been affecting his sleep and health. He has a chronic health condition, so health insurance is a huge factor in our plan. My job isn’t super stressful, but I want to keep it low-stress. I don’t want a higher-level role than I have now, and I want more flexibility. In a perfect world, one of us would stay full-time, or close to it, in a low-stress role to carry benefits for the family, and the other would work part-time to manage the home and kids, freeing up more time for both of us.

The challenge is that good part-time options are hard to find in both of our fields. We’re open to retraining, but we don’t know what to retrain into. AI is also on our minds, especially in tech, because it’s changing the work and making us question career security. We also frequently fantasize about completely changing our jobs to do something totally different from our desk-bound office lifestyles.

Here is where we are financially: Our house and cars are paid off, and we don’t plan to take on any debt going forward if we can avoid it. Together, we earn roughly $290,000 a year. We have around $100,000 in a high-yield savings account for emergencies, about $330,000 in taxable brokerage accounts, and a bit over $700,000 in retirement accounts. We save about 40% of our income on average. For the kids, we have modest 529 plans and a separate brokerage account, totaling about $40,000 saved.

We conservatively assume we will need about $100,000 a year to live on once daycare ends, which is in four years when we target our downshift. Coast FIRE appeals to us because stopping work entirely feels scary at our ages, and we want to keep a foot in the working world. The idea is to earn enough to cover our living expenses without drawing down our investments.

Are we at Coast FI now? What about in four years when we plan to downshift? If we are, what should we be thinking about as we pivot, and what careers could realistically provide benefits while allowing part-time work?

Daniel asks: I am a recent retiree from the Phoenix area, and I am now planning college for my sophomore daughter. She is a 4.0 student looking to go to a high-echelon school, maybe an Ivy League institution. I’m trying to get information regarding the college financial aid system, specifically ways to lower my 1099 income for FAFSA, since that interest is what I live off of. I’m also looking to gather information on admissions advisors and SAT tutoring.

Anonymous (let’s call them “Ezra”) asks: I am a younger millennial in my early 30s, and I’ve recently started thinking about my finances a little more seriously in a way I didn’t in my 20s. In the back of my head, I’ve always assumed I would be a homeowner by my mid-to-late 30s, which is when my parents moved into the house where I grew up. But as is the case with so many in my generation, that has started to feel like an impossible dream. Making matters worse is the fact that I live in New York City, and I love it here—it’s the greatest city in the world, and I don’t want to leave.

Do you think homeownership is still a good or even viable financial goal for folks in my generation? Is being a lifelong renter just a financial death sentence that I should avoid, or are there other ways to think of building wealth that might look a little different from how my parents did it, but fundamentally get me to the same place of financial security?

Key Takeaways

The biggest ideas from the conversation

+
  • Coast FIRE means you can stop retirement saving: My definition of coast FIRE: you have enough in retirement accounts that you no longer need to contribute to them. With $1 million invested by their early 40s, Beth and her husband could see that money double roughly every nine years at 8% returns.
  • Hitting coast FIRE frees up your savings rate: Once retirement is covered, the gap between income and expenses doesn’t have to go into retirement accounts anymore. That delta can fund short-term reserves, or simply let one of you cut back hours now, while the kids are young.
  • The best part-time jobs hide in tiny businesses: Below roughly 25 employees, bureaucracy fades and you get a direct line to the decision-maker. Look for W-2 roles with reduced hours, contract work with a small business, or a handful of contract clients cobbled together.
  • For Ivy League aid, the CSS Profile matters most: Lowering your 1099 income won’t move the needle much. The Ivies lean on the CSS Profile, which looks at assets as well as income. Many cover some or all of tuition for families earning under roughly $200,000 to $250,000, if your assets match that income.
  • Put SAT and ACT prep ahead of admissions counselors: With rampant grade inflation, standardized test scores are a better signal of preparedness than GPA, which is why testing is back in vogue at top schools. Prep resources are cheap, and a strong score matters more than polished extracurriculars.
  • Let the price-to-rent ratio decide rent or buy: Divide a home’s purchase price by its annual rent. Under 15 is a slam-dunk buy; over 25 is a slam-dunk rent; 15 to 25 is a gray zone. In Manhattan, it’s over 25, so I’d rent there and own somewhere it pencils out.

Following the success of last year’s event, the University of Utah is teaming up with Woodward Park City for their second annual Rail Jam. The University’s Student Union Lawn will transform into an urban terrain park, starting November 1, with multiple rail and box features. The Rail Jam event will take place on November 6, from 4 to 9 p.m.

“Last year’s inaugural Rail Jam showed just how much energy and creativity this event can bring to campus,” said Erica Andersen, University of Utah executive director of Student Engagement & Leadership, in a press release about the event. “We’re thrilled to bring it back this year, in even bigger and better ways with the help of Woodward, and give students another opportunity to experience this exciting sport and forever connect it to the U.”

Students at the U can register to compete in the men’s ski, men’s snowboard, women’s ski, or women’s snowboard category. The event will take place in a jam-style format, allowing athletes multiple chances to showcase their skill. The winner of each division will get a Woodward Park City Mountain Park Season Pass.

You can see the event recap from last year below.

In addition to the student-athlete competition, a pro-level demo will take place with professional skiers Taylor Lundquist and Tom Wallisch (who is also a University of Utah alum). Admission to spectate the event is free for students and University staff and faculty. Tickets are $10 for the general public.

“Partnering with the University of Utah gives students a chance to drop in, push their progression, and feed off the energy of pro riders right on campus,” said Gar Trayner, the general manager of Woodward Park City, in the press release. “Whether they’re chasing the podium, dialing in a new trick, or experiencing a rail jam for the first time, we want everyone to feel the creativity, camaraderie, and pure stoke that make snowboard and free ski culture so special.”

Crim spent $7K+ on AI services in extortion attacks, then left evidence for all to see

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A n0n ransomware operator tried to break into 23 organizations across 14 countries, but couldn't secure its own front door, leaving its server exposed with all of its methods on view.

The criminals used a mix of new (AI) and old (stolen credentials) tooling during their data-theft spree, and in some cases not only stole and encrypted files, but also deleted data and backups, eliminating victims’ ability to recover their information.

And despite the fear and hype around the breachpocalypse, with AI finding and developing exploits for critical vulnerabilities affecting most organizations across the globe, these crooks did not exploit a single bug to gain entry into any of their victims’ IT environments.

“In every documented compromise in this investigation, access relied on weak or stolen credentials,” said Ensar Seker, chief information security officer of threat intelligence firm SOCRadar.

“What stands out is the combination of AI-assisted development and very conventional entry points,” Seker told The Register. “We found no evidence of vulnerability exploitation in the cases examined.”

The gang breaks into organizations at scale using widespread Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) scanning and automated FortiGate SSL-VPN credential stuffing. To access US-based IT managed services provider ChiBiTek, the crew used a 15-thread residential SOCKS5 proxy pool to test credentials (ip:port:user:password) against FortiGate portals (GET /remote/login).

The operators also gained entry using exposed cloud credentials and reused hosting portal sessions. In one case linked to e-commerce site Fanatics, SOCRadar found that n0n used an exposed AWS access key with permissions to list, read, and delete objects in S3 buckets. The criminals then used this key to pull data from 40 S3 buckets, with up to 110 GB per bucket, and ultimately deleted data, including bank-transaction archives, and purged keys across 12 sensitive buckets.

AI-assisted extortion

While the threat hunters didn’t find any evidence of a fully autonomous attack system, the ransomware operator did use AI to assist with these intrusions, incurring more than $7,216 in AI usage costs by October 22.

Specifically: the criminal or criminals used OpenCode, an open-source AI coding agent, configured with 154 model entries across seven providers. Models included Gemini, Mistral, Nemotron, Llama, Claude, GPT-5.x, Grok, Kimi, GLM, DeepSeek, MiniMax, Qwen, and many, many others, some with safety training removed and others marketed as “uncensored” models.

“The operator used AI to write and debug hundreds of scripts. That shows how AI can help an attacker turn basic access into a broader, tailored operation,” Seker said.

“The destructive behavior is equally significant,” he added. “The operator did more than steal and encrypt files: in some cases, it deleted data and backups, and in one case changed firewall policies to block traffic. That creates a recovery problem that a decryption key alone cannot solve.”

SOCRadar’s threat hunters found the operator’s workspace - a publicly accessible Python HTTP file server hosted in Sweden at 185.157.161[.]148 on port 8877 - last month, and documented the exposed directory in snapshots on September 25 and 26.

“Across those snapshots, we identified 1,719 files, including attack scripts, ransomware source code, extortion infrastructure, and an AI coding agent environment,” Seker said. “This gave us visibility into the operation behind the victim listings, including how the operator prepared attacks and handled stolen data.”

Meet n0n

It also provided a close look at n0n, a newcomer to the data-theft and extortion game that has claimed 108 victims since it emerged last month.

On October 12, the group added 38 maritime, port and logistics organizations across 18 countries to its leak site in a single batch. Researchers have linked at least 21 of them to customers of South Korean maritime software provider CyberLogitec, although the software provider has not confirmed an intrusion.

The crooks set an October 19 deadline for the maritime firms, with ransom demands ranging from $400,000 to $28 million, payable in Bitcoin. After the deadline, however, none of the 38 listings were marked “settled” and none of their payment addresses showed any transaction. All affected organizations’ stolen data was published.

While a handful of the gang’s claimed victims have been marked “honored - data destroyed” on n0n’s leak site, indicating the victim orgs paid the ransom demand, SOCRadar hasn’t validated any transactions on-chain. In its profile details, the security shop rates the gang a 10 out of 10 for "aggressiveness" and a zero out of 10 for “lethality.”

The group is believed to be based in Russia, although the server uncovered by SOCRadar, which it attributed to a single operator or small team, didn’t provide any definitive geographic or national attribution.

Every case that the exposed server documented followed the same lifecycle, moving from initial access to establishing persistence and credential collection via hidden admin accounts disguised as services, covert proxies, credential and session-token theft, mailboxes and emailed multi-factor authentication codes.

The crooks then collected sensitive data and sent everything to the operator’s central storage with the attack impact shifting between data-theft, encryption, destruction, and network disruption, depending on the targeted organization.

After breaking into Transcom, a Netherlands-based financial services provider, the crims sent a secondary ransom alert directly to PayPal's security team, even though the compromised data was only on Transcom's network.

Stupid mistakes and n0n security basics

Finally, the crims send ransom notes, post a countdown clock on the leak site, establish negotiation rooms and emails for victims - and then leak the stolen files or destroy backups when the organizations don’t pay up. In some cases, and to apply increased pressure on victimized companies, n0n also broadcasts automated countdown warnings to businesses’ service providers, clients, and media outlets.

Despite its aggressive tactics, the operator in this case - as with other stupid criminals The Register has gleefully documented in earlier stories - still struggles with basic security hygiene.

“There is a striking gap between operational ambition and security discipline,” Seker said. “The operator invested thousands of dollars in AI services while leaving its own workspace exposed. The locker also contained hardcoded encryption keys, with evidence suggesting reuse across victims. An operation can have serious destructive capability while still making fundamental mistakes.”®
