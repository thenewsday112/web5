7 Places on the Body That Hidradenitis Suppurativa Can Appear

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Key takeaways

  • Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, progressive skin condition that causes boil-like lumps to form in areas with hair follicles and apocrine glands. These lumps may become inflamed, infected, and painful, though HS itself isn’t an infection.
  • HS commonly appears in areas prone to sweat and friction, including the underarms, breasts, groin, inner thighs, buttocks, nape of the neck, and waistband area. Friction from skin rubbing on skin or clothing may trigger flares.
  • Genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors may play a role in HS, though the exact cause isn’t known. If you suspect you have HS, talk with a dermatologist who can provide a proper diagnosis and treatment plan.

Despite what Instagram and photo filters would have us think, no one has perfectly smooth skin over the entirety of their body. We’re all prone to the occasional bump, rash, zit, or ingrown hair. These issues generally go away after the blocked follicle, clogged pore, or minor skin irritation resolves.

But hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is different. HS is a chronic, progressive skin condition that typically appears as boil-like lumps in some of your most sensitive regions (and beyond).

HS lumps or bumps can form interconnected tracts underneath the skin that may become inflamed and infected, causing discomfort and pain. Although infections are often a part of HS, the disease itself is not an infection — it’s due to an ongoing inflammatory response in the body.

In addition to causing pain and discomfort, HS can be extremely frustrating and impact your self-esteem. Identifying HS correctly and early is crucial for getting optimal care, easing symptoms, slowing disease progression, and improving your mental health.

While we don’t know what exactly causes HS, genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors may play a role. Skin regions prone to HS include those where we sweat often and where friction is super common. Friction can occur from skin rubbing on skin, or where clothing can chafe or rub.

Here are seven areas of the body that are often impacted by HS.

Armpits

Our underarms are packed with hair follicles and apocrine glands (aka, sweat glands). With HS, these follicles and glands may become clogged, and bacteria can start to grow, leading to the inflamed boil-like bumps of HS.

If you’ve ever gone for a run and come back with the dreaded armpit chafing, you’re all too familiar with how this area of the body can be prone to skin-on-skin friction or excessive rubbing from clothing. (Lookin’ at you, sports bra!) Friction and chafing can also cause an HS flare-up.

Complicating matters is that some people shave or wax their armpits, and many understandably slather this region with antiperspirants or deodorants. These factors can also clog or irritate sweat glands and hair follicles.

Breasts

The breasts, including underneath and even on the areolae (the area around the nipple), are a common place for HS to show up.

The breast area is a frequent spot for chafing from bras or other clothing. This chafing not only causes skin irritation — it can also trap sweat and bacteria on the skin. The areolae contain apocrine glands and hair follicles that can easily become infected. All these factors can contribute to an HS flare. 

Research also points to sex hormones playing a role in HS, so a hormone shift or imbalance may also make breast tissue more prone to HS lesions. However, more research is needed on the connection between HS and hormones.

Groin

No one can deny that the crotch becomes a sweaty area when life gets steamy, either from the weather, a workout, or both. It’s also a common area for clothing to rub against just from walking and everyday movements. 

As with other HS-prone areas, the groin and genital areas contain apocrine glands and oodles of hair follicles, all of which can become blocked as part of HS, leading to a flare. Speaking of hair down there, many people groom their pubic region, which can also lead to blocked hair follicles.

Again, hormonal shifts or imbalances may also play a role in why these sensitive areas can become hot spots for HS.

Inner thighs

The inner thighs are an extension of the groin area. Similarly, sweat also hangs out here, and it’s a common spot for chafing to occur from skin-on-skin friction, or from clothing rubbing and irritating the area. 

Buttocks

The entire buttocks area is also prone to HS in many people. Areas commonly affected include the anus, the butt crack, and the gluteal fold — or the crease where the buttocks meet the backs of the upper thighs. 

Apocrine glands and hair follicles are abundant here, too. Sweat and other bacteria sources can also lead to HS infections. The buttocks are also subjected to pressure from sitting, which can lead to further irritation.

Although more research is needed, some studies suggest a link between HS and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). IBD is an umbrella term for conditions such as Crohn’s and ulcerative colitis. The potential links between HS and IBD may play a role in why the buttocks area is a common site for HS.

Nape of the neck

Although less common than in other areas, HS can also show up at the nape of the neck. This might be due to friction from clothing collars, jewelry, and other clothing or accessories. The neck is also a common site for sweat, which could block hair follicles in this area.

Waistband area

If skin folds at the waist, or clothing waistbands (especially tight ones!) cause friction, it can lead to HS in the waistband area. As a result, HS lesions may show up on the abdomen, back, or sides. 

tl;dr

HS tends to appear on some of the body’s most sensitive places, including the underarms, breasts, groin, and buttocks. These areas are prone to sweat, friction, and irritation from hair removal — all of which may contribute to an HS flare.

If you suspect you have HS, talk with a knowledgeable dermatologist who can give you a proper diagnosis and help you develop the right treatment and self-care plan to combat irritation and flares so you can feel more comfortable in your skin.

Are De Zerbi's Spurs finding elusive consistency?

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →
Tottenham Hotspur are finally showing signs of consistency under head coach Roberto de Zerbi [Reuters]

The long wait is over. After almost 21 months, Tottenham Hotspur have won three Premier League games in succession again.

Spurs cruised to victory on Saturday against Crystal Palace, who were reduced to 10 men after 13 minutes when striker Jean-Philippe Mateta was sent off for an ill-judged lunge at Micky van de Ven.

It means Tottenham have moved into the dizzy heights of the top half of the table.

Archie Gray and Jan Paul van Hecke scored as Spurs built on a platform established by their first league win of the season at home to Coventry City, then fortified by the outstanding victory at Chelsea - only Spurs' second league triumph at Stamford Bridge in 36 years.

But the prevailing mood inside the Tottenham Hotspur Stadium before kick-off against Palace, undoubtedly shaped by the past, was that the visit of a struggling side represented a banana skin rather than an opportunity.

Tottenham's players weren't having that, though. Not a bit of it. Instead, Spurs grasped gratefully at the helping hand offered by Mateta's recklessness to end up convincing winners.

Three straight wins represent riches indeed. The last time this happened for Tottenham was in February 2025, more than 600 days ago.

Three wins is as many as Spurs had in their previous 26 league games combined, in which they drew 10 and lost 13, going back to midway through last season.

And, if a further indicator was needed as to just how poor Spurs have been in recent times, this is the first occasion when they have recorded back-to-back home wins in the league for two years.

This latest sequence represents consistency of the sort that has eluded Spurs in recent years, the previous incarnation being the consistency of regular defeats and two successive 17th-place finishes in the Premier League.

We are witnessing a significant mood shift at Spurs, reflected inside this magnificent arena since the psychological barrier of the vital first league win was crossed against Coventry.

Head coach Roberto de Zerbi told BBC Sport's Match Of The Day: "The passion we showed - we didn't lose the focus, the concentration. We made an incredible reaction when we lost the ball.

"We could have scored more goals, but we created many chances. We have to be more nasty, especially in the last third of the match, especially in the finishing. I'm happy because I knew before this would be a tough game."

De Zerbi pleaded for patience for his big summer acquisitions, with midfield duo Sandro Tonali, a £100m arrival from Newcastle United, and £85m signing Mateus Fernandes, who came from West Ham United, both starting slowly.

While any optimistic verdict on Spurs in recent times always comes with natural caution attached, the expensive pair are now showing signs of why De Zerbi was prepared to pay such prices to make them part of his transformation.

Tonali started to come to life against Coventry City and here he was pivotal with his running power, physicality and creation. Fernandes was lively throughout, always looking for the opening, the pass that could make a difference.

And once again, to the usual rapturous reception after his long injury absence, Dejan Kulusevski delivered a lively cameo as substitute, a real threat as he also forced a fine save from Palace keeper Dean Henderson.

There is a sense that something is finally stirring at Spurs, that the £300m spent in the summer might just reap a reward – as you would expect for such a lavish outlay.

The statistics confirmed Spurs' superiority, although Crystal Palace battled gamely with their numerical disadvantage, never looking like a side who had given up on head coach Pierre Sage, although he will now come under increasing scrutiny as they lie in the bottom three with only two wins from nine league games.

Spurs had 34 shots and faced just two, the biggest shot difference (32) they have had against any opponent since such Opta records began in 2003-04.

It is also the biggest for any side since Liverpool had 36 shots against four from Wolverhampton Wanderers in May 2024.

Spurs also had 79.4% possession, their second highest on record in a Premier League match after they had 80.2% against Newcastle United in August 2019.

It all left De Zerbi delighted as he talked with renewed optimism and ambition, saying: "Listen, we are Tottenham. We can't forget to be ambitious, to be hungry, to win games in a row, and to make our fans happy.

"At the same time, we can't forget two weeks ago where we have been in the table, to change the last two to three seasons of this club. Three games in a row is not enough. We have to keep this behaviour, this spirit. This condition to prepare every game in the same way.

"After the international break, we spoke together honestly, and decided to change the mood quickly because in football, you can't ask for time, especially if you have good players like we have. I think nothing has changed, but the mood, spirit, the relationship with the players are totally different."

The mood has shifted and - for now - the curve looks to be moving in an upward direction for Spurs and De Zerbi.

Nuno’s West Ham Project Is Beginning To Look Fundamentally Flawed

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

West Ham’s 3-1 defeat against West Bromwich Albion was another deeply disappointing afternoon at the London bio, but I am increasingly convinced that the problems at this football club go far beyond individual players. There is something fundamentally wrong with the way this team is playing, and Nuno Espírito Santo is running out of excuses.

The Hammers were second best for large periods, struggled to retain possession, repeatedly exposed their own defence and looked painfully short of confidence. Jarrod Bowen’s excellent equaliser briefly offered some encouragement, but three avoidable goals and another disjointed performance eventually left home supporters heading for the exits before the final whistle.

What concerns me most is the lack of cohesion. We look like a collection of individuals rather than a properly functioning football team. West Ham cannot defend, cannot consistently pass the ball and look incredibly slow whenever opponents show a little bit of energy or attacking ambition.

West Brom Expose West Ham’s Midfield Problems

West Ham actually started reasonably well, winning a corner inside the opening 20 seconds. However, that early enthusiasm lasted barely a minute before West Brom began taking control.

Arne Engels had a particularly difficult opening ten minutes. He was dispossessed in midfield, struggled with his passing and repeatedly found himself on the wrong side of West Brom’s Ousmane Diakité. At times it looked like a mismatch, with Max Kilman having to step out of defence to compensate for problems in front of him.

There is a much bigger issue here than simply one midfielder having a bad afternoon. West Ham are frequently outnumbered in central areas and struggle to retain possession. Even relatively straightforward passes from deep positions can dissect our midfield and leave enormous gaps between the full-backs and centre-backs.

Divine Mukasa is another example of how badly we are utilising the players available. He is a technically gifted footballer, but spent far too much time dropping deep to collect the ball from the centre-backs. We are wasting his ability by making him come looking for possession rather than getting him further up the pitch to influence the game.

I have previously highlighted the potential of the Engels and Mukasa midfield combination, but there is little evidence of the fluidity and understanding that should be developing between these players.

Arne Engels struggled

Bowen Provides A Brief Respite

West Brom deservedly took the lead after 20 minutes when a long ball completely split the West Ham defence. Jimmy-Jay Morgan got in behind Kilman and Morato before squaring for Felix Horn Myhre to score.

It was embarrassingly easy. There was very little ingenuity involved, just one long ball and a complete failure to deal with the danger.

Morgan should have made it two shortly afterwards, blazing over when presented with another excellent opportunity. West Ham were fortunate not to be further behind, and the frustration around the London Stadium was becoming increasingly apparent.

Jarrod Bowen provided a moment of genuine quality on 29 minutes. Advancing into the penalty area, the West Ham captain produced an excellent right-footed finish to make it 1-1.

It was desperately needed because West Ham had been massively under the cosh. Bowen looked lively, showed some lovely footwork and was one of the few players attempting to make things happen.

But make no mistake about it, West Brom were comfortably the better team during that opening half-hour.

There was another important intervention from Kilman to prevent Diakité making it 2-1, while Kyle Walker-Peters occasionally looked dangerous going forward. Unfortunately, his defensive positioning was repeatedly exposing the right-hand side.

West Ham were not playing with anything resembling the authority of a home team. Supporters were becoming increasingly frustrated, and I found myself thinking that this was simply not bums-on-seats football.

Austbø Error And Another Defensive Collapse

The second half could hardly have started worse.

Within two minutes, West Ham had given away possession through a sloppy pass from Edvin Austbø. West Brom worked the ball forward, with Morgan once again getting the better of Airidas Golambeckis before finishing to make it 2-1.

I felt desperately sorry for Golambeckis because he had actually been one of West Ham’s better players during the opening stages. His reading of the game and anticipation had impressed me enormously, and he had produced some excellent one-on-one defending. That said, he did repeatedly drop 5 yards behind his defenders and played the opposition onside too frequently.

There is no escaping the fact that he was beaten for the goal. Equally, Austbø’s careless pass had handed West Brom the opportunity in the first place.

Airidas Golambeckis was far from West Ham’s worst player.

It was another example of a West Ham team that simply isn’t playing with confidence. Players look nervous in possession, passing becomes increasingly careless and mistakes are being punished.

To be fair, West Ham did attack regularly after going behind. Austbø continued to threaten down the left, and there was a noticeable improvement in the amount of possession the Hammers enjoyed in dangerous areas.

The problem was the lack of a cutting edge.

A Jarrod Bowen corner produced a good header from Kilman which was unlucky not to find the net, while Walker-Peters forced an excellent save with a powerful effort from distance after being found by Manor Solomon.

West Ham were getting into promising positions, but there was very little creativity through the middle. Almost all our attacking threat was coming from the wings.

Morato Makes A Complete Hash Of West Ham’s Afternoon

Just as West Ham appeared to be building some pressure, another dreadful defensive error effectively settled the game.

On 72 minutes, West Brom won a corner and Morato made a complete hash of his clearance. Rather than properly getting the ball away, he simply flicked a foot at it, producing virtually no distance and failing even to clear the penalty area.

Callum Styles collected the opportunity and struck a half-volley which bounced through a crowd of players before finding the net.

It was another horribly avoidable goal.

West Ham had enjoyed plenty of possession in the second half and were not necessarily short of attacking opportunities. But the defensive mistakes were proving catastrophic, and there was precious little evidence of a team capable of mounting a serious comeback.

Nuno Reverts To His Familiar Plan

Once again, when West Ham found themselves chasing a game, Nuno reverted to his familiar solution of introducing three centre-backs.

Kyle Walker-Peters had picked up an injury after being booked, and Joël Veltman came on alongside Tomáš Souček and Taty Castellanos. Nuno reshaped his side, withdrawing Golambeckis.

But it didn’t appear to cause West Brom too many problems. If anything, the visitors were left with plenty of opportunities to attack, while West Ham seemed to almost completely surrender control of midfield.

I struggle to understand what Nuno is trying to achieve with some of these tactical changes. There is a recurring pattern of West Ham falling behind, changing formation and then looking even less convincing as a team.

Souček barely featured, Castellanos was almost anonymous and his two efforts from distance hardly suggested a striker desperate to rescue his team. One particularly ambitious attempt sailed so high it had a better chance of ending up between the posts at Twickenham than troubling the West Brom goalkeeper.

West Ham supporters began streaming out of the London Stadium with ten minutes still remaining.

West Ham Have A Much Bigger Problem

I don’t believe this can simply be explained away by pointing at individual players. Yes, there were some dreadful performances and some equally dreadful mistakes, but there is something inherently wrong with this team.

We have no confidence and no cohesion. We struggle to pass the ball, we struggle to defend and we look painfully slow. It doesn’t take particularly good opposition to cause us problems. Anyone with a little bit of effervescence or vibrancy seems capable of making life extremely uncomfortable for West Ham.

I cannot fault Pablo and Mohamadou Kanté for their effort, and I suspect Kanté could develop into an effective footballer. But the attacking partnership was thoroughly uninspiring and never looked particularly threatening.

There is also a question about the way Nuno is managing this squad. The constant changes, the formations and the apparent inability to settle on a system are not helping matters. I would genuinely be intrigued to see what another coach could achieve with these players.

West Ham have sold a considerable number of players and rebuilt much of the squad. There is an argument that the manager needs time, but there is also an expectation that a team containing this much Championship quality should be considerably more convincing than this.

The supporters are entitled to expect better.

Plans to improve the atmosphere at the London Stadium with fan zones and dedicated singing sections are all well and good. But if the team is going to play like this, it will be a futile exercise.

We stand on the precipice at the moment. The club, the manager and the players all have serious questions to answer.

And after another afternoon like this, I am not sure any of them have provided particularly convincing answers.

But they’ve only won once in the league so far this season and it could catch up with the mastermind of their 2025 promotion.

Some Sunderland fans are starting to turn against Le Bris – but what direction would they go in if they made a change?

It’s a hard one to predict, given how much the landscape has changed since Sunderland last had to pick a new manager as a Premier League club. That was 10 years ago, when they handed the reins to David Moyes before going down.

If Sunderland get rid of Le Bris, they need someone who can both be a firefighter and continue the project they’ve been building in recent years.

We’ve picked out six options who could keep Sunderland up.

Sean Dyche

Nothing says panic button quite like turning to Dyche these days but we can’t help but feel he’s become a bit underappreciated.

It never worked out for him at Nottingham Forest and he wasn’t up to the task at Everton before that, but his efforts over a decade with Burnley earned him plenty of admirers.

It’s been less than five years since that venture ended and at the age of 55, Dyche still has something to give as a manager – if he’s willing to put his rebirth as an influencer on hold.

Dyche’s Burnley teams at their best were hard to break down and got the basics right. Football may be evolving, but Sunderland were caught short with those kind of principles against Coventry, conceding far too much space to their opponents and giving the ball away cheaply.

We imagine Dyche wouldn’t stand for any of it.

Thomas Frank

There aren’t many managers who’d produce a more polarising outcome if you asked the fans of their last two clubs how good they are.

Frank worked wonders at Brentford before being a disaster at Tottenham. Could Sunderland fit the bill as a club with expectations somewhere in between for him to get back on track?

Brentford have kicked on even after Frank left, which makes you wonder if his success there was more down to the broader network.

But you can’t ignore that he was able to get a newcomer into the Premier League and stabilise them there. At this rate, Le Bris might fall at the second of those hurdles.

Frank knows what it takes to keep a team away from danger while still playing adventurous football, so could be the kind of figure Sunderland need to stay on track in the short-term and bigger picture.

Vitor Pereira

Pereira’s dismissal by Nottingham Forest after steering them to safety last season was on the harsh side – especially since it happened via email.

Forest were one place above the relegation zone when he replaced Dyche in February, but they finished the season in decent form, only losing one of their last 10 league games.

While Pereira didn’t have to lift them out of the bottom three, he helped them stay clear of it.

If Sunderland can find that sort of impetus, it’d be most welcome. Whether Pereira would be the right man to fix things in the long term, like Forest wondered, is up for question, but his efforts last season shouldn’t go unnoticed.

Dino Toppmoller

If Sunderland opt to go further afield, like they did when hiring Le Bris in the first place, then Toppmoller might be a manager of intrigue.

He recently lost his job at Lens just six games into his reign, reportedly over disagreements with the club over who should stay on his coaching staff.

Before that, he built his reputation up over a two-and-a-half-year spell with Eintracht Frankfurt, who were a consistent top-half Bundesliga side in his time there.

He also led them to the Europa League quarter-finals in 2025, which Sunderland would surely love to emulate this season.

Does he have the credentials to oversee a relegation battle? That’s a bit less clear, but Sunderland should be thinking big anyway after what they did last season. Get a run of form together and they’ll be fine – and Toppmoller might be capable of inspiring just that.

He’s turned an underdog into a team that always need to be taken seriously. That’s what Sunderland want to do too, even if they can’t boast the unique environment that traps some of Knutsen’s opponents in the Arctic circle.

Knutsen’s track record is strong enough to suggest he delivers big wins not just because of conditions but because of footballing and tactical quality. This is the kind of opportunity he always seems to be in the conversation for and deservedly so.

Expulsion from a league is not without precedent. Both Bury and Maidstone United were kicked out of the Football League due to financial reasons but, crucially, had not played a match that season.

It would, though, be unheard of in Premier League history and would have a knock-on effect for all other 19 clubs, not to mention the ones who have already played City.

If City are expelled during the season, the most likely outcome is that their results would be expunged from the record. How different would that make the current Premier League table look?

If City were expelled during the season, Premier League Rule C.15 states that their results would be expunged from the league table and the number of relegation places reduced by one. However, if they were expelled after the season had concluded, C.15 would not apply in the same way, meaning their completed results would not automatically be wiped from the table.

City had won their first seven Premier League games, meaning none of their rivals would have lost any points from the hypothetical expulsion.

But losses to Aston Villa and Brighton change things slightly. They’d be the only team to lose three points, having become the first side to take anything off City this season.

The obvious place to start then is that every team would move up one spot due to City currently being top of the table.

That would put Arsenal top with 21 points from eight matches, five clear of Brighton in second. Chelsea would be third, albeit with a game in hand.

At the other end, Crystal Palace, Nottingham Forest and Sunderland would occupy the bottom three, although the reduction in relegation places would mean only Forest and Sunderland would be going down.

As for the goals, Sunderland would be happy to see five wiped off their conceded tally although would lose three of their own. Liverpool and Ipswich would see three goals wiped off too.

It would be win-win for United and Coventry who would have one taken off their conceded without anything happening to their scored column.

Bournemouth and Palace both scored one in their defeats to City so would also be up overall in terms of goal difference.

Brighton would boast the best goal difference in the division at +16, having scored an impressive 23 goals in their eight matches against the other 18 clubs.

It reinforces something we’ve known since we first started watching, playing, and consuming this glorious sport: teams function better when roles are clearly defined. “Positionless” basketball is a lazy term for those who support teams that do not possess defined roles. For teams with duplicative archetypes in the same starting lineup. “It’s gonna make sense,” one will say of the starting five. “Why?” a curious and befuddled onlooker will question. “Because the NBA is now ‘positionless’”.

I’m guilty. I’ve bought into the cheap trick and five-dollar fantasy that positionless basketball could equate to success. I purchased some stock in the belief that a Booker/Beal backcourt could be productive, and it’s why I’ve been cautious about the Booker/Green backcourt since its inception. Fool me once, shame on me n’ such. Sure, you can have a pair of combo guards in the backcourt, but they still need to have defined roles, defined areas of space in which they operate, and one better be pretty damn good at being the primary ball handler. Some call those roles “positions”.

Positionless basketball is something that has become a product of the AAU circuit, where young players are encouraged to pad their statistics because, ultimately, those numbers help them get noticed and create opportunities to make it big one day. With the NIL era upon us, getting noticed has become even more financially important. Somewhere along the way, traditional point guard play, facilitation, and distribution have become less of a priority. Those are the things that make everyone around you better, even if they don’t always show up in the box score.

Within the concept of positionless basketball, there still needs to be an understanding of roles and, more importantly, who is actually capable of executing them. What we witnessed Friday night, and quite frankly what we’ve seen throughout a season and a half, is another reminder that this offense simply functions better when Collin Gillespie is involved. He’s not a traditional point guard, but he excels in that role. That’s what the Suns need right now.

Why? Because Jalen Green was having an off night, something that has unfortunately plagued him throughout his career. Consistently, he’s been inconsistent. 20 points on 8-of-13 shooting against OKC, 2 points on 1-of-6 shooting against Denver. When you look at his career scoring trend, you see dots scattered about like duck shot on an outhouse.

I can’t blame the young lad. This team wants Jalen Green to be a facilitator, to essentially play point guard. And when you’re forced into an unfamiliar role, it takes time to adjust. Unfortunately, point guard is one of the most difficult positions to play in the NBA. It requires selflessness, court awareness, and the ability to process information at an incredibly high speed.

You can almost feel Jalen thinking out there early this season. He’s trying to read the defense, make the right decision, and execute what’s being asked of him. And the best flashes we’ve seen? They come when he stops thinking and starts reacting. That’s his superpower. His instincts, explosiveness, and ability to attack without hesitation are what make him dangerous.

It reminds me of Ryan Dunn, who’s being asked to become a true 3-and-D player despite that not necessarily being what his natural skill set lends itself to. Once again, the Suns find themselves asking a player to become something he isn’t, rather than maximizing what he already does well. And in the process, they’re diminishing the very qualities that make him valuable.

Green is better off the ball, operating as a piece of the offense rather than being the driving force behind it. Therein lies the roster construction conundrum we’ve been staring at for two seasons: 57% of your cap is tied up in duplicative players. Because the same could be said about Devin Booker. He excels when his role is not the offensive facilitating fulcrum. That, my fellow Suns’ citizens, is criticism I’ll save for another day…

We heard throughout the offseason that Green had spent time working with Steve Nash, with the goal of improving his ability to read opposing defenses and make quicker decisions. Early this season, it’s apparent that his processing is taking a split second too long. His assist percentage is 14.1% (33rd percentile), his assist-to-turnover ratio is 1.3 (14th percentile), and his assist points created is 6.0 per game (25th percentile).

In the NBA, where you’re facing elite athleticism and basketball IQ night after night, a split second can make all the difference in the trajectory of a career. Unfortunately, when you’re looking for someone to make high basketball IQ plays with split-second decision-making, Green typically isn’t the first player who comes to mind.

It’s a reminder that Jalen Green entered the NBA following his time with the developmental G League Ignite as a player whose greatest asset was his elite athleticism. He jumped off the high school screen with his explosiveness, his ability to finish around the basket, his shooting potential, and his impressive physical tools. He possessed the kind of raw talent that makes scouts salivate and front offices dream about what a player could eventually become.

His career thus far has been relatively average, with much of his scoring production coming through volume rather than efficiency. The physical gifts are undeniable. The challenge has been turning those gifts into consistently productive basketball.

Collin Gillespie took an entirely different path. He went from fighting for an opportunity on a two-way contract to earning a fully guaranteed deal and eventually a contract extension. And what keeps him on the floor? It certainly isn’t elite athleticism. It’s his defensive effort, his ability to read the floor offensively, his understanding of how to operate within a system, and his efficiency, particularly from beyond the arc.

So why am I bringing up the career paths of these two players? Because they represent two fundamentally different approaches to succeeding in the NBA. Green entered the league relying on extraordinary athleticism and the freedom to operate outside traditional positional responsibilities. Gillespie has built his career by understanding those responsibilities, embracing them, and executing within the structure around him. One was given opportunities because of what his physical gifts suggested he could become. The other earned his opportunities by consistently demonstrating that he understood exactly what his team needed him to be.

Collin Gillespie is more comfortable handling the ball, navigating his way through defenses, creating mismatches, and reading what the opposition is doing as those opportunities develop. Through four games this season, his assist percentage is 18.1% (38th percentile), his assist-to-turnover ratio is 3.3 (36th percentile), and his assist points created is 14.5 per game (64th percentile). Not gaudy numbers, but certainly improvements over what Green has provided in this small sample size to start the 2026-27 season.

When Booker was off the court and Green was tasked with the “bring the ball up the court” duties, the ball became sticky. The offense was less fluid. He’d dribble for a while, attack the basket, get blocked, and that was the possession. No movement, no opportunity for anyone else to get involved.

Green killed the Suns’ momentum on that possession. Dribbled into a crowded paint, never looked to kick it out, and forced a shot that got blocked. Murray answered with a three.

I’ve hit you with a ton of advanced metrics and lineup data, but do we really need all of that? Pope Leo tells Villanova and Notre Dame basketball teams he is neutral. Prevost has other ideas

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

VATICAN CITY (AP) — Pope Leo XIV on Saturday told the basketball teams from his alma mater Villanova and fellow Catholic university Notre Dame — in Rome for their season opener — that in matters of sport, the pontiff is neutral.

Then the pope, who was born Robert Prevost, said his former self might disagree.

“Prevost has another opinion, but we won’t do that now,” he said to laughs during a whooping, cheering audience of 7,000 people from both schools — players, coaches, fans and their families — in the Vatican audience hall.

The teams were in town for the U.S. college basketball season opener in Rome. The Villanova and Notre Dame men’s and women’s teams will play a doubleheader on Sunday at the 11,000-seat Palazzo dello Sport, about a half-hour drive from the Vatican.

The event has been dubbed the Eternal City Tip-Off and was created to honor the first American pope.

Jay Wright, the former Villanova coach who led the men’s team to two NCAA championships and was inducted this month into the National Collegiate Hall of Fame, said “this pilgrimage to Rome and meeting the pope is bigger than the game.”

The audience had been up in the air after the 71-year-old pope underwent lung cancer surgery on Oct. 13 and is still recovering. But Leo kept the appointment ahead of Sunday’s opener.

Leo graduated in 1977 with a Bachelor of Science in mathematics from Villanova, the Augustinian school near Philadelphia. He was born in Chicago — not all that far from Notre Dame, which is located in South Bend, Indiana.

Leo dribbles and poses

Leo, an avid sports fan and tennis player, dribbled a golden basketball presented to him by the Villanova women’s team. And when the towering players from Notre Dame presented him with a jersey and another golden ball, Leo stepped up to the podium where his chair sits to try to gain a few inches (centimeters) on them.

He gamely posed for a photo with the Fighting Irish, holding the banner they gave him “Pope Leo, Play like a champion today” — a riff on the famous Notre Dame sign at the university’s football stadium.

But he gave Villanova equal time, making the “V'” gesture with his hand as chants of “Let’s Go ‘Nova” erupted in the audience hall.

“He is a true Villanovan,” said the Villanova women’s coach, Denise Dillon. “I said, ‘OK everyone: V’s up.’ And he immediately was in with it.”

Sport as a way to saintliness

After mistakenly starting out in Italian, Leo switched to his mother-tongue English and spoke off the cuff, recalling that in his native Chicago, he grew up hearing “Go Irish,” the Notre Dame chant.

“But as you all know, Prevost graduated from Villanova,” Leo said, referring to his past self in the third person.

“So the pope doesn’t take sides,” he said to laughs, before revealing that “Prevost has another opinion.”

In his prepared remarks to the crowd, Leo urged the teams and their families to embrace sports as a way to grow in saintliness.

“No one is born a champion, just as no one is born a saint,” Leo said.

He warned against letting sporting rivalries get out of hand and he said he hoped the fans in Rome this weekend would actually get to know one another as they cheer, “even passionately.”

“In any competition, the members of two teams, even when they have a competitive rivalry, are certainly not enemies,” he said. “I hope, therefore, that despite the competitive nature of college basketball, there will be opportunities for the players and the fans of both teams to get to know each other in your days here in Rome.”

He noted that the game will take place on All Saints’ Day, and that the church teaches Christians to pursue saintliness in every aspect of their lives, including in sports.

“Our true goal, beyond emerging victorious in any one event, is to win the prize of everlasting life,” he said.

Leo and past popes have exalted sports

Like popes before him, Leo has praised the value of sport for both physical and spiritual development, calling sport a “school of fraternity” that nurtures friendship, respect and teamwork. But he also warned about how the win-at-all-cost mentality of sport has contributed to episodes of doping, match-fixing and other forms of corruption.

The Rev. Robert Dowd, president of Notre Dame, acknowledged Villanova had something of an edge in Sunday’s game, considering it counts the pope as an alumnus. But in an interview Saturday, he said, “The pope is a pope for all of us.

“The pope talks about the importance of bridge building,” Dowd said, “and I’m sure he’ll be a bridge builder when we play each other.”

Villanova’s president, the Rev. Peter M. Donohue, said there was “a little extra pride” being with Leo, given his allegiance. But he said what happens on Sunday was not the point.

“No matter what happens on the court, this is the true highlight of the trip,” Donohue said in his introductory remarks to the audience. “Tomorrow we will cheer, we will compete, but today we are simply grateful.”

Philadelphia Archbishop Nelson Perez celebrated a private Mass for traveling fans in St. Peter’s Basilica on Saturday morning, and asked the thousands of people in the pews to give a round of applause to the players. The English-language liturgy was unusual in that it had all the trappings of a papal Mass celebrated at the main altar and televised live by Vatican Media.

Leo is an avid Chicago White Sox fan, and has been known to follow basketball, too.

Asked about the upcoming games on the papal plane during a recent trip to France, Leo said, “They tell me that the people coming to the match, 90% are Villanova and 10% Notre Dame, so the voice of the people of God will be very strong on the Villanova side.”

Some wondergoals are almost impossible to replicate, seeming to defy the laws of physics. Others less so.

What makes a goal great? The recipe seems to be a mix of technical skill, distance from goal and ideally some contextual importance. (Can a friendly produce wondergoals? Arguably not.)

In more general terms, great goals are often marked by their difficulty.

When Gareth Bale or Cristiano Ronaldo execute a perfect bicycle kick, you think to yourself: only they could do that.

And when Lionel Messi glides past about 47 Sporting Gijon players before slotting the ball into the net, you get a similar feeling.

But just how significant is the difficulty factor when evaluating a wondergoal?

Sure, you sit up and take note when Roberto Carlos bends the rules of physics. But are bent physics part of the criteria? Can’t a Sunday League goal be as good as a World Cup goal?

Using these nine famous strikes as evidence, I’d say they can.

In fact, sometimes a goal is better when you think, “Yeah, but my nan would have scored that.”

You just can’t teach that technique: the way he lets the ball drop over his shoulder before pulling the trigger is part instinct, part superpower.

The height on that diagonal ball, while making for a delightful aesthetic spectacle, hardly makes things easy for him, but it raises an important question: is it worth trading off efficiency for beauty?

The goal might have been more achievable if his team-mate had carried the ball forward and found a better angle for the delivery, but then it would have been just another goal.

The execution would have been difficult even without the ball passing over the head of a defender. The Porsche MA1.04

In the bible of engine reliability, you need a couple of things: A proven design and architecture, proper usage cycles and care, and religious maintenance and service intervals. The Porsche MA1.04 is part of a series of internal combustion engine families from Stuttgart that is regarded as some of the brand’s most reliable powertrains it has ever produced, so for one to come into the shop for a teardown is somewhat of an event.

Even some more modern Porsches aren’t exempt from the occasional engine problem here and there. Porsche’s very quick to revise their design once a flaw is identified, but somehow, the MA1 series just got it mostly right. While not perfect, the flat-six still has a few failure points and maintenance-intensive areas that owners should know about, but none of those failure points appeared to be the culprit when this motor decided to blow.

So that’s why it blew up.

Right off the bat, I Do Cars found the root cause. There’s a crack in the oil pan. So the engine ran without oil once the level dipped to dangerous levels, and it soon became a coffee table.

The damage done was reportedly $10,000 at the time for a new motor without labor. The engine was graciously donated by a fan of I Do Cars after being sat for a while. While the damage to the owner’s wallet is documented, the full extent of the damage to the motor and why it was replaced was only revealed towards the end of the video.

In the middle of the teardown, I Do Cars did notice a few things that were not ideal for a motor, like some shavings and fragments in the combustion chamber. That was just a teaser of things to come, however.

I Do Cars - YouTube

What Blew Up?

So after getting all the nuts and bolts out of the assembly, the engine comes apart. It’s interesting to note that the MA1 splits apart right down the middle. Once the flat six turned into two inline-threes, we get to see what went wrong. Up until this point, everything was peak German precision. However, the deeper I Do Cars dug, the more heartbreaking it ended up being.

All the rods and pistons were intact with acceptable wear, and even the rod bearings were good, but they have seen better days. That all changes, however, when the camera pointed to the main bearings. One bearing welded itself to the crankshaft, while the rest looked like it had been mangled.

That’s not the worst part, though. On one half of the block, we can see a nasty score at the crankcase. Basically, chunks of aluminum are just sitting there in a crevice. This is a bad case of a spun bearing, and it was enough to basically stop the engine dead in its tracks.

The fact that this was all caused by a hit to the oil pan makes this even more heartbreaking. Now, yes, some parts from this motor can still be reused by resourceful builders, but the block itself might be out of commission. An extremely skilled machinist might be able to repair it, but it could cost more to repair than to replace as a whole unit. Maybe the block is destined to be turned into a coffee table after all.
