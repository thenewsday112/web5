Crime Is Down: Why Leadership Matters

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

For years, Americans were told by the left that rising crime was either exaggerated, the result of poverty, caused by COVID, or simply the unavoidable cost of modern life. None of those assertions is true.

Victims, police officers, prosecutors, and families living in high-crime neighborhoods knew better. They understood that crime is not an abstraction. It is a stolen vehicle, a broken window, a family traumatized by violence, or a neighborhood slowly surrendered to fear.

That is why the preliminary results of the just-released 2025 National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS) deserve serious attention. The numbers point in one direction: crime fell substantially across nearly every major category measured by the survey.

The results are preliminary, and responsible observers will await final estimates and additional analysis. But the trend is unmistakable—and it coincides with a national agenda that has placed public safety, border security, law enforcement, and support for victims at the center of federal policy.

According to the preliminary estimates from the Bureau of Justice Statistics, the rate of serious violent victimization—violent crime excluding simple assault—fell 25%, from 9.9 victimizations per 1,000 people age 12 or older in 2024 to 7.4 per 1,000 in 2025. Aggravated assault declined even more sharply, dropping 31% from 4.5 to 3.1 per 1,000.

>>> Americans Lost Over $20 Billion to One Type of Crime. Here’s How We Can Fight It

The property-crime figures are equally striking. Property victimization declined 19%, from 138.0 per 1,000 households to 111.2 per 1,000. Burglary or trespassing fell 25%. Motor vehicle theft declined 30%, representing approximately 349,000 fewer stolen vehicles. Other household theft declined 19%, while burglary and trespassing represented roughly 460,000 fewer victimizations.

These are not minor statistical adjustments. They represent fewer people harmed, fewer families disrupted, and fewer communities forced to absorb the economic and emotional costs of crime.

The NCVS is particularly important because it captures criminal victimization whether or not the victim reported the offense to police. Each year, the Bureau of Justice Statistics gathers information from a nationally representative sample of approximately 240,000 people age 12 or older in about 150,000 households.

It therefore complements the FBI’s Uniform Crime Reporting data, which are based primarily on information supplied by law enforcement agencies.

The two measures tell a consistent story. The FBI’s 2025 data showed the largest year-to-year decline in violent crime since the agency began tracking national crime trends in 1936. Murder and non-negligent manslaughter declined by an estimated 18.1%.

The NCVS now provides an important second measure, reflecting the experiences of victims themselves.

What explains the decline? No serious analyst should pretend that a single policy produces every change in national crime. Crime rates are influenced by many factors, including local policing strategies, prosecution decisions, economic conditions, demographics, and community involvement. Preliminary survey results also cannot establish a definitive cause-and-effect relationship.

But policy choices matter. A president who makes securing the border a priority, removes violent illegal aliens who are subject to deportation, directs the Justice Department to cooperate with the nation’s approximately 18,000 police departments, 3,400 sheriffs’ offices, and 2,300 elected district attorneys, funds law enforcement, and supports victims is sending a clear message: the government’s first responsibility is to protect the innocent.

That message matters. Criminals should expect enforcement, not indulgence. Police officers should know that federal officials recognize the risks they face. Prosecutors should have the resources and cooperation necessary to hold dangerous offenders accountable. Victims should not be treated as an afterthought in a system more concerned with the comfort of defendants than the security of communities.

“This report is proof that President Trump’s ’Make America Safe Again’ agenda is working,” Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward said, the No. 3 official in the Justice Department.

The statement captures the broader significance of the data, even if conclusions should await final estimates: public safety improves when government treats it as a core mission rather than a campaign slogan.

The lesson is straightforward. Effective enforcement, cooperation among federal, state, and local authorities, secure borders, adequately funded police departments, and meaningful support for victims are not outdated ideas. They are practical necessities.

Americans should celebrate these preliminary gains while remaining vigilant. The goal is not merely to report that crime has fallen. The goal is to ensure that it stays down—and that every community, regardless of size or location, receives the protection its residents deserve.

Food-Themed Fashion Is Starting to Feel Gluttonous

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Loewe | Loewe

There was a time, not too long ago, when a purse shaped like a baguette felt like a thrilling — even subversive — novelty. Now, we hardly bat an eye when we see that Moschino has made a trompe l’oeil celery purse, or that Big Bud Press is making a hot-dog-and-pretzel-print jumpsuit in collaboration with a Los Angeles bar. How did we get here? And has it all become a little too much? 

Fashion has long found inspiration from the culinary world, but the influx of food-fueled prints, graphics, and trompe l’oeil accessories that picked up steam during the pandemic have now reached a cultural tipping point. 

Today, there are truly countless examples of these food motifs and edible, wearable trompe l’oeil creations, at all price points. Loewe’s summer collection featured a $4,150 lambskin tomato clutch, while Walmart’s included a line of rotisserie chicken bag-like purses and charms starting at $5.97. As with many trends, this trajectory started in high fashion and worked its way down to mass-market ubiquity. In 2020, a chocolate chip cookie went down one of New York Fashion Week’s most buzzed-about fall runways. It protruded from a black purse by the designer Puppets and Puppets, a design that quickly went viral for its whimsical inclusion of a classic dessert on a high-end handbag. 

The then-emerging brand made food a central theme of its vision: Its cake-topped aviator caps, Ferrero Rocher heels, and beeswax-sculpted cheese hats — hyperrealistic, absurdist food designs capable of achieving instant social media virality. Prior to working in fashion, Puppets designer Carly Mark was working as an artist whose pieces revolved around candies and snacks. As she told Business of Fashion in 2023, the Puppets universe felt like the ideal pairing given what she considers food and fashion’s motivators: pleasure and money. 

View this post on Instagram

During the pandemic, the much-duped “strawberry dress” took over TikTok, and Tombolo Company found a hit on its hands with its vongole shirt, which debuted in 2019, but found peak popularity in 2021. The shirt appeared in Vogue in 2020, and InsideHook called it an “obvious bedfellow to day-drinks, rooftops and weekenders” — in other words, a status symbol. Katie Kimmel’s T-shirts made chicken-parm enthusiasm akin to being a devout sports fan, and in 2022, this very publication called “seafood kitsch” the look of the summer, as items like Rachel Antonoff’s super-popular seafood-tower dress flew off the digital shelves. 

Why was food so primed to become a motif in clothing at this point in culture? One could make the argument that it has roots in the millennial optimism years: Earlier in the 2000s and 2010s, the concept of “foodies” was new. Brands printed tongue-in-cheek food- and coffee-related motifs and slogans (a fervent love of avocados comes to mind) on hipster apparel, as the idea that “you are what you eat” developed growing fervor. That period was a key stepping stone in the evolution toward the far more elaborate and pervasive marketability of food-as-identity we see today. Content creator Cole Poska, who is a partner and creative director at Beyond Studios, tells Eater that “food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.” Poska’s own Instagram often reimagines restaurant merch just for fun; his designs for Cheesecake Factory apparel went viral, such as a hat that resembles the iconic brown bread. He points out that people will defend their favorite fast-food order like it’s a family member, and with the kind of loyalty that’s hard to manufacture. The same could be said about their martini order, or favorite taco spot; it’s all a way of telling others who we are through what we like.

View this post on Instagram

But the fatigue is undoubtedly here. In 2024, Bon Appétit asked “Why Does Every Dress Have Pasta and Cocktail Shrimp on It?” And as the novelty factor of a shrimp-print dress has worn off, the next question might be: Will there be any true longevity for these food-as-fashion moments? Our feeds overflow with products that are hurriedly designed for flash-in-the-pan viral trends and quickly destined for a landfill; arguably more so if the object in question prioritizes shock value and gimmicks over function and brand identity. 

This is undoubtedly all related to the accelerated speed of trend cycles and shortened consumer attention spans. However, as Leah Asmelash wrote last year for CNN, the rising costs of food also casts an increasingly sinister shadow over food-themed fashion. In 2026, a $425 cardigan emblazoned with oysters has waning originality compared to a few years ago; it falls into banality at best and the Hunger Games at worst; its wearer indicating that they’re financially comfortable and, more importantly, well-fed. In that light, beyond its role in identity, food has become more of a status symbol than ever. A sweater touting one’s love of caviar is shorthand for their access to and appreciation of luxury, culinary and otherwise. But sporting the mass-market Temu version? It’s more akin to wearing a faux Gucci belt.  

“Food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.”

Cole Poska

If it’s possible to escape the sense of accelerated-trend-cycle disposability, it may have to be through a more subtle approach than, say, a bag that looks like a giant stick of butter. Sam Salad is the co-founder of Meals, a Los Angeles-based, workwear-inspired apparel company that, by its own description, “bakes ‘food culture’ into apparel, blurring the lines between the clothes we eat and the food we wear.” Utility is at the forefront of Meals, which offers a classic chef pant with a slightly fuller cut, in both basic colors and subdued aesthetic homages to blue cheese and watermelon. When Meals launched in 2019, Salad was selling premium denim along Venice’s Abbot Kinney Boulevard, a hotbed of Instagrammy food culture and trendy restaurants like Gjelina that helped inspire his own brand. “Design today has to translate across languages and platforms,” Salad tells Eater. 

Food is hardly a new bedfellow for fashion designers, even if today’s hyper-fast trend cycle has kicked it into overdrive. “Food is totally universal,” Salad says. “Issey Miyake once merchandised their pleats collection as little omakase sets.”  He cites that collection among other fashion-meets-food moments that he considers successful, such as the iconic Salvador Dalí- and Elsa Schiaparelli-designed lobster dress. At the time of its 1937 creation, the dress was a scandal: It assumed a conformity-defying sartorial stance and was fittingly worn by Wallace Simpson. Today, its highly devolved spawn are available on Shein for $13. 

As it stands, one clear way out of corny, food motif purgatory is for brands — fashion or food — to focus less on quantity and more on storytelling and quality. “We do a few things really well, and that’s enough,” Salad says, “Like In-N-Out [Burger].” 

Poska’s philosophy is similar. “I’d rather make one thing people talk about for a year than 20 things people forget about tomorrow,” he says. “The first person to make a candle look like a stick of butter is a genius. The hundredth person is just making a candle.”

No, Mamdani, Socialism Does Not Increase Affordability

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Of the many inane comments that socialists have made this year, the one that democracy is “the building block of socialism” might be the most absurd.

Who made the comment? You guessed it: New York City’s boy genius, Mayor Zohran Mamdani.

In a recent episode of “This Past Weekend,” Mr. Mamdani discussed socialism’s ability to ensure affordability with comedian Theo Von. The interview was replete with Marxian nonsense, but Mr. Mamdani also discussed many Americans’ economic frustrations: homeownership feels out of reach; rent is expensive; childcare is unaffordable; and grocery prices are substantially higher.

Young people increasingly feel that the American dream is “slipping away.”

Recognizing affordability problems, however, is not the same as identifying the causes. Mr. Mamdani avers that the free market system cannot solve these problems and that socialism is the silver bullet.

Consider his assertion about democracy. When Mr. Von brought up the concept, Mr. Mamdani interrupted him and claimed, “That’s the building block of socialism.”

>>> Mamdani’s City Grocery Plan Could Leave Taxpayers Paying for Every Banana

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels would like to have a word.

To them, it was clear that implementing socialism—a word that they used interchangeably with communism—would require the opposite of democracy: tyranny.

In their 1848 “The Communist Manifesto,” the owner’s manual for Marxism, Marx and Engels rightly predicted that people would fight back when the government seized their property—something Mr. Mamdani should bear in mind as he upsets more and more New Yorkers with property-grabbing schemes.

“The proletariat will use its political supremacy to wrest, by degrees, all capital from the bourgeoisie, to centralize all instruments of production in the hands of the State,” Marx and Engels wrote. “Of course, in the beginning, this cannot be effected except by means of despotic inroads on the rights of property, and on the conditions of bourgeois production.”

So, no, Mr. Mamdani. Socialism’s pillars are totalitarian, based on forced nationalization and coercive state control.

Now, let us deal with the fanciful claim that socialism is the best way to order society: “Socialism to me means that you have whatever you need to live a dignified life, and it’s the government’s job to deliver on that.”

It sounds compassionate, but what happens when the government assumes responsibility for determining, financing and delivering the necessities of a “dignified life”?

Here, we begin to understand why Mr. Mamdani mixes socialism with democracy. To him, democracy is not just about electing leaders; it is also about letting people choose things that they cannot afford, courtesy of taxpayers.

In line with his big-government agenda, he explained socialism as “the extension of democracy from the ballot box to the rest of your life … your workplace, your home.”

Yet socialists devalue real democracy when they define it as the “right” for people to own what they have not earned. They also devalue the protection of inalienable rights when they insist that 51% of the electorate can suppress individual liberties such as property rights—something America’s Founders called “mobocracy.”

A free society empowers majorities, but it also protects individual rights. More government intervention will neither expand liberty nor make New York City more affordable.

The Big Apple is indeed the most expensive city in the United States and the third most expensive in the world. Mr. Mamdani said that is because “greed is baked into a lot of this economy.” He portrays capitalist profit incentives as a motive for firms to exploit workers, reduce the quality of goods and break the law.

Still, profit and wealth are not social defects to be discouraged; quite the contrary. In competitive markets, signals indicate that entrepreneurs have found ways to provide goods and services that consumers value. These incentives encourage businesses to innovate, produce more, reduce costs and discover better ways to meet people’s needs.

This must be a foreign concept to Mr. Mamdani, who believes that it is the government’s duty to provide the public with everything, from rent to groceries. He ignores commonsense solutions that have worked the world over, such as tax cuts and deregulation—and deportations, for that matter, which would reduce costs for households and businesses alike.

>>> Mamdani Is about to Make Housing Even More Expensive

Instead, Mr. Mamdani turns to central planning, even though government bureaucrats created many of the affordability problems he now insists they will solve.

Take housing. Mr. Mamdani argues that housing costs are central to New York’s economic problems, and many economists agree. Yet the city’s affordability crisis results from decades of restrictive zoning, burdensome permit requirements, development constraints, rent regulation and political barriers that limit housing construction.

The pattern is familiar: Government intervention distorts the market, consumers suffer the consequences, then socialists point to those consequences as evidence of market failure requiring still more state intervention.

Throughout his interview with Mr. Von, Mr. Mamdani framed his socialist ideology as empathetic, not the state-controlled subjugation that it is. Yet remember, the more “necessities” the government promises to provide, the more power bureaucrats require to deliver them.

When the government assumes control over housing, childcare, grocery access, transportation and healthcare, citizens become less self-reliant and more dependent on political institutions.

Americans are right to be concerned about affordability, but the challenge facing them today is how to improve markets with more freedom and opportunity, not socialism. 

XRP Whales Now Hold 68.5% of Supply in Highest Share Since 2022 — Is a Massive Rebound Quietly Brewing?

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

XRP may be struggling to regain its bullish momentum, but something intriguing is unfolding beneath the surface. While the Ripple-linked cryptocurrency languishes near $1.30, its largest holders have quietly tightened their grip on the token’s supply to levels not seen in nearly four years. 

With deep-pocketed players seemingly unfazed by XRP’s lackluster performance, could the cross-border payments token be laying the groundwork for a spectacular comeback?

XRP Whale Holdings Reach Highest Share of Supply Since November 2022

On-chain analytics platform Santiment has highlighted a notable shift in XRP’s supply distribution. 

According to its latest data, wallets holding at least 10 million XRP now account for approximately 46.87 billion tokens, equivalent to 68.5% of the circulating supply tracked by the firm. That is the highest concentration in whale hands since November 2022, suggesting that XRP’s biggest wallets are strengthening their position despite the token’s recent price weakness.

The rising concentration of XRP among major holders comes as the token continues to struggle on the price charts. 

XRP was trading at $0.32 at the time of writing, with a market capitalization of approximately $83 billion. The token remained 63.8% below its January 2025 all-time high of $3.65, highlighting the extent of its price decline. Notably, whale accumulation has continued throughout this downturn rather than emerging only after a recovery began, suggesting that major holders have been increasing their exposure despite persistent market weakness.

What does it mean for XRP?

For XRP, growing concentration among major holders could matter if it reflects sustained accumulation rather than routine exchange or custodial movements.

Santiment believes continued buying during a downturn may help support a recovery if selling pressure eases and fresh demand enters the market. However, the signal remains inconclusive, especially as XRP struggles to break decisively above $1.30. 

A sustained move toward $1.50, accompanied by further whale accumulation, would more strongly indicate returning bullish momentum. Until then, the latest on-chain data points to a potential recovery setup rather than confirmation of an impending parabolic rally.

What Is The Difference Between The Edge 4 Solar And Edge 4 Pro Solar?

When I see “Pro” added to a product, I assume it has features that put it in a class above the standard model. That’s the case with the Edge 4 Solar and Edge 4 Pro Solar. The Pro captures high-resolution, high-detailed 4K video and 50MP photos. I was highly impressed with the 1080p video and 40MP photos the Edge 4 Solar captured, and I’m excited to see the enhanced detail.

According to Moultrie, “detail” drove the Edge 4 Solar Pro. Moultrie’s continued growth largely comes from its ability to meet consumer expectations. Consumers asked for more detail, and the Edge 4 Pro Solar is Moultrie’s response.

The Edge 4 Solar Pro trumps the Edge 4 Solar’s trigger speed by .1 second. That’s not much, but when a buck is hot on the heels of a doe, rapid trigger speed is important.

The internal interface makes setup quick and headache-free. A series of lights lets you know when the camera is ready to transmit images and videos. (Photo/Jace Bauserman)

Two Big Features Hunters Will Appreciate

Moultrie did an excellent job with its Edge 4 Series, and the Edge 4 Solar Pro looks to be the pinnacle. I’m a fan of Buck Shot. The Edge 4 Pro Solar uses Moultrie AI to detect big bucks. When the camera detects a big buck, it sends a tightly cropped, max-resolution image to your smartphone. This lets you immediately identify the buck and examine its headgear. And with the Pro model, Moultrie AI recognizes shooter bucks and switches the camera from photo mode to 4K video. Buck videos arrive immediately. Like the zoomed and cropped image, you don’t have to request or wait for the 4K video.

Setup And Deployment

The Edge 4 Pro Solar comes with everything you need. The solar panel is connected, and the rechargeable lithium battery pack is inserted. Log into your Moultrie account, add a new camera, scan the QR code, and start tinkering with the camera’s many settings. As I noted in my review of the Edge 4 Solar, I like the new internal panel. The twist-and-turn dial ensures a positive on/off, and Moultrie left the battery-life indicator, which I appreciate. Once powered on, all three lights (Not Connected-red, Connecting-yellow, Connected-green) turn solid. Next, the Connecting light flashes slowly, and after about 45 seconds, the green Connected light turns solid green. At that point, you can close the push-button door. The camera connects to the strongest cellular signal in the area and starts transmitting photos immediately. Best of all, perhaps, is that you don’t need to insert an SD card. I hate SD cards. They are a pain. I love Moultrie’s included storage.

In a partnership with onX, Edge 4 cameras are instantly pinned the second they are placed. I have multiple trail cameras—cellular and conventional—spread across the West and Midwest. Too often, I get in a hurry and forget to drop a camera pin when I’m using trail cameras. However, if I’m using Moultrie, I can forget, and the map-pinning technology saves me.

Built Tough

The Edge 4 Pro Solar has a quick trigger and captures game—even at night—at long distances.

My Edge 4 Solar withstood and continues to withstand rough conditions. We’ve had an extended monsoon season in my neck of the woods. Rain is one thing, but hail is another. All of my Edge 4 Solar cameras, including the new Pro, have zero damage to the housing or panels. Sadly, the Edge 4 Pro Solar did succumb to golf-ball-sized hail. The camera was facing straight into the storm. I’m not sure how many hailstones struck the photo lens cover, but one cracked it and some water got inside. The good news is that the camera is still functioning. The photos and video aren’t as clean and crisp as they were before the hail damage, but they are still solid. I called Moultrie and they are going to replace the camera. Moultrie has excellent customer service.

Moultrie Edge 4 cameras utilize waterproof IP67 waterproofing, and the housing and solar panel can take serious abuse. I was a little concerned that water would get into the removable solar-panel slot but that hasn’t happened and would have by now. If you want to remove the solar panel, push the unlock button on the back of the camera and pull the panel off.

Moultrie Edge 4 Solar Pro in the Field

Use the solar panel adjustments to ensure your camera gets as much sunlight as possible. (Photo/Jace Bauserman)

As of this writing, my Edge 4 Pro Solar has been in the field for two weeks. The hail damage occurred 11 days into my field test. Until that point, the camera’s images and video were excellent. The detail and color, especially the video, was incredible. I couldn’t tell much difference between the 40MP and 50MP photos, but I could absolutely see the difference between the 1080p and 4K video.

After two weeks in the field, battery life is 94 percent. That percentage dips a bit when the panel isn’t getting sunlight, but as soon as the panel gets sun, it charges the camera quickly. I like the direct-to-housing panel attachment. The system eliminates wires that tangle and hang up everywhere.  Be sure to take advantage of the twist-and-lock solar panel direction and angle. Both are adjustable, allowing you to set your panel to absorb as much sunlight as possible.

I’ve had zero image delivery glitches; deer and other game animals are centered in the frame, and Moultrie lowballs the camera’s max detection range. Like the Edge 4 Pro, the Edge 4 Solar Pro captured deer beyond 100 feet. Moultrie brands the Edge Pro Solar trigger speed at .3 seconds, and my testing agrees. I set up an Edge 4 Solar and Edge 4 Pro Solar in my backyard, and the Pro proved quicker to trigger when I ran past the camera at 50 feet.

Like the Edge 4 Solar, the Pro model improves Moultrie’s nighttime image capture game. Moultrie used enhanced No Glow IR LEDs to illuminate every image evenly. The result is clearer edge-to-edge images. The only issue I’ve noticed the more I’ve tested Moultrie’s Edge 4 Solar models is the flash. The IR is almost too powerful. If the subject is close to the camera, the image gets a bit blown out. However, I’ve yet to have a deer spook from the Edge 4 Pro Solar, and I’ve run the camera on open ag fields and tight trail crossings.

The Edge 4 Solar, awarded Gold in our annual Best Cellular Trail Camera test, has consistently impressed with its performance, durability, and affordability. At $169.99, it offers outstanding value, especially when paired with the aftermarket Power Mag II battery pack. The solar panel and robust battery life combine to deliver a seamless experience in demanding conditions, from heavy rainfall to extreme heat.
