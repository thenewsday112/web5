This Is How Fast You Should Walk to Help Lower Your Risk of Death

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
People who walk faster may have better underlying health. Lol2368/E+/Getty Images
  • A study found that walking speed can help predict people’s risk of dying from any cause.
  • Both a clinical test and a test done in daily life were able to predict mortality risk.
  • Experts say walking speed predicts death risk because it’s an indicator of overall health.
  • If your walking speed slows, it’s a good idea to talk with your healthcare professional.

New research shows that how fast you walk, whether measured in a clinical test or through daily activity, can help predict your risk of dying from any cause.

The study compared two ways of measuring walking: a short walking speed test conducted in a clinic and the step rate people take in their everyday lives, measured with a wrist-worn device.

Both measures were linked to mortality risk and showed similar ability to predict it.

The findings, published on October 23 in JAMA Network Open, could help doctors and researchers choose the best method for assessing health risks in different settings.

Clinical walking speed vs. everyday walking

The study was part of the Whitehall II accelerometer substudy in the United Kingdom.

It included 4,067 participants, mostly males, with an average age of about 69 years. About one-third of the subjects were female.

The researchers collected data from January 2012 to December 2013 and followed up through August 2024.

To measure clinical walking speed, participants completed a standard walking test. They walked a marked 2.44-meter course at their usual pace. The test was repeated three times, and the average speed in meters per second was used for analysis. Participants who normally used a cane were allowed to use it during the test.

For free-living walking measurements, participants wore a wrist-worn GENEActiv accelerometer for 9 consecutive days. This device recorded movement at a high frequency and used special software to detect walking episodes and count steps.

From this data, two key measures of walking cadence were calculated: the median walking cadence (the middle value of steps per minute during walking periods) and the 95th-percentile walking cadence (a higher step rate indicating brisk walking). The study also calculated the average number of daily steps.

The researchers additionally gathered information on factors known to affect mortality, such as:

  • age
  • sex
  • education
  • race and ethnicity
  • marital status
  • smoking
  • alcohol use
  • diet
  • body mass index (BMI)
  • hypertension
  • chronic diseases

National death records were used to identify deaths during the follow-up period.

Statistical methods were then used to estimate the risk of death associated with walking speed and cadence while accounting for other risk factors.

The models were adjusted step-by-step: starting with no adjustments, then adding sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, and finally health-related factors.

The ability of walking measures to predict mortality was assessed using a statistical measure.

The study also examined differences between males and females.

The researchers additionally conducted analyses to test how well the results held up when they excluded early deaths, looked at subgroups by age and health status, and used different definitions of walking cadence.

Faster walking speed linked to 23% lower risk of death

During a median follow-up of nearly 12 years, 739 participants died.

The average clinical walking speed of participants was 1.2 meters per second. The median walking cadence during daily life was about 75 steps per minute, and the 95th percentile walking cadence was about 112 steps per minute.

The study found only a weak correlation between clinical walking speed and free-living walking cadence, meaning the two measures do not strongly reflect the same walking behavior. However, both measures were independently associated with a lower risk of death.

After adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle, and health factors, for every standard increase in clinical walking speed, the risk of death was 23% lower.

Similarly, higher median and 95th-percentile walking cadences were associated with 17% and 18% lower risks of death, respectively.

When examining how well these walking measures predicted mortality, each on its own showed modest predictive ability.

Adding clinical walking speed to a model with other known risk factors slightly improved mortality prediction, while adding free-living walking cadence also improved prediction, albeit to a similar degree to that of clinical walking speed.

Combining clinical walking speed with free-living walking parameters provided a small boost in predictive ability, but the effect was not significant after accounting for detailed health factors.

The study also found that walking speed and cadence had similar predictive power for both men and women, although the overall prediction models performed slightly better for women.

Several sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. Results were consistent when excluding early deaths, focusing on healthier or older participants, and using different ways to define walking cadence.

Overall, this study suggests that clinical walking speed tests and free-living walking cadence measurements from wrist-worn devices are both useful in predicting all-cause mortality risk. They provide similar information but reflect different aspects of walking behavior.

The choice between methods may depend on the purpose of the assessment, available resources, and the context in which the measurement is taken.

Faster walkers may live longer because they are healthier

Neha Sabharwal, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and director of rehabilitation specializing in geriatric mobility and functional decline at Vintage Faire Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Modesto, CA, said that walking speed helps predict longevity because it’s an indicator of someone’s overall health.

“Walking is something most of us do without thinking about it, but it requires several body systems to work together,” she told Healthline. “Your heart and lungs have to keep up, your muscles need to produce enough force, and your brain has to coordinate each step while keeping you balanced.”

Sabharwal, who wasn’t involved in the study, said that in her work, she pays attention to when someone starts walking more slowly than before.

“It is easy to dismiss that change as part of getting older, but there may be something else going on,” she said. This could include muscle weakness, pain, reduced endurance, balance problems, or underlying medical conditions.

According to Sabharwal, the current study is valuable because it suggests that useful information about people’s movement can be obtained outside a clinical setting.

However, she cautioned against misinterpreting the findings to mean that simply walking faster will make you live longer.

“People who naturally walk faster may already have better underlying health,” said Sabharwal.

“I would be less concerned about whether someone walks faster than their neighbor and more interested in whether they are walking slower than they did six months ago,” she said.

What to do if your walking speed slows

Theodore Strange, MD, chairman of medicine at Northwell Staten Island University Hospital and senior vice president of the Metro Market Region at Northwell Health, shared some insights for those who may notice their walking speed has declined. Strange wasn’t involved in the study.

“I mean, if it’s slowing down because a week or two ago, you were walking up a flight of steps with no problem, and now, you’re having some shortness of breath, I think it should be evaluated,” he told Healthline.

Strange added that if you’re experiencing aches or pains that weren’t there before, you should consult a healthcare professional, as they can make exercise more difficult.

It’s also essential to manage any chronic diseases that you have so that you can exercise as well as possible within the constraints of your condition, he said.

“And I think slow and steady wins the race here,” said Strange. He suggests monitoring your walking with a device like a pedometer and progressively increasing your steps.

“Everybody talks about 10,000 steps a day. I think the most recent studies show probably 7,000 to 8,000 is adequate,” he said. You can start where you are and work your way up gradually.

Strange further noted that physical therapy can sometimes be helpful if you have issues with gait mechanics, balance, or strength. Additionally, you might benefit from exercise that is tailored to your condition, for example, if you’ve had a stroke.

Finally, Strange advised stretching before and after exercise to keep your muscles moving.

“I go back to adequate nutrition and hydration being important,” he said. You want to make sure you are adequately supporting your body’s ability to exercise, said Strange.

The Connector Was Never the Work: Five Questions I Ask Before Estimating an Integration

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every integration ticket I have been handed started with the same sentence: we just need this system to talk to that one. It reads like a connector. It gets estimated like a connector. Then it turns into a project, because the connection was never the work.

The HTTP call is the easy part. What eats the weeks is unspecified behavior: what happens when both sides edit the same record, when the receiving system rejects a row for a validation rule nobody mentioned, and when a human edits by hand the field your automation is also writing. Those are business decisions wearing technical clothes.

This is the checklist I run before estimating one, adapted from a guide I wrote for my studio's clients.

First, name the shape

There are three shapes, and they cost very different amounts.

A one-way feed. Data goes from A to B and never comes back. Orders into accounting, leads into a CRM, events into a warehouse. Cheapest shape, because there is nothing to reconcile.

A two-way sync. Both systems can change the same record and both changes must survive. This is where the money goes. Every field needs an owner, every conflict needs a rule, and you have to stop the two systems bouncing the same update back and forth forever. Most "sync" requests turn out to be a one-way feed plus two or three fields that genuinely go both ways. Finding that out early is worth weeks.

A process that spans systems. A new order triggers a credit check, a stock reservation, a notification and an invoice across four tools, with conditions at each step. That is not a connection. It is a small application whose interface happens to be other people's software, and it should be scoped like one.

Most of my voice agent work is the third kind. The agent on the phone is the visible part; the value is where the outcome lands.

The five questions that size it

1. What does each end actually give you?

Two documented APIs with a sandbox is the good case. In practice you meet an API that covers the reads but not the writes, a rate limit that quietly caps how fast you can backfill history, or no API at all.

With no interface, the options are a file exchange, a read replica, the vendor's export, or, at the very bottom, scripting the UI, which is fragile and should be called fragile out loud. I confirm sandbox access and rate limits before agreeing to any date, because both routinely change the plan.

2. Who owns which field?

Source of truth is per field, not per system. The CRM owns the phone number. The billing platform owns the payment terms. Where two systems both let a human edit the same field, you either pick a winner or write a rule. Leaving it unstated is how you get a sync that flaps between two values while each team insists its system is right.

This shows up in code more literally than people expect. In VoiceDash, the Retell webhook upserts a call into a Conversation row, but the update block deliberately leaves out tags, notes, summaries and evaluations. Those fields belong to the analysis step and to the humans using the portal, and a late webhook must never overwrite them. I wrote up the full version of that in Undefined Is Not Null. The field map was not a document. It was which keys I allowed into an update: {} object.

3. How fresh does the data have to be?

Nightly batch and event-driven within seconds are different builds, not a config flag. Real time brings webhooks, queues, ordering questions and a replay path for events you missed while you were down. So ask what actually breaks if the data is an hour old. Often nothing.

Set freshness per flow, not per project. And if you do go event-driven, remember that a webhook tells you something happened, not that you have everything. You will want a backfill path anyway.

4. How is the same thing recognized on both sides?

Two systems almost never share an identifier. The same customer is a record in one and a contact in the other, matched on an email that has since changed or a phone number stored in three formats. You need an explicit matching rule, and you need a place for the records that do not match, with a person who checks it.

Data that does not reconcile is worse than missing data, because people keep trusting it. The accounting software I build at Null Studio lives and dies on this; more in A Stored Balance Is a Rumor.

5. What happens when a transfer fails?

Not if. A destination will be down, a record will be rejected, a credential will expire. The difference between an integration that survives and one that quietly stops is the failure path:

  • retries with sensible backoff
  • idempotency, so a retry does not create a second invoice
  • a queue that holds what did not go through
  • an alert that reaches a named human
  • a reconciliation report somebody actually reads

The cheapest idempotency I know is a unique constraint on the other system's ID. VoiceDash keys conversations on @@unique([agentId, platformCallId]) and upserts against it, so a webhook delivered twice lands on the same row, and the backfill job that re-reads calls from Retell looks rows up by the same key instead of inserting:

await prisma.conversation.upsert({ where: { agentId_platformCallId: { agentId: agent.id, platformCallId: call.call_id }, }, create: { /* full row */ }, update: { /* only the fields this source owns */ }, });

It also writes a raw WebhookLog row before parsing anything, so when a payload surprises me I can see what actually arrived instead of guessing.

That failure path is roughly half the engineering, and it never appears in the estimate. Someone also has to watch the exceptions after launch; monitoring voice agents in production is the same problem with phone calls attached.

When no-code is the right answer

I build custom integrations for a living and still recommend no-code tools often. If volume is modest, the mapping is simple, and a missed record costs an apology rather than money, use one.

The signs you have outgrown it:

  • Volume. Per-task pricing that was trivial at a hundred records a day is a line item at fifty thousand.
  • The logic became a program. Branches, loops and lookups in a visual editor are still software, just without tests, version control or review.
  • Error handling beyond a red icon. Retries, ordering, partial failure and reconciliation are engineering.
  • The data cannot go through a third party. Regulated or contractual data often settles it on its own.
  • Nobody can safely change it. The person who built it left, and now it runs a process nobody wants to touch.

The sensible middle: no-code at the edges for low-stakes notifications, a properly built spine.

What AI changes, and what it does not

The build side compresses a lot. Field mapping, transformation code, adapters, retry handling and the pile of tests an integration needs are the mechanical work coding agents do well under review.

Some integrations get replaced by an agent that just does the task. Careful with that framing: an agent that reads your CRM, checks availability and books an appointment is an integration project wearing new clothes. All five questions still apply, and two apply harder because nobody is clicking the button: idempotency, so a retried booking does not become two, and permissions, so it can only touch what it should. I argued the second one in Give an AI Agent Write Access One Verb at a Time.

What AI does not compress is deciding who owns which field and how fresh it must be. That needs the people who run the operation, and they are usually the critical path.

How I scope one now

  1. Read both ends' docs before estimating.
  2. Write the field map. Every field that moves, which direction, how often, and which side wins a conflict. That document is the project.
  3. Set freshness per flow.
  4. Design the failure path first. Retries, idempotency, a reject queue, one named person on the alert.
  5. Build the thinnest end-to-end slice with real data. One record type, all the way through, in production conditions.
  6. Run it next to the manual process and reconcile before anyone switches the manual step off. The diff finds the rules nobody wrote down, which is the same lesson as rewrites failing in month seven.

An integration is not a wire between two boxes. It is an agreement about who owns what, how fast it travels and what happens when it does not arrive. Make those decisions on purpose and you get a boring system that runs for years. Skip straight to the connector and you get one that works in the demo and needs someone watching it forever.

How to Audit AI Crawler Traffic and Identify Spoofed Bots in Logs

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Unmanaged web scraping by artificial intelligence agents can consume server bandwidth, increase egress costs, and skew operational analytics. While legitimate crawlers identify themselves via their request headers, malicious scrapers often spoof those same headers to bypass rules or scrape protected assets under the guise of well-known automated systems.

Analyzing access logs manually across multiple servers is inefficient. You need a systematic way to parse server logs, match known crawler signatures, estimate hosting expenses tied to crawler bandwidth, and verify whether the IP addresses sending those headers actually belong to the claimed crawler networks.

The script aibot_ledger_crawler_log_reporter.py addresses this issue directly. It inspects web server access logs, identifies automated AI crawlers, performs Forward-Confirmed Reverse DNS lookups to catch spoofed clients, checks paths against your robots exclusion file, and generates server-level mitigation templates.

Install & Usage

The script relies entirely on standard modules included with Python, such as urllib.robotparser, socket, gzip, and json. You do not need to install any third-party libraries using pip.

To get started, save the source code into a file named aibot_ledger_crawler_log_reporter.py.

You can view the command-line help interface by running:

python aibot_ledger_crawler_log_reporter.py --help

Because the core processing logic is encapsulated in the AIBotLedger class, you can also import and execute the ledger directly within your own administrative scripts:

from aibot_ledger_crawler_log_reporter import AIBotLedger # Initialize the ledger with custom bandwidth pricing and DNS verification ledger = AIBotLedger(cost_per_gb=0.09, verify_dns=True) # Ingest standard or compressed log files ledger.ingest_file("/var/log/nginx/access.log") ledger.ingest_file("/var/log/nginx/access.log.1.gz") # Print the formatted terminal summary print(ledger.generate_terminal_summary())

How It Works

The tool is organized around log parsing, pattern matching, DNS validation, and rules checking.

1. Dual Log Parsing and Compression Handling

The ingest_file function checks the file extension. If the target file ends in .gz, it transparently reads the stream via Python's built-in gzip module.

Log entries are processed by parse_log_line, which detects both standard Combined log entries (typical for Nginx and Apache) and structured JSON log lines (typical for Caddy):

def parse_log_line(line): """Parses standard Combined format or Caddy JSON logs.""" line = line.strip() if not line: return None # Try Caddy JSON log format if line.startswith("{") and line.endswith("}"): try: data = json.loads(line) req = data.get("request", {}) headers = req.get("headers", {}) ua_list = headers.get("User-Agent", [""]) ua = ua_list[0] if isinstance(ua_list, list) and ua_list else str(ua_list) ip = req.get("remote_ip") or req.get("client_ip") or "" if ":" in ip and not ip.startswith("["): # Handle host:port ip = ip.rsplit(":", 1)[0] return { "ip": ip, "time": data.get("ts", ""), "method": req.get("method", "GET"), "path": req.get("uri", "/"), "status": int(data.get("status", 200)), "bytes": int(data.get("size", 0) or 0), "ua": ua, } except Exception: pass # Try Combined / Nginx / Apache format m = COMBINED_LOG_REGEX.match(line) if m: d = m.groupdict() b = d["bytes"] bytes_val = int(b) if b.isdigit() else 0 return { "ip": d["ip"], "time": d["time"], "method": d["method"], "path": d["path"], "status": int(d["status"]), "bytes": bytes_val, "ua": d["ua"], } return None

2. Signature Detection

The script maintains an internal collection of bot definitions named BOT_PROFILES. It matches user-agent values against regular expressions corresponding to entities such as GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Bytespider, CCBot, Google-Extended, PerplexityBot, Cohere-AI, and Diffbot. Each entry maps the signature to a classification category (such as Live Browsing, Dataset Mining, or Aggressive Scraper) and assigns legitimate root domains used for verification.

3. Spoof Detection with Forward-Confirmed Reverse DNS (FCrDNS)

Anyone can configure an HTTP client to send User-Agent: GPTBot. To prove authenticity, the script uses Forward-Confirmed Reverse DNS via verify_fcrdns:

def verify_fcrdns(ip, expected_domains, timeout=2.0): """Forward-Confirmed Reverse DNS (FCrDNS) check. Returns: 'VERIFIED', 'SPOOFED', 'UNVERIFIED', or 'SKIPPED'. """ if not expected_domains: return "SKIPPED" if ip in _DNS_CACHE: return _DNS_CACHE[ip] # Don't resolve private or special addresses if ip.startswith(("127.", "10.", "192.168.", "172.16.", "172.17.", "172.18.", "172.19.", "172.20.", "172.21.", "172.22.", "172.23.", "172.24.", "172.25.", "172.26.", "172.27.", "172.28.", "172.29.", "172.30.", "172.31.")): result = "SKIPPED" _DNS_CACHE[ip] = result return result orig_timeout = socket.getdefaulttimeout() try: socket.setdefaulttimeout(timeout) try: hostname, _, _ = socket.gethostbyaddr(ip) except (socket.herror, socket.gaierror, socket.timeout, OSError): result = "SPOOFED" _DNS_CACHE[ip] = result return result hostname_lower = hostname.lower() domain_matched = any(hostname_lower.endswith(domain.lower()) for domain in expected_domains) if not domain_matched: result = "SPOOFED" _DNS_CACHE[ip] = result return result try: forward_ips = socket.gethostbyname_ex(hostname)[2] if ip in forward_ips: result = "VERIFIED" else: result = "SPOOFED" except (socket.gaierror, socket.timeout, OSError): result = "SPOOFED" finally: socket.setdefaulttimeout(orig_timeout) _DNS_CACHE[ip] = result return result

This verification process consists of three distinct checks:

  1. It queries the reverse DNS record for the IP address to obtain a hostname.
  2. It verifies that the returned hostname ends with one of the legitimate domain suffixes configured for that bot.
  3. It performs a forward lookup on that hostname to ensure that the originating IP address is present in the resolved address list.

If any check fails, the record is flagged as SPOOFED and recorded for auditing.

4. Cost Calculations and Defense Export

By accumulating byte counts associated with verified and unverified crawlers, AIBotLedger multiplies total gigabytes transferred by the cost_per_gb parameter to provide an estimate of hosting bandwidth charges.

It also includes standalone helper functions—such as generate_nginx_snippets and generate_robots_snippet—to output firewall maps, rate-limiting directives, and exclusion lines targeted at the specific crawler names encountered in your logs.

Limitations

While practical for audits, the current code has several built-in constraints:

  • Synchronous DNS Resolution: The verify_fcrdns function runs synchronously using Python's standard socket module. Although lookups are saved to an in-memory dictionary cache (_DNS_CACHE), processing very large uncompressed logs containing thousands of unique IP addresses will experience delays due to network timeouts.
  • Rigid Log Formats: The parser handles standard Apache/Nginx Combined log patterns and Caddy JSON logs. If your server outputs custom access log layouts missing status codes, request sizes, or user-agent fields, lines will fail to match and will be silently ignored.
  • Hardcoded Profile List: The tool identifies only the twelve specific agents defined inside BOT_PROFILES. Unlisted crawlers, custom research bots, or newer AI agents will not be cataloged unless you manually add new entries to the profile list.
  • Robots Rule Interpretation: Compliance tracking relies on urllib.robotparser. It does not interpret non-standard directives like Crawl-delay or vendor-specific path expansions that are outside standard robot exclusions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does running this script require elevated root privileges?

No. The script only reads log files from disk and performs standard outbound DNS resolution queries. As long as your operating system user has read permissions for the target log files, root or administrative access is not required.

Can the script process logs stored in .gz archives alongside plain text logs?

Yes. The ingest_file method inspects the file name. If it detects a .gz suffix, it uncompresses and reads the file stream via Python's gzip module without requiring prior extraction on disk.

Why does an IP show up as spoofed even if the user agent looks valid?

A request is marked as spoofed when the client supplies a known AI crawler user agent, but its IP address either has no reverse DNS entry, resolves to a domain outside the provider's authorized domain list, or fails forward verification when matching the hostname back to the originating IP address.

My November 2026 shortlist, with the examples, traps and follow ups that make each question worth your time.

You can recognize an interview question, write a clean solution, and still solve a different problem from the one you were given.

Let tasks run in any order and a scheduling problem becomes a counting exercise. Keep their original order and that same shortcut can give the wrong answer. Both versions look reassuringly familiar until one little sentence changes the whole job.

That is what I want my November practice to catch. I’m choosing ten questions that make us read carefully, explain a decision and handle the follow-up without starting from zero. Your evenings are too valuable for another enormous bookmark folder.

I’ve added small practice examples and review prompts, and called out where the original report leaves details open.

Start with the section closest to your next round. Questions 1–3 cover algorithms, 4–7 cover practical implementation, and 8–10 cover system design and database reasoning. Each gives you something specific to explain when you finish.

1. Waymo: can you schedule the tasks in a different order?

Coding practice · Source page dated October 30

Run unit length tasks on one worker while leaving a cooldown between two tasks of the same type. You may reorder the tasks, and you want the earliest possible finish.

Try AAABBB with cooldown 2. One valid schedule is A B idle A B idle A B, which takes eight time units. Cooldown starts when a task finishes, so consecutive starts of the same type must be at least three units apart.

For n tasks, count the most frequent type. If its frequency is f, and m types share that frequency, the answer for nonempty input is max(n, (f − 1) × (cooldown + 1) + m). Empty input returns zero.

Before trusting the formula, explain the gaps forced by the busiest types and why enough other tasks can fill them. Counting the fixed alphabet takes O(n) time and constant extra space.

Then change one rule. Preserve the original order of AAABBB. It now takes 14 units. Track the next allowed start for each type and simulate; the eight-unit schedule is no longer available.

Try the full scheduling question yourself.

2. Lyft: can your smallest window handle repeated letters?

Coding practice · Source page dated October 28

Find the shortest substring of s containing every character required by t, including repeated copies. Matching is case-sensitive, and ties go to the earliest window. Return an empty string when no window qualifies.

For s = BAXAB and t = AAB, the answer is BAXA. Both four-character windows qualify, but the earlier one wins. Returning AB would quietly lose an A.

Grow a window until its character counts meet the requirements, then shrink it while it remains valid. Keep the best start and length, taking the final substring once. Moving both pointers forward gives O(|s| + |t|) time; the fixed letter alphabet bounds counting space.

Your check is the moment a count falls below its requirement. Explain why removing one surplus A can be harmless while removing the next A invalidates the window. Update the answer only for a strictly shorter window to preserve the earlier tie.

For a useful follow-up, ask whether the same constant-space claim survives arbitrary Unicode input. Your character model suddenly matters.

Work through the complete substring problem.

3. Google: can you reuse the answer when the tree root moves?

Coding stretch · Source page dated October 29

Given a tree with positive edge weights, return the sum of distances from every node to every other node. Running a traversal from each starting node works, but takes O(n²) time.

Use the path 0 —2— 1 —3— 2. The distance sums are [7, 5, 8]. We can check all three by hand before reaching for a larger example.

Root the tree once, compute subtree sizes and the root’s distance sum, then move the answer across each edge. For an edge of weight w from parent u to child v, let s be the child’s subtree size.

answer[v] = answer[u] + w × (n − 2s)

Those s nodes get w closer; the other n − s nodes get w farther away. Two traversals give O(n) time and O(n) space. Use a sufficiently wide integer type and avoid recursion-depth trouble on a long chain.

Finish by explaining the formula without looking at it. Then try a star-shaped tree. The practice payoff is understanding what changes across one edge, so you can derive the expression again.

Try the weighted-tree question from scratch.

4. Perplexity: can you build a file system without being too helpful?

Practical coding · Source page dated October 29

Build an in-memory file system with directory creation, listing, file appends and file reads. The important restriction is that mkdir creates only the final directory. Missing parents stay missing.

After mkdir(/notes), calling mkdir(/notes/november/day1) must fail because /notes/november does not exist. Automatically creating it feels helpful, but violates this version’s contract.

Agree on the error behavior and what ls returns for a file before writing methods. The report leaves those details open. For practice, choose explicit exceptions for invalid paths and a one-element filename list when listing a file.

Represent files and directories distinctly, and share one path resolver across operations. Resolve and validate the parent before changing state. Directory listing costs O(k log k) for sorting k names, in addition to walking the path.

Check the state after a failed operation. That unsuccessful call must leave no half-created directories behind. Then add recursive directory creation as a deliberate new feature and decide whether its failures should also be atomic.

5. Palantir: can you learn the code before trying to improve it?

Code comprehension · Source page dated October 30

Read an unfamiliar SQL-like query class, explain how it runs, then solve tasks using its mapping and accumulation operations. The original class was not included in the report, so any replacement implementation is a practice stand-in.

Use a small query API with lazy where and select steps, plus a terminal fold action. Trace one row through it before touching the implementation.

Suppose completed orders have amounts 12 and 8, while a cancelled order has amount 100. Filtering completed rows before summing must return 20. If you first project only amount and then filter by status, that later operation no longer has the field it needs.

Explain when work actually happens. Building a lazy query records steps; executing an action consumes rows. For a fold, say what the accumulator represents after every row and why the empty result has a sensible initial value.

Then inspect whether running the same query twice reuses a list or consumes an exhausted iterator. This is a lovely way to practice learning unfamiliar code without turning the session into a rewrite of somebody else’s library.

6. Anthropic: can your crawler stop at the right time?

Concurrency practice · Source page dated October 26

Starting from one URL, discover reachable pages on exactly the same hostname. Remove fragments before deduplicating, handle cycles, and fetch each eligible page at most once. Build the single-threaded version before adding workers.

Two workers can discover the same link simultaneously. Checking the visited set and claiming that URL must happen atomically, keeping only the individual set operations safe does not protect the whole decision.

Keep network calls outside the shared state lock. Then account for outstanding work, because an empty queue can coexist with a running fetch that will discover five more pages. Completion needs both no queued work and no active work, observed through coordinated state.

Make your check deterministic. Use a fake parser that pauses one worker while the queue becomes empty, then releases another link. The crawler must wait and discover it. Afterwards, inject a fetch error and explain how work accounting still reaches completion. A hanging fetch additionally needs a timeout or cancellation policy.

Practice the crawler and its concurrency follow-up.

7. MongoDB: can a waiting writer ever get its turn?

Concurrency stretch · Source page dated October 26

Implement a read-write lock using a mutex and condition variables. Several readers may enter together, but a writer must be alone. For the first version, exclude reentrancy, upgrades and timed acquisition.

Track active readers, an active-writer flag and waiting writers under one mutex. Condition waits belong inside loops that recheck the admission rule, as Python’s condition-variable documentation explains.

Now imagine one reader enters, a writer starts waiting, and new readers keep arriving before the current readers finish. Exclusion can remain correct while that writer waits forever.

Block new readers once a writer is waiting, so existing readers can drain. This addresses reader-induced writer starvation, but does not by itself guarantee FIFO service among writers. Specify fair queueing if every waiter needs eventual admission, and assume holders eventually release.

Draw the arrival sequence before writing more code. Show R1 entering, W1 waiting, R2 waiting, R1 leaving, then W1 entering. Follow up by asking whether sustained writer traffic can now starve readers. Fairness needs its own argument.

Try the complete read-write lock exercise.

8. Instacart: does your inventory system actually own the inventory?

System design · Source page dated October 30

Design an inventory system for grocery delivery. The original report names the topic without a detailed specification, so the ownership and freshness assumptions below are our practice framing.

Start by separating a warehouse you control from a partner supermarket whose shelves you only observe. Reserving the last unit makes sense in the first case. A reservation in your database cannot prevent an in-store shopper from taking the same item in the second.

For partner stores, track product and store identity alongside each stock signal’s source, observation time and arrival time. Keep enough history to resolve stale or conflicting reports according to an explicit policy.

9. eBay: can you make your own system asynchronous?

System design and project discussion · Source page dated October 28

Explain a system you have genuinely worked on, then change one synchronous request into asynchronous work. This is where your project experience becomes part of the technical problem.

Choose a slow operation whose result the caller can wait for. An export is a useful practice example. The initial response can return an accepted job ID, while a separate status endpoint eventually returns success, failure or a download location.

Trace acceptance, durable job creation, dispatch, worker execution and result retrieval. Each arrow needs an answer when the process stops halfway through.

Pause at the gap between saving the job and sending its message. If the process crashes there, how does the job become discoverable again? A transactional outbox is one option, a durable job table that workers poll is another. Pick one and explain it.

Then deliver the same work twice. Show how repeated execution avoids duplicating its side effects, and how permanent failures reach the caller. Async changes the interaction and absorbs bursts, it does not erase work, guarantee faster completion or make an overloaded dependency healthy.

10. MongoDB, can you explain what happens after SQL leaves your editor?

Database discussion · Source page dated October 26

Walk through a query’s lifecycle inside a disk based database, then explain the buffer pool and optimizer. A relational engine is a useful practice model, but settle the engine and scope first.

Follow parsing and name resolution into planning, execution, page access and returned rows. Explain how cached pages avoid some reads, why a page in active use cannot simply be evicted, and what changes when a page is dirty.

Why Does Open Innovation Matter?

Ollama + open weights mean the entire haunting runs on your laptop, your abandoned code never touches a third-party API. I Don’t Trust an AI Fix Until I Can Make the Bug Come Back

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A missing ticket, a misleading green test, and a repeatable way to check what your coding agent actually repaired.

Imagine asking Claude to fix a bug, getting a tidy patch, and watching the tests turn green. You are already thinking about the next task when somebody asks which test failed before the fix.

You scroll back through the conversation, and suddenly the reassuring summary is doing a lot of work.

Maybe the fix is correct.
Maybe the test never exercised the broken behavior.
Maybe both the implementation and its new test agree on the same wrong assumption.

Passing result cannot tell those stories apart by itself.

I prefer a slightly awkward question before getting comfortable with an AI generated repair. If we put the original mistake back into a disposable local copy, does the same test complain again?

That question gives us something more useful than another request for the model to double-check its answer. It gives us a small experiment we can understand.

In its account of code review, the Claude Code team describes giving agents runnable checks and asking for their output before human review. My favorite place to start is smaller than a whole review system, with one bug and a test whose expected answer comes from the requirement.

Let’s lose one support ticket without raising an error

Here is an invented, deliberately small example. A support dashboard lists tickets from oldest to newest, and the user presses Next to continue where the previous page ended.

For this exercise, the dataset stays fixed while we paginate. Each ticket has an integer creation time and a unique integer ID. Sort by creation time first, then ID, and return only tickets strictly after the last one already shown. The page size is a positive integer.

Our entire dataset fits on a sticky note. Ticket 41 was created at time 100, ticket 42 was also created at time 100, and ticket 43 was created at time 101.

With one ticket per page, the reader should see 41, then 42, then 43. No advanced algorithm is hiding here, which is exactly why this makes a useful review exercise.

# Each row is (created_second, ticket_id). def next_page(rows, after, limit): ordered = sorted(rows) if after is not None: ordered = [ row for row in ordered if row[0] > after[0] ] return ordered[:limit]

After the first page, our cursor is (100, 41). The function keeps only tickets with a creation time greater than 100, so it jumps straight to ticket 43. Ticket 42 quietly disappears from the walk through the results.

Nothing crashed, and the remaining ticket looks perfectly reasonable. Checking that the response contains one ticket would let this bug stroll right past us.

Write the expected answer before admiring the patch

Before asking an agent to repair anything, preserve the smallest example that demonstrates the problem. In this case, we can justify the answer by hand because ticket 42 follows ticket 41 in the agreed ordering.

def test_next_page_keeps_a_ticket_with_the_same_time(): rows = [(100, 41), (100, 42), (101, 43)] result = next_page(rows, after=(100, 41), limit=1) assert result == [(100, 42)]

Following the original code, the actual result is [(101, 43)], so this assertion should fail. That failure matters because it connects our test to the complaint. These are hand-traced outcomes for the example, rather than a claimed test run.

Notice where the expected answer came from. We worked it out from the ordering rule. We did not call another helper from the implementation to calculate what the result should be, because that helper might carry the same mistake.

If the test passes on the original code, pause before applying the patch. Check that it imports the intended function, that the fixture actually contains a tie, and that the test runner discovered the test. If it fails because a dependency is missing, we still have setup work to do before we have evidence about pagination.

A failure needs to fail for the right reason

An assertion showing ticket 43 where ticket 42 belongs is useful. An import error, a timeout from an unavailable database, or a test skipped by configuration does not demonstrate this particular bug.

Honestly, this is where a confident AI summary can make a messy situation feel cleaner than it is. Keep the actual command and output so somebody else can distinguish a reproduced defect from an environment problem.

Now repair the rule that lost the ticket

Our ordering uses two fields, but the filter remembers only one. The correction is to compare the complete cursor in the same order used for sorting.

def next_page(rows, after, limit): ordered = sorted(rows) if after is not None: ordered = [row for row in ordered if row > after] return ordered[:limit]

Python compares these tuples in order. A later creation time qualifies immediately. When the creation times match, a larger ID qualifies. That makes (100, 42) greater than (100, 41), so ticket 42 stays in the result.

Changing the time comparison from > to >= looks tempting, but it lets ticket 41 back into the next page. We need to include the other tickets at the same time while excluding the ticket already shown.

Keep this repair narrow enough to explain. Renaming every variable, introducing a pagination class, and reorganizing the repository would make it harder to see which change addressed the missing ticket.

The small function is a teaching model that sorts an in-memory list. A real database implementation needs a matching order, cursor predicate and suitable index. With ascending non-null fields, its filter would express created_at > cursor_time OR (created_at = cursor_time AND id > cursor_id). It also needs defined behavior when records change between requests, which our fixed-dataset example deliberately leaves outside its contract.

Bring the old mistake back while the test stays still

Once the focused test passes on the patch, make a disposable local copy containing both the patch and the test. Restore only the old timestamp only comparison there, leaving the test and its expected answer unchanged.

The same assertion should fail again, with ticket 43 replacing ticket 42. Now we have checked that this test can distinguish the repaired behavior from the original defect.

This is a small mutation check, meaning we deliberately put a known mistake into the code to see whether the test detects it. Keep that experiment away from production, and retain the clean patched version separately.

If the restored bug still passes, investigate before celebrating. The test might be importing a different copy, a mock might be bypassing the comparison, or the assertion might be too weak to notice which ticket returned.

One successful mutation check does not prove the whole program correct. It tells us something narrower and genuinely useful, which is that this test detects this mistake under these conditions.

Give the fix a few uncomfortable neighbors

Our first case protects ticket 42, but the repair also has to preserve the next page, the end of the list and the page-size rule. These nearby cases make it harder for a special-case patch to masquerade as a general correction.

In a real test file, keep the input data fixed and write the expected results explicitly. Here is one compact way to express those cases using pytest parametrization.

import pytest @pytest.mark.parametrize( ("after", "limit", "expected"), [ ((100, 41), 1, [(100, 42)]), ((100, 42), 1, [(101, 43)]), ((101, 43), 1, []), ((100, 41), 2, [(100, 42), (101, 43)]), (None, 1, [(100, 41)]), ], ) def test_page_boundaries(after, limit, expected): rows = [(101, 43), (100, 42), (100, 41)] assert next_page(rows, after, limit) == expected

I deliberately shuffled the input so the test also asks the function to honor its ordering rule. For a larger practice exercise, walk all the pages and compare the collected IDs with a hand-written expected list. That catches omissions and duplicates across page boundaries.

Then run the relevant existing suite. A focused regression test can protect our missing ticket while missing a broken route, a changed response field or an authorization check elsewhere.

Match the check to the place where users get hurt

For a database backed endpoint, add coverage that reaches the actual query and cursor encoding. For a frontend race, control the order in which requests resolve and check what appears on screen. For a permission bug, exercise the relevant user boundary instead of checking only a helper that returns true.

Choose the next check by asking which layer could still break the promised behavior. That is a better guide than trying to make an arbitrary test-count number look impressive.

Give your agent a job that ends with evidence

You can ask an AI assistant to help construct the reproduction, but review the expected answer before letting it repair the implementation. Otherwise, the assistant can accidentally write a test that politely agrees with its own patch.

For this kind of bug, a useful request can stay wonderfully plain.

Reproduce the reported failure before changing application code. Explain why the expected answer follows from the requirement. Run the focused test on the original implementation and show the command, the failure, and why it matches the report. Make a focused repair without weakening the assertion. If a test expectation needs changing, explain the contract conflict first instead of silently updating it. Check the patched code with the same test and nearby cases. In a disposable local copy, restore the original defect and check that the regression test detects it again. Report what ran, what did not run, and what remains uncertain.

This request does not make an agent infallible. It makes its work easier to inspect, and it gives you specific places to intervene when a result is missing or an assumption is wrong.

If the project cannot run locally, preserve the reproduction and explain the blocker. A reasoned expectation can still help a reviewer, but label it as unexecuted. There is no need to dress up a prediction as a green test result.

Use your next practice session to leave a better trail

For a follow-up, try an existing API debugging exercise and choose one failing behavior before touching the rest. That exercise covers a different movie-search API, so our ticket-pagination patch is not its answer. The reusable part is the evidence trail.

Keep one note with the requirement, the smallest reproduction, the cause, the focused change and the checks you actually ran. During an interview, that note becomes a clear explanation of your decisions. During a review, it gives the next engineer somewhere useful to start.

Some real bugs are too intermittent to reproduce on demand. For those, capture the relevant timing, versions and observations, then use controlled scheduling, replay or monitoring where possible. Be honest about the uncertainty instead of promising that one neat unit test settles the incident.

What I want from an AI fix is a trail I can follow from the complaint to the corrected behavior. Once I understand that trail, I can explain the change without rereading the assistant’s entire conversation.

Before accepting your next patch, try keeping the test still while bringing the mistake back. If it catches the bug again, you have earned a much more useful kind of confidence.

If California undid its ‘original sin’ and matched Texas on taxing property, it would raise more revenue than the billionaire tax, economists say

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

California voters will decide on Proposition 40 soon, but looming over the controversial billionaire wealth tax proposal is another state ballot measure from nearly 50 years ago.

In 1978, voters approved Proposition 13, which capped property taxes at just 1% of assessed value, limited annual hikes to 2%, and allowed reassessments to market value only after sales or substantial renovations.

It came amid a wider tax revolt leading up to Ronald Reagan’s election as president and an era of conservative governance in America. After Prop 13’s passage, the state had to find other ways to raise more revenue and relied more on taxing incomes, including capital gains. 

California now has the most progressive income-tax structure in the country with a top marginal rate of 13.3%. When combined with federal income tax rates, that can send the total to more than 50%.

The top 1% of earners account for up to half of state revenue, though it also means state coffers are volatile and swing on stock market returns. At the same time, the ultra-wealthy often don’t receive much income and instead leverage assets to finance their lifestyles, avoiding some taxes.

Now enter the billionaire tax, which calls for residents worth more than $1 billion to pay a one-time tax equivalent to 5% of their assets. The union pushing Prop 40 has said the wealth tax could raise $100 billion in revenue and help offset federal cuts to health spending.

Supporters point to the AI boom and say California’s ultra-rich would still be among the world’s wealthiest. Opponents have warned about the impact the tax could have on economic growth and startups. They have also said it would force founders to sell big pieces of their companies and eventually cause the wealthy to flee to another state, resulting in a net loss of revenue over time.

If California is looking for a large source of undertaxed wealth, “a much better, bigger target lies right beneath their feet,” according to Princeton economist Owen Zidar and University of Chicago Booth School of Business economist Eric Zwick, authors of the book The Everywhere Millionaire.

“The original sin underlying California’s tax structure is Prop 13, the 1978 law that rewrote the rules of property ownership,” they wrote in a recent New York Times op-ed. “Getting rid of it would be a more efficient and durable way to make the rich pay their fair share.”

Prop 13 also contributed to rapidly increasing home values, which have risen faster than property taxes. As a result, California homeowners pay an average of just 0.8% tax on their property, Zidar and Zwick estimated, generating $100 billion in revenue in fiscal 2025.

By contrast, Texas homeowners pay 2% on average. If California matched Texas, overall residential property tax revenue would more than double, while also easing home prices by around 13%, they added.

To be sure, property taxes in Texas have stirred their own voter revolt during the midterm election season. While the state doesn’t levy income taxes, Texans complain that the overall tax burden remains onerous due to property taxes, which are levied by local governments as well.

And getting rid of Prop 13 in California wouldn’t go down well with voters either as many would suffer big increases on their property taxes. Still, California’s tax base is already narrow, and Prop 40 would make it even more top-heavy. Meanwhile, the state plans to spend $540 billion this fiscal year.

“It is simply not possible to fund the state’s needs by leaning on a few hundred people, as politically appealing as that may be,” Zidar and Zwick argued. “For California to tax wealth in a progressive fashion and meaningfully improve its fiscal position, it needs to broaden the aperture beyond billionaires to the hundreds of thousands of other very wealthy people who could easily afford to contribute more by paying a real estate tax that finally works.”

Laura Tyson, a former White House economic adviser, and Lenny Mendonca, a former chief economic adviser to California Governor Gavin Newsom, warned billionaires respond strongly to estate taxes when deciding where to live.

Already, six billionaires who would have owed about one-quarter of Prop 40’s expected revenues left the state before the proposed effective date on the wealth tax, they pointed out in a Project Syndicate column.

And since Prop 40 proposes a new, untested method of taxation based on all assets, it would fuel a “tidal wave of litigation, rendering the promised funding both speculative and unreliable,” they wrote.

“Instead of a tax on billionaires, the U.S. needs to raise taxes on the capital income that feeds wealth,” Tyson and Mendonca argued. “There are a variety of ways to do so: through higher corporate tax rates; through taxes on realized and unrealized capital gains; by closing the most egregious loopholes, like carried interest; and by increasing inheritance taxes.”

Alan Shearer has revealed he once turned down the chance to manage Cardiff City.

Shearer took interim charge of Newcastle for eight games 17 years ago, but was unable to save his beloved Magpies from relegationGetty

But the all-time leading Premier League goalscorer decided that he was done with management at that early stage, instead turning has hand to punditry with the BBC – most notably as a regular on Match of the Day.

Why did Shearer say no to Cardiff?

Explaining his decision to snub Cardiff, Shearer told the BBC: “Newcastle were in desperate trouble and I could never say no [to taking that job] – we were bottom of the league, fighting for our lives.

“I should have known that when I signed the contract on April Fool’s Day that it wasn’t going to work out!”

Following his brief spell in the St. James’ Park dugout, Shearer was initially open to staying in management – but he ultimately changed his mind.

“I always felt that I was going to go into it again and I wanted to,” he continued.

“I had a couple of interviews and got offered the job at Cardiff. And then things changed… and I thought, ‘What am I doing here?'”

Shearer stays loyal to Match of the Day

Shearer has now been part of the Match of the Day team for the best part of two decades, also working for the BBC on live coverage of the World Cup, FA Cup and more.

The 56-year-old also co-hosts The Rest Is Football alongside Match of the Day colleagues Gary Lineker and Micah Richards.

Lineker fronted Match of the Day when Shearer came onboard, but left the BBC last year.

Since Lineker’s departure, the iconic Premier League highlights show has employed a rotating presenting line-up of Kelly Cates, Mark Chapman and Gabby Logan.

An alarming new survey from media and tech non-profit Common Sense Media has found that children begin interacting with AI chatbots at an alarmingly early age — with nearly half of 9-12-year-olds having bypassed the 13+ age restriction common to most LLMs. 

The survey is titled The 2026 Common Sense Media Census: Tech and Media Use by Tweens and Teens. In the paper, the organization conducted interviews with more than 1,200 American children aged 9 to 17, asking questions about AI use as well as television, gaming, and social media habits. More importantly, the interviews asked these young children how they feel about their own media consumption habits.

Standout findings include the sharp rise in smartphone ownership, with more than half of 11-year-olds already owning a smartphone and near-universal smartphone access by age 17, as well as the incredible amount of time spent on screens: eight hours per day for teenagers and five hours per day for tweens. 

Because social scientists were caught unprepared by the massive impact social media has had on teen mental health, there's a strong push for more studies into the effects of AI interaction on teenagers, especially in the areas of school preparedness and mental health. 

A second study, titled "Exploring Artificial Intelligence [AI]: Understanding Relationships between AI Use and Mental Health Among Middle and High School Students," was commissioned by the JEF Foundation, a mental health advocacy group. They interviewed more than 5,000 American middle and high school students from 21 different schools between Oct. 2025 and April 2026, which resulted in the following four findings, taken verbatim from their report:

  • Students use AI for a wide range of cognitive and emotional support, including turning to AI when they’re struggling emotionally.

  • Certain uses of AI are associated with worse mental health and well-being outcomes, and greater lifetime history of suicidal thoughts and attempts.

  • Students who use AI while emotionally struggling have worse mental health and well-being outcomes, and greater history of suicidal thoughts and attempts.

  • Patterns of AI use differ across demographics, with important implications for mental health disparities.

JED Foundation psychologist Kayla Anderson summarized the findings to NPR: "When young people are using AI, when they're in emotional distress, they're more likely to report poor mental health and well-being outcomes," but she was also quick to stress that the study did not establish causality. In other words, they don't know if AI is making teen mental health worse, or if teens with worse mental health are gravitating to AI chatbots for help.

Circle Begins CCTP V1 Wind-Down Amid Doc Mismatch

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Burn limits on Circle's legacy USDC bridge began falling on October 31, and the V1 contracts pause on December 1.

On the same migration page, a block of instructions written for AI coding assistants still says Noble, Sui and Aptos "still require V1." Circle's own release notes say two of those three chains moved to V2 weeks ago.

Circle's legacy cross-chain transfer protocol entered its final month today. From October 31, burn limits on CCTP V1 start falling toward zero, and on December 1 the V1 contracts pause. Any app still using them will stop moving USDC between chains.

Most coverage has focused on those two dates. But the guide developers are told to follow, Circle's V1-to-V2 migration page, contains a mistake in the section written for AI tools.

Embedded in the page is an "Agent Instructions" block, guidance meant for AI coding assistants that read the documentation for developers. It says: "Noble, Sui, and Aptos still require V1." For two of those three chains, Circle's other published records say the opposite.

What Starts On October 31

The schedule is fixed. Circle announced in an August 27 blog post that "CCTP V1 (Legacy) deprecation begins October 31, 2026 and completes on December 1, 2026."

From today, V1 burn limits fall, and the migration guide says "message limits will tighten over time until no new burns can be initiated." On December 1 the V1 contracts pause, and any integration still pointing at them can no longer transfer USDC between chains.

Circle has been firm about this. "Migration from CCTP V1 (Legacy) to CCTP V2 is required, not just recommended," the company wrote, adding that V2 "uses separate contracts and APIs and is not backward compatible."

Funds already in transit are protected. The guide says "all pending redemptions will remain available" during the phase-out, and that Circle "will maintain minter allowances greater than the total of pending attestations."

The Line Hidden In Circle's Agent Instructions

The migration page has two audiences. Human readers see the main guide. AI coding assistants also parse a separate instruction block, which tells them how to answer developer questions.

In that block, the guidance is clear: "Noble, Sui, and Aptos still require V1."

The main text above it says something different. According to the human-facing guide, "CCTP V2 contracts are available on all CCTP V1 chains except for Noble and Sui," and "Sui will be supported by V2 before the deprecation begins." Aptos isn't listed as an exception there at all.

So one page gives two different answers about the same chains, and the out-of-date answer is the one written for AI tools.

What Circle's Own Records Say About Sui And Aptos

Circle's 2026 CCTP release notes show how out of date the instruction is:

  • August 25: "CCTP V2 support for Aptos added (domain 9)." The note says Aptos had previously been available only on CCTP V1 (Legacy).
  • October 8: Sui mainnet and testnet were added as supported CCTP chains (domain 8). The migration guide had said this would happen before deprecation began.

Circle's supported chains and domains page now lists both Sui and Aptos in its CCTP table, with Standard Transfer support. The page's V1-only section names one chain: Noble.

As of today, Aptos has had V2 for more than two months and Sui for more than three weeks. The instruction telling AI tools that both "still require V1" was overtaken by Circle's own updates before the shutdown began.

Noble Is The Real V1-Only Chain

Noble is the one chain in that line where the instruction is still accurate, and it is leaving CCTP entirely.

In a September 10 announcement, Circle said "Noble will not receive CCTP V2" and set out its exit:

  • October 13: new USDC minting to Noble through Circle Mint stopped.
  • October 31: CCTP V1 burn limits began falling.
  • December 1: CCTP exits from Noble may be limited to destination chains that still support CCTP V1.
  • January 12, 2027: the Noble USDC contract and CCTP routes are fully paused, and Circle takes a snapshot of remaining balances.
  • January 13, 2027: Noble is no longer reachable through Circle Mint or CCTP, and a manual redemption process opens.

For Noble, V1 is the only route available, and it is being closed.

Why A Stale Instruction Matters Now

A wrong line in documentation would normally be a small issue, but the timing makes this one more serious.

Many developers now ask AI assistants to write or update integration code, and those assistants read documentation like this page. A developer asking how to bridge USDC to Sui or Aptos could be told, based on Circle's own instruction block, to use V1. That is the route whose burn limits started falling today and which pauses in a month.

The fix in the code is not small either. Moving to V2 means:

  • pointing at new contract addresses
  • using new ABIs, since depositForBurn takes extra parameters
  • choosing a transfer speed through finalityThreshold
  • replacing the attestation flow with /v2/messages/{sourceDomainId}
  • accounting for fees, which V1 did not charge

An integration built on V1 now would have to be rebuilt within weeks.

What Integrators Should Check Before December 1

For teams working with Sui and Aptos, the published records point one way: build on V2, using the contract addresses for domains 8 and 9. Don't follow the V1 guidance from the agent-instruction block.

For Noble, the deadline is January 12, 2027. Before then, integrators need to remove Noble as a CCTP route, and holders need to move or redeem their USDC.

For everyone else, the burn-limit reductions have started. December 1 is the last day V1 integrations work, and any app still on V1 after that will stop moving USDC between chains.

Generosity is a marketing strategy. Unit economics is a survival strategy. Here is how my "Founder's Launch Program" saved my indie app.

The best week of my indie life.

It started with a stranger on Reddit.

He'd tried my native notes app, EpicNotes, on his Xiaomi phone and left a comment: "Feels fast and nice. But there's some minor visual weirdness on my phone." I replied offering him a free 3-month Pro code as a thank-you for the feedback. He said yes. Then came more. A fellow tester who cancelled his subscription on principle but still sent me bug reports with screenshots. A student from India who emailed: "I'm a student, so I can't afford it. If you could make a lifetime promo code available for me, I would be able to fully use it and provide suggestions and bug reports. Please 🙏"

I granted him lifetime Pro in about two minutes. It felt amazing. People were using my app. People were talking to me. After six months of building native Kotlin Multiplatform code at night after my day job as a product manager, this was the part that finally felt real.

I thought I was doing indie development right. I was doing indie development feelingly. Not mathematically.

The math I refused to do.

Here's the embarrassing part: I'm a product manager by day. I build business cases for a living. I would never ship a feature at work without a rough cost-benefit estimate.

And yet at 1 a.m., I was handing out free Pro access to an app with AI features without ever opening a spreadsheet.

So let me open it now, in public.

What a free Pro user costs me:

  • Every AI summary and smart web clip is a call to a third-party AI API. Pennies per call — but power users summarize a lot.
  • Every synced note is compute, storage, and bandwidth on my cloud backend.
  • Every 90-day code, and especially every lifetime grant, is a commitment to pay both of those bills for months or years.

What a free Pro user pays me: $0.

My actual revenue at the time: three paying subscribers. Single-digit monthly recurring revenue.

The scary part wasn't that the number was big. It was that the number was small enough to ignore — and big enough to bleed. A leak doesn't need to be a flood to sink a boat that isn't moving.

Why free users felt like growth (and weren't).

The indie playbook says: give things away early, collect feedback, build goodwill. And the playbook is right — partially.

That Xiaomi tester? His free code paid for itself ten times over. He sent screenshots of two real bugs (a settings sheet clipped by system insets, a formatting popup buried under the soft keyboard), his exact device model, and unprompted pricing research: "I have a rule against using subscription-based services." The announcement and the numbers

The most concrete starting point is the dated disclosure from Binance. It offers a specific basis for assessing Binance’s $6.3 billion hot-wallet breach without turning the story into unsupported price speculation.

The company reported approximately $3 billion of affected assets frozen on-chain through coordination with issuers, investigators and other industry participants. The development arrives as digital-asset markets remain sensitive to funding conditions, infrastructure reliability and the gap between promotional claims and operational results. It warrants attention on its own facts, without assuming a particular cryptocurrency must rise or fall in response.

Why the development matters

The question for market participants is what Binance’s $6.3 billion hot-wallet breach establishes today and what remains uncertain. An immediate reaction is not the same as evidence of a durable economic outcome.

For traders and institutions, the issue is not confined to the headline loss. A platform can promise balance-sheet compensation while users remain exposed to withdrawal interruptions, unsettled orders and uncertainty about the integrity of infrastructure on which they rely.

Users face two different forms of risk during a security incident. Asset losses are the direct financial concern, while withdrawals, service interruptions and unclear communications can make it difficult to assess or manage exposure. Investigations are strongest when confirmed technical evidence is separated from hypotheses about attackers.

Investors also need to separate changes in infrastructure from changes in token demand. Revenue may accrue to a company, a protocol treasury or a service provider without necessarily creating a one-for-one benefit for holders of a related token. Market impact depends on adoption, competition and the design of whatever fees or incentives are involved.

Risks the headline does not resolve

None of this removes the need for independent assessment. The published evidence identifies a development, while its risk and long-term economics are still open to examination.

Binance initially cited a potentially compromised laptop, but said subsequent work identified further points of intrusion. It stated that investigators had not found evidence that wallet private keys were leaked. That distinction leaves the precise authorization and infrastructure failure unresolved until a verified technical post-mortem is published.

Frozen tokens are not the same as recovered cash, and assets immobilized on-chain still require legal and technical steps to return. The exchange’s reimbursement commitment is an announcement rather than independently audited proof that every affected account has already been restored.

Security controls have to account for both malicious transactions and legitimate users who need access. Freezing, recovery and reimbursement mechanisms are not interchangeable: a frozen asset may be immobilized, a reimbursed user may be made whole, and a forensic investigation may still be incomplete.

The milestones to watch

The immediate checklist is relatively straightforward: look for verifiable implementation details and avoid interpreting a stated target as an outcome that has already occurred.

Binance said it was targeting a phased restart of withdrawals after security validation, planned an updated proof-of-reserves report by November 2 and expected to disclose further verified technical findings. Those milestones, rather than speculative attacker attribution, are the tests for the recovery process.

A durable signal would be consistent follow-through: actual usage, transparent reporting and a credible explanation of risk. Short-term token-price moves can be driven by macroeconomic conditions or leverage and may say little about whether the product or initiative succeeds over months and years.

From a market perspective, the first task is to separate the scale of the announcement from the scale of economic activity that has actually occurred. A company may report a large addressable market, an institutional name or a future capability, but those are not equivalent to settled transactions or recurring fee income. This is why the primary disclosure is a better starting point than a trading narrative attached to it.

How artificial intelligence is changing the meaning of human creativity

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence can now create stunning artwork in seconds, producing images that once required years of training and practice.

But something important may be lost when the creative process becomes almost effortless.

A new paper argues that the greatest value of making art has never been the finished product alone. It is how the creative process changes the person behind it.

As AI increasingly separates artistic achievement from human experience, one researcher is raising questions about what creativity should mean in a world where machines can produce remarkable results.

Amit Raphael Zoran is a computer scientist at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, where he builds craft and fabrication tools in its School of Computer Science and Engineering.

His paper presents a philosophical argument rather than experimental findings, so its conclusions remain open to debate.

“For centuries, creating art meant going through a process of learning, experimenting, and changing as a person,” Zoran said.

How art changes us

Zoran’s answer to what human creativity is for starts by splitting what art does into two jobs.

One is the artwork itself: the painting, the song, the building that carries a meaning from one person to the next.

The other is what he calls artistic becoming, the change that making art, or meeting it, brings about in a person.

In his account, the change happens at three levels. A painter’s eyes and hands are trained over years. A viewer walks out of a gallery seeing differently.

And whole cultures shift: Renaissance perspective reorganized how people saw space, and Cubism unsettled assumptions about objects and viewpoint.

Zoran doesn’t claim the split itself is new. The philosopher John Dewey separated the art product from art as experience in the 1930s.

What Zoran adds is a claim about history: for most of it, the two stayed tied together only because making a sophisticated work required the maker’s years of change.

AI changes the process

Earlier computer art kept the tie. Harold Cohen’s drawing program AARON and the interactive installations that followed it supplied precision and repetition, while people supplied interpretation and meaning.

Generative models work on a different material. They are trained on the recorded traces of human culture, what Zoran calls an unevenly weighted and culturally skewed slice of the human record.

From that material, they produce images and music with qualities that once took years of training. The years are no longer required.

Zoran argues that AI has separated the quality of a creative work from the years of human experience traditionally required to produce it.

Nor does the maker’s change disappear, in his telling; it moves. Prompting, curating, and iterating with a model are real skills.

But they work on symbols instead of clay or wood, and many of them can be picked up without a long apprenticeship.

“Today, AI allows us to produce sophisticated works without necessarily undergoing that journey. But this doesn’t mean human creativity has lost its value. It means we need to rethink where that value lies,” Zoran said.

Judging the tools differently

Image and music generators are usually judged on output: realism, coherence, fluency, how closely a song fits its genre.

Zoran wants a second set of questions. Does a tool widen what a person explores or narrow it? Does it push users toward familiar forms or expose them to unfamiliar ones?

Zoran acknowledges that measuring how creativity changes a person is difficult. A new way of seeing leaves no trace in a model’s outputs, and asking people is a weak instrument.

So he proposes measuring conditions instead. An engineer can count how broad a range a system leads users to explore, and how often they explore instead of merely retrieving.

A tool’s defaults can be checked for how far they drift toward the statistically familiar. Recommendation feeds have already shown how easily software narrows what people encounter.

Experiencing art differently

Zoran describes three projects from his own creative work to illustrate how technology might expand human experience.

However, the projects do not establish whether these approaches actually produce lasting changes.

In the first, a live lecture on perception turns out to be partly prerecorded.

Several versions of the speaker start talking to one another, so the audience feels how easily confidence in a digital presence can be manipulated.

For the second, participants taste an essence reconstructed from an old oak. It was put together from connections among historical alchemy, botany, and chemistry that would have been hard to assemble without computers.

The third puts a tree at the center of a multisensory environment. Visitors smell and taste an essence from the tree and listen to sounds recorded from it.

Generative AI turns those sounds into music that a human musician performs acoustically, while projections show structures a person can’t normally see.

No one measured what any of this did to the people in the room, and Zoran wrote as much.

Zoran names a limit of his own. His examples and the thinkers he leans on are all Western.

East Asian calligraphy has long valued the brush stroke as a record of the writer’s training, and Japanese craft treats repair and imperfection as a continuation of an object’s life.

The tie between a work and its maker may run tighter there, and the thesis has not been tested against those traditions.

Creativity beyond the finished product

He closes with commitments. Artists should treat the effort a model saves as a budget and spend it on experience the model can’t supply.

Schools, museums, and funders should value process and documented engagement alongside finished pieces, since a finished piece no longer certifies them.

Tool builders should design against convergence and make provenance legible, because these systems increasingly shape what people notice and prefer.

Zoran argues that the future of creativity should not be judged solely by what artificial intelligence can produce. The more important question is what people gain, or lose, when they use it.

If technology removes much of the effort involved in making art, that freedom could open new opportunities for exploration, experimentation, and personal growth.

But whether AI ultimately expands human creativity or narrows it may depend on how people choose to use these increasingly powerful tools.

The full study was published in the journal AI & Society.
