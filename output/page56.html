When Electric Cars Were for Women

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


At the turn of the last century, the automobile industry was still new and evolving rapidly. In 1898, there were about 50 carmakers in the United States. A decade later, that number had increased fivefold. In 1901, the companies produced 7,000 vehicles. By 1910, production had reached 181,000.

For several decades, electric cars vied with those powered by steam and internal combustion, and nobody was sure which technology would come out on top. Steam had the legacy of locomotive technology behind it but came with a risk of fire. Steam-powered vehicles could also become exceedingly hot, depending on the placement of the boiler and the steam piping. Both gasoline and electric cars faced logistical challenges: How far could you travel without refueling or charging? You could always throw an extra can of gasoline in the back of the car, but charging a battery involved more planning. The infrastructure for long-distance travel was not yet in place.

Before the invention of the electric starter in 1911, gasoline-powered engines had to be started with a hand crank; once running, they were loud and emitted noxious fumes. Fully electric vehicles, by contrast, were easy to start; you just turned on the main battery switch, often with a key, and there was no foul exhaust to deal with.

Electric carmakers looking for a way to differentiate their products decided that these attributes made their vehicles perfect for women. (Electric cars were also marketed to doctors so that they could make house calls in the middle of the night without waking the neighbors.) General Electric, which made car chargers based on mercury-arc rectifiers, was so captivated by the idea of women driving electric cars that the company created a series of advertisements featuring stylishly dressed women plugging in their cars and posing with their charging stations.

In ads for its electric car chargers, General Electric featured women using the equipment.miSci—Museum of Innovation and Science

A young woman named Fannie Goodnow was an early EV adopter. She owned a 1908 Bailey Victoria Phaeton, an open two-seater that really did look like a horseless carriage, and she used a GE mercury-arc rectifier to charge it.

Who Invented the Mercury-Arc Rectifier?

Electrical engineer Peter Cooper Hewitt invented the mercury vapor lamp, which became the basis for the mercury-arc rectifier used in car-charging systems. Bettmann/Getty Images

Electrical engineer Peter Cooper Hewitt is generally credited with the invention of the mercury-arc rectifier. During the first decade of the 20th century, Hewitt received several U.S. patents for electric lamps and other apparatus for transmitting and using electric currents. U.S. patents 968,896 and 1,163,706, granted to Percy H. Thomas, advanced Hewitt’s designs. Thomas was chief electrician for the Cooper Hewitt Electric Co., which Hewitt had cofounded with George Westinghouse to manufacture and sell his lamps.

The mercury-arc rectifier functioned as a diode. In a car charger like the one Fannie Goodnow used, it converted alternating current from the power grid into direct current needed to charge the car’s battery. A small amount of liquid mercury contained in a glass tube would vaporize and glow as electricity passed through it. Coupled with the carbon anodes, the vapor allowed current to flow in only one direction. This unit took about 12 hours to charge a drained battery.

A Carriage Maker Pivots to Electric Cars

I couldn’t find any records as to why Fannie Goodnow opted for an electric car. But as a well-to-do woman living in the Boston area, she had more than a half dozen automobile producers to choose from. The one she chose was S.R. Bailey & Co. of Amesbury, Mass.

This early electric car charger used a mercury-arc rectifier to convert AC to DC.Heritage Museums & Gardens

According to Galen Handy, who maintains the Early Electric Vehicles website, Samuel Robinson Bailey started in the carriage and sleigh business as a teenager, winning a best-in-show prize at a carriage exhibition in Portland, Maine, in 1855. Bailey developed a reputation as an inventor and designed processes to streamline the manufacture of his vehicles. He went into business for himself in 1882. Just over a decade later, one of Bailey’s carriages won a gold medal at the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago.

Bailey’s son Edwin attended the exposition, where he likely would have seen a vision of the future: the Morrison electric wagon. This six-passenger, battery-powered vehicle ferried visitors and VIPs around the fairgrounds. It wasn’t long before automobiles began eating into the carriage business. In December 1906, with the carriage industry collapsing, S.R. Bailey & Co. announced it was getting into electric vehicles.

Thomas Edison Designed a Bad Battery

Bailey’s first model, the Victoria Phaeton, used an Edison alkaline battery. Thomas Edison had been working on a lighter, more reliable, and more powerful battery for the auto industry since the 1890s, and announced the achievement with great fanfare in 1903. Unfortunately for Bailey and other carmakers who deployed it, the battery leaked and wouldn’t hold a charge. Edison was forced to go back to the drawing board. By 1910, he had a better battery in production, but by then his friend Henry Ford had introduced the Model T, setting the stage for the internal combustion engine to take over the market.

While Edison toiled to improve his tech, Bailey pivoted to a battery from the Gould Storage Battery Co. of Depew, N.Y. Gould also supplied batteries for railroads, utilities, subways, elevators, and farms. Goodnow’s Victoria Phaeton used a 30-cell lead-acid battery housed in a wooden box that was suspended under the chassis. The standard battery had a range of 64 to 80 kilometers; a larger battery with 50 percent more capacity was available for an upcharge. With the standard battery, the Victoria Phaeton cost US $2,000 (about $73,000 today, or about the list price of an upscale EV like the Audi Q8 or BMW i5).

Fannie Goodnow’s electric car charger now resides at the Heritage Museums & Gardens in Sandwich, Mass. But her 1908 Bailey Victoria Phaeton has been lost to history. Shown here is a 1911 version of that model.Sarah Thornington/Heritage Museums & Gardens

Goodnow installed a mercury-arc rectifier charging station in the barn adjacent to her parents’ summer home in Hull, Mass. Bill Lillie of Vintage Electric Cars notes that the charging plug was of an unusual design. This is, of course, one of the banes of any early adopter: nonstandardized plugs and cables. The Electric Vehicle Association of America, which formed in 1910, tried to put forward a standard so that electric vehicles could be charged at any charging station. (Modern EV owners can no doubt relate.) But savvy electric car owners knew to carry spare plug adapters and cables when traveling.

When Edison’s improved battery was finally available, Bailey began using it in the Victoria Phaeton and then went on a publicity blitz. In September 1910, the carmaker sponsored a round-trip tour from New York to Boston, with a side trip up New Hampshire’s Mount Washington, totaling about 1,600 km. Sadly, the “This Car Climbed Mt. Washington” bumper sticker hadn’t yet become popular.

Thomas Edison supplied the batteries for an electric car made by S.R. Bailey & Co., which then went on a publicity road trip from New York to Boston. miSci—Museum of Innovation and Science

Although Thomas Edison posed for a photo with the new Bailey Victoria Phaeton that featured his new battery, he himself chose a Detroit Electric Victoria for his own use. (Edison was known to be a terrible driver, so someone else likely chauffeured him around.)

Unable to compete against the rising tide of Model Ts, S.R. Bailey sold its factory in 1915, having produced about 500 to 600 electric vehicles.

Although the vehicle Fannie Goodnow chose turned out to be just a blip in automotive history, I like to imagine her driving about town in a beautiful car that embodied all the quirks of early adoption. I hope her friends didn’t make fun of her for investing in electric just before internal combustion took off. I hope she didn’t get stranded without the proper charging cable. I do not know what happened to her or her Victoria Phaeton, but her charging station remained in situ until 2022, when it was donated to the Heritage Museums & Gardens in Sandwich, Mass. Now it stands ready to tell stories of technological change and adoption that continue to resonate today.

Fujifilm Instax Mini Evo 2 review: A hybrid instant camera that changed my mind

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I thought previewing photos would take the fun out of instant photography. Instead, the Mini Evo 2 made me want to experiment.

Abby Ferguson

When Fujifilm released its first hybrid instant camera in 2017, I was a bit flummoxed by the concept, which combines the features of a digital point-and-shoot with those of an instant camera. Subsequent models didn’t do much to change my mind, either. Much of the joy I find in instant cameras comes from their inherent spontaneity, and the prospect of seeing my photos before printing them seemed as if it would take away from that. I hadn’t ever used one, though, so this was all an assumption based on what I knew the cameras to be.  

Nearly a decade later, I finally had a chance to use one of Fujifilm’s hybrid instant cameras, via the Instax Mini Evo 2. And after spending some time with the camera, I have to admit that I now consider myself a fan of the format.  

What is the Instax Mini Evo 2?

Abby Ferguson

As with the rest of the Evo series, the Mini Evo 2 captures and stores digital photos that you can transfer and share like any other digital file, and you can also print them on Instax film. They also let you print as many copies of individual photos as you want, unlike traditional instant cameras, which produce a one-off Instax image. 

The Mini Evo 2 is the successor to the original Mini Evo, bringing a larger sensor and replacing the 3-inch LCD with a 4.5-inch touchscreen. It also gains a new wide-angle mode, face-detection autofocus and a video mode called “Immersive Movie” that records short clips before you take a photo. The camera retains its retro-inspired controls, including the film advance-style lever that triggers a print, a dial for changing the film effects and a dial on the lens for adjusting lens effects.

As the “mini” in the name suggests, the camera uses Fujifilm’s Instax Mini film. This is the smallest film in the company’s lineup, and results in a 62 x 46mm (2.4 x 1.8″) image area. The camera has some limited built-in storage, with Fujifilm specifying the ability to store 45 still images (it doesn’t provide details on capacity beyond that). It accepts microSD cards, which is necessary if you want to use the Immersive Movie mode or save the larger, wide-angle images. 

Regaining creative control

The dial on the top left of the camera adjusts the “film effect” mode. Abby Ferguson

After only a few shots with the Mini Evo 2, it won me over. In fact, it addressed a lot of the complaints I had with the Instax Square 2. 

One of those improvements is the inclusion of autofocus, which takes away the need to judge subject distances and select a focus zone. With the Square 2, I frequently misjudged, resulting in out of focus images. I found the autofocus to be generally reliable, especially for what I’d expect from a camera like this.

Abby Ferguson

I didn’t take many portraits, but some of the ones I took were in low light, and it still managed to find the face and focus appropriately. I had less success with Macro mode, which sometimes struggled to focus on small subjects even when they were beyond the specified minimum focusing distance of 10cm (3.9″). But for general photos, the autofocus gave me reliably in-focus images without any work on my part. 

Beyond getting subjects in focus, the Mini Evo 2 also gives you much more creative control. It offers 10 lens effects, including vignetting, light leaks, soft focus and the aforementioned wide-angle mode. You can also choose from 10 film effects, which change the colors in your photos. With those two dials alone, you can get 100 different looks. 

A selection of the lens effects available on the Mini Evo 2. Abby Ferguson

Not all of those looks appealed to me, but my favorites were the double exposure lens effect and the monochrome film effect, both of which I found myself using a lot. I have used double (and more) exposures in my conceptual and fine art work for a long time, and I could see myself utilizing this camera to continue that work in a different way. 

I really enjoyed creating monochrome double exposures. Abby Ferguson

The camera also offers a new and slightly perplexing “Expanded View.” When toggled on, you’ll see a framing guide with a view of the scene beyond that. Fujifilm says this is to help you “compose with greater intention.” Interestingly, though, switching it off gives the normal lens effect mode the same field of view as the wide-angle effect.

What I appreciated even more than the creative modes, were the manual controls. You can turn the flash on or off (or leave it on auto), and you can choose from standard white balance presets. The camera also features exposure compensation (from -2 to +2 EV), so if it’s not exposing how you would like, you have the option to lighten or darken the image before pressing the shutter. 

The botton on the front of the camera, near the flash, is a second shutter button, making it easier to take vertical photos and selfies. Abby Ferguson

The camera also offers a self-timer, a cold shoe and a tripod mount. You can even edit photos on the camera before printing them, which I appreciated since I found myself wanting to adjust the look of prints pretty frequently (more on that later). 

Immersive Movie was the one feature I didn’t find much use for. Saving a few seconds of movement alongside a photo isn’t something that appeals to me on my phone, and it didn’t add much to the experience here, either. I tried it a few times, but was much more interested in making still images and prints.

The printing experience

Abby Ferguson

One of the things I love about instant cameras is being able to photograph someone and immediately hand them a print. But with a traditional instant camera, giving that print away means I no longer have a copy. Sometimes that’s fine; other times, particularly during a more formal shoot, I want one for myself. The Mini Evo 2 gives me the best of both worlds without actually having to take two pictures, which won’t be exactly the same.

I first used the camera during a shoot for a personal series of documenting artists in their studios. Those images eventually go on my website, so I want to have copies of them. However, it’s a nice little treat for me to be able to hand something physical to the artist at the end of the shoot, too. Because of the hybrid format of the Mini Evo 2, I could print images for the artist on the spot, then make another set for myself to scan and share on my website, or even just share the digital files.

Abby Ferguson

Additionally, even the smaller format Instax Mini film isn’t exactly cheap, and being able to review an image before printing it is a nice benefit. This is especially helpful for the double exposure mode, since I was able to verify if the image actually looked interesting before deciding to print it. Sure, it takes away some of the spontaneity and guesswork that I really enjoy about analog photography, but I still get a physical photo a few moments after I take it, if I want to. And if it’s bad, I don’t need to waste roughly $1 on a piece of film.. 

The images print very quickly from the Mini Evo 2, and you get to watch a digital version of the film make its way out of the camera on the large display. It’s a small detail that doesn’t have any practical implications, but I find so much joy in it that it makes printing photos even better. The images still take the standard 90 seconds to fully develop, so while they come out quickly, you’ll need to wait a little bit to see the actual image. 

Abby Ferguson

While there’s a lot to love about printing images with the Mini Evo 2, there are some downsides, too. First, the prints I made had noticeably less contrast and less vibrant colors than the images on the display. This was especially disappointing with black-and-white images, since monochrome was one of my favorite effects to use. They looked crisp with just the right amount of contrast on the display, but the prints came out a bit flat with varying tints.

My other complaint about the camera’s physical images is that they are tiny. The Instax Mini film is certainly the most approachable in terms of price, but I am not a fan of its diminutive size. As someone who likes looking at photos and seeing at least some detail, I would enjoy a larger film size much more. Of course, that would require a much larger camera body, so there would be a trade-off. 

The digital experience

Abby Ferguson

The hybrid format has more benefits, too. Most notably, if you run out of film while you’re out taking photos, it doesn’t mean you have to stop. You can simply keep on shooting away and print later, or stick to digital-only files. It also means that you can share those digital files without needing to take a photo of or scan the print. And, if you don’t want that white Instax border, you aren’t left cropping an already tiny image. 

In light of that, I’d be remiss if I didn’t touch on the digital image quality. While Fujifilm did increase the sensor size in this camera compared to its predecessor, it’s still a small, Type 1/2.77 (20.6mm²) sensor. By default, the camera produces 6.1MP files. However, resolution gets a bit complicated, as it depends on both the framing settings and where you save the files.

Abby Ferguson

With a microSD card inserted, selecting the wide-angle effect or switching off the “Expanded View” setting, which uses a wider field-of-view, produces 3100 x 4128-pixel images (approximately 12.8MP). With Expanded View on, the other lens effects produce 2144 x 2860-pixel images (approximately 6.1MP). Additionally, images saved to internal memory are all limited to 6.1MP regardless of those settings.

Most of my images were the higher-resolution files, and I was actually pretty pleased with the digital image quality. I wouldn’t want to blow them up into giant prints, but they hold up well beyond the tiny Instax Mini film format. I’d happily share them on social media or use them on my website as part of the aforementioned artist series. There’s a fair amount of detail captured, especially when there’s plenty of light. The dynamic range isn’t outstanding, with plenty of clipped highlights, but I wouldn’t expect much more than that given the sensor size and the jpg format.  

Nearly the sweet spot for an instant camera

Abby Ferguson

Despite my reservations, I had a blast using the Mini Evo 2. That’s not to say that it’s perfect. The prints’ lower contrast was a letdown after seeing the images on the large, vibrant touchscreen. And the tiny size of the prints doesn’t make as much of an impact as larger film formats. However, being able to review images before printing didn’t take away as much of the joy as I’d expected. Instead, it gave me room to experiment without committing a sheet of film to every attempt. It even seemed to increase my enjoyment and make me want to just play, which I always appreciate.

All of the above features add up to a camera that gives you lots of control and provides plenty of room for creativity. You can keep things completely basic and automatic if you want, but if you want to use the camera for more creative pursuits, you have the flexibility to do so. I still feel like I’ve barely scratched the surface of what you can do with this camera, and as an artist, that’s really exciting.

About the Author: Abby Ferguson holds an MFA in photography and blends a fine-art background with extensive editorial and commercial experience. She built and led an in-house imaging program for a large vacation rental company and has served as an associate editor at Popular Photography. Ford Transit Trail Owners May Get $6,000 Over Rubbing Tires

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A Class Action For Wheel Fitment

The Ford Transit with the Trail trim package was positioned as an outdoorsman’s camper van, and it totally looked the part back then. Indeed, the trim package did end up enticing buyers and leasers who were led to believe that this was the ultimate setup for an adventure van, or so you’d think.

It came with adjusted suspension with a 3.5-inch lift, a 2.5-inch wider track width, and also all the skid plates, tow hooks, and cladding that you may “need” while driving your van out in the wilderness. However, even with the additional lift, Ford failed to check whether the meaty 30.5-inch diameter all-terrain tires would clear the Transit’s wheel arches in all conditions. A class action lawsuit was served, and now Ford has agreed to a settlement.

2023 Ford Transit Trail

Ford

Ford to Pay $6,000 per Customer

The class action lawsuit alleged that the Ford “Transit Trail” failed to provide enough wheel and tire clearance in the wheel wells of the vehicle. In certain driving conditions, the 30.5-inch all-terrain tires would rub against the fender liners while turning, braking, or while loaded up with equipment or people. Even the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) is aware of the situation, issuing a recall in March 2024 due to “insufficient packaging allowance” for the front tires.

It seems like Ford didn’t even test whether the wheels would fit. Remember, the wheels on the van [bus] go up and down, not just round and round. Enthusiasts, whether on the upper or lower end of the spectrum, pay close attention to wheel fitment, off-roaders especially. For Ford to miss this but agree to a settlement at least tells us that it’s owning up to its mistake.

Mind you, Transit customers paid Ford $12,500 to get the Trail trim, which is no small amount. Ford is no stranger to off-roaders, and products like the Bronco and any one of the Raptors will tell you that you can trust the Blue Oval to deliver on a trail package. Apparently not, I suppose.

2023 Ford Transit Trail. (2023 Ford Transit Trail available Fall 2022. Preproduction model shown.)

Ford

Eligible Parties

Class members include parties that qualify as an eligible original owner or lessee. That means that you must be the first owner of the Ford Transit Trail, purchased it from an authorized dealer or outfitter/upfitter or other intermediary that acquired the vehicle for resale or conversion. Sadly, parties that bought a used Ford Transit Trail are not included in the settlement, so too are the individuals that have already sued Ford for the same issue.

That being said, if you purchased or leased a Ford Transit Trail before June 1, 2024, as an original owner or lessee, but no longer own the vehicle, you may still be included in the settlement class.

Settlement class members who submit a valid and timely claim form with documentation and proof of ownership or lease can receive a $6,000 payment per vehicle. That’s almost half the value of the package, and probably enough to get something custom done to address the wheel clearance issues.

To process the claim, you can head on over to the class settlement’s website and submit the needed info and documents on or before January 15, 2027. Eligible class members will be paid via check once the court determines whether to grant final approval to the Ford Transit Trail class action settlement following a hearing on March 16, 2027. After this, settlement money will be made available after the approval and any appeals have been resolved.
