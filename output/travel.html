On October 31, 2026, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s Climate Prediction Center updated its monthly outlook maps for November. The fresh data provides a major update on which U.S. states face heightened odds for heavy snow and high-impact winter weather in the coming month.

Which U.S. States Face the Heaviest Snow and Winter Weather Conditions in November?

Weather map showing temperature probability zones in orange, red, and white across the United States.

Photo credit: National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration/NOAA.gov

NOAA has just updated its monthly outlook maps for November. As reports on weather conditions for the coming month have been forecasting for weeks now, the map points to a high likelihood that most American states will face above-normal temperatures in November 2026. That may make some observers assume that heavy snow will not be a major factor in the coming weeks, but that certainly isn’t true.

As the November Temperature Outlook map shows, a significant portion of the country still has an equal chance of seeing above-, below-, or normal temperatures according to NOAA. That includes large areas in Southeast California, South Nevada, South Utah, South Colorado, Southeast Arkansas, East Louisiana, most of Oklahoma, and all of Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas. Simultaneously, all of those regions fall under areas in the November Precipitation Outlook that have a 33% to 90% chance of experiencing above-average precipitation.

With high probabilities of wetter-than-average conditions and equal chances of above-normal, normal, or below-normal temperatures, the parts of those states in the white equal-chance zone could face heavier snow than normal in November. That is especially true in high-altitude zones across Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, Colorado, Nevada, and Eastern California, where snowpack can accumulate quickly.

In addition to those places that are included in the equal chances area on the Monthly Temperature Outlook, there are other areas of the United States that will still almost certainly see heavy snow. That holds true even if it is warmer there than average. Those areas include several northern and high-latitude regions where temperatures are normally so cold that even when it is warmer than typical, precipitation will still freeze. Obvious examples of that include the snow belt, the Northern Rockies, the Cascade Range of Washington and Oregon, and the Alaska Range.

November Is Going to Be Very Wet Across Most of America

Photo credit: National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration/NOAA.gov

Similar to how the Temperature Outlook map is dominated by above-normal areas, the same holds true for the Precipitation Outlook map. In warmer states like Florida, Texas, and Louisiana, NOAA’s forecast indicates that heavy rains are likely approaching, while other areas face a mix of rain and snow. On the other hand, there are areas in the Northeast and Northwest that are facing either equal chances or even below-normal levels of precipitation this month.

The big-racked, muscled-up brute crashes through cover. Operating like a pedigreed cutting horse, he carves out two rival bucks and sends them packing for distant hills. Quickly, he returns to the small patch of cover where the wide-eyed estrus doe waits. The pair will likely stay in that obscure piece of real estate for the next 24 to 48 hours. That’s well away from most other deer — and hunters, who are focusing on better-looking ground with better traditional habitat hundreds of yards away. But it doesn’t have to be that way.

While traditional rut stands work just fine during the peak rut, other unorthodox spots can, too. Hunters should be aware of both sides of that coin. The rut-hunting playbook isn’t as small as some might believe. That’s why I’ve ranked the best 15 spots for peak-rut deer hunting.

1. Funnels and Pinch Points

Funnels and pinch-points are among the most orthodox stand locations during the rut. These are simply natural terrain features that encourage deer to travel through confined spaces. Of course, these are crucial for bowhunters, but they serve as excellent stand locations for gun hunters, too.

Because they’re so effective, however, funnels and pinch-points draw attention from a lot of hunters. On public land and shared private properties, such spots can become pressured and overhunted. In these scenarios, study a parcel’s pressure levels to determine if these spots are worth your time.

2. Doe Bedding Area Fringes

Does usually use the same, or the same few, bedding areas throughout the year. Over time, bucks learn where these locations are. During the rut, they’ll visit these in search of estrus does. Hunters should know where these are located, too.

Hunting doe bedding can be tricky, though. As with hunting any bedding area, you must get in early — long before daylight. Settle in on the downwind side of bedding areas and intercept bucks cruising these edges as they scent check for does. If multiple bedding areas are connected by cover, set up along these routes as bucks travel from one to the next.

3. Upper-Third Ridge Benches

Topography plays a major role in deer movement. Specific features can influence how, when, where, and why deer traverse certain routes. One such feature is an upper-third bench. This is a bench that, as it sounds like, spans the higher part of a ridge — but not the top of the ridge itself. Because morning thermals rise, this is a common area to catch bucks cruising the first few hours of the day. They can smell potential does down below. Furthermore, bucks often prefer the leeward side of the ridge because they can smell from both directions.

4. Lower-Third Ridge Benches

Similarly, lower-third benches are important, too. These are more effective hunting locations later in the day. In the evening, and sometimes even earlier in the afternoon, thermals fall. As the evening air pulls downward, scent collects and pools in the lowest points of the landscape. That’s why bucks travel along lower-level benches to detect potential does that are traveling, or have recently traveled, along the ridge above.

Don’t be afraid to still-hunt along the downwind sides of peak-rut hotspots. Photo by Honeycutt Creative

5. Leeward Ridge Lines

Now, back to those leeward ridges I mentioned above. These are simply the downwind sides of ridges, and they’re excellent locations to intercept deer travel patterns. These serve as great bedding areas as well as travel routes for deer, which mean they’re especially great stand locations during peak rut. Whether deer are bedding there, or merely using these as travel routes, hunters can intercept them.

6. Saddles in the Hills

Another hill-country feature to hunt is the famous saddle, or a low spot between two ridges. It might be where the same continuous ridge dips down and rises back up. Or it might be where two ridges running in different directions taper and meet. Either way, a saddle allows deer to pass through from one side or the other without having to climb up and over. It’s a smart spot to lie in wait for when they do.

7. Overgrown Fields

Throughout the whitetail range, early successional habitat is vital to deer. Overgrown fields can provide that necessary edge cover. Oftentimes, these fill up with briars, brambles, grasses, and even forbs. Such growth might not look like much to us, but it provides good bedding cover and food sources. During the rut, bucks might even push estrus does into such areas to get them away from rival bucks.

8. Water Sources

Deer drink water year-round, but bucks get especially thirsty during the rut. All the cruising and chasing develops a need to rehydrate, and they frequent watering holes. Isolated water holes are especially attractive. Deer have limited options for finding water, particularly in dry conditions, and that increases the odds of shooting deer over one. You can hunt natural water sources or create your own, although creating these in huntable locations can require some serious strategy.

9. Quality Field-Based Food Sources

Deer spend most of their lives within cover, and that’s the best place to find a big deer. But during the rut, don’t overlook the power of a quality field-based food source. Standing soybeans, freshly cut corn, or any other crop with waist grain on the ground can be great. So can food plots, especially those planted with timely species. Hunting deer over food sources that are directly adjacent to quality bedding cover can produce sightings of a lot of deer, including mature bucks that are checking does or replenishing their energy.

10. Buck Bedding Areas

Buck bedding areas are incredibly productive spots throughout the early season, pre-rut, and late season. Don’t overlook these areas during the rut. Often really old bucks will continue bedding in the same areas they have been using all fall, and only search for does at night. If they don’t find a hot doe, they retreat to the bedding areas they feel safest in.

So, continue to monitor the edges of these areas with trail cameras, and move in to hunt as necessary if you think a buck is still bedding there. Keep in mind that bucks and does often bed within the same block, but bucks tend to bed at higher elevations or in areas that are more advantageous.

The author shot this Missouri buck along the edge of a food source with adjacent bedding. Photo by John DePalma

11. Scrape Lines

I know, scrapes, go cold during the peak, rut, right? Sure, scraping activity drops off. But they don’t completely shut down. Bucks continue to check scrapes, and so do does. Does, and bucks that are between does, continue checking scrapes. So it doesn’t hurt to consider their prcense as a secondary or tertiary element when weighing strand locations, especially if scrapes are nearby an area you already plan to hunt.

12. Random Brushy Cover

Bucks don’t like competition. Because of this, older deer that know what they’re doing tend to push does into random areas the remainder of the deer herd rarely uses. Often this includes random brushy areas. Maybe it’s a fencerow, thick sinkhole, patch of grass, or other obscure — but maybe visually obvious — location.

13. Small Pockets of Cover

Similarly, other small pockets of cover can be good, too. Even a half-acre, or even quarter-acre of cover can hold a breeding pair of deer. If it looks like a spot at least two deer could hide from other deer, and hunters, they might just be there. It might feel weird or unproductive to hunt these spots, but if you have good reason to, it just might pay off. Perhaps you saw a buck run into such a spot, maybe you didn’t. But during gun seasons, you should at least glass these areas or consider doing a deer drive through  these small pockets.

14. Drainage Ditches

Drainage ditches are always overlooked. After all, who wants to hunt a hole in the ground? But drainage ditches with the right characteristics can hold deer. At the very least, deer might travel across or through it. Ditch crossings can be good all season long, but during the rut, it’s not uncommon for bucks to push does into brushy areas within ditches.

15. Brush Piles

This is another area you wouldn’t expect a deer, but one worth considering. Brush piles with enough cover can hold a couple deer, and breeding pairs often shack up in such spots. Finding ways to hunt these can be difficult, but don’t overlook potential activity in such areas.

The fight over border construction in Big Bend National Park has reached the U.S. Supreme Court after the Trump administration asked justices to override a federal judge who had ordered work to halt.

On October 29, plaintiffs in an ongoing lawsuit over the border construction, including the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and a local Rio Grande river guide, urged the justices to keep construction on hold. They warned in a 50-page filing that roads and barriers could permanently scar the fragile Chihuahuan Desert ecosystem surrounding Big Bend National Park, threaten river access and local livelihoods, and damage historic sites. The document cited earlier testimony from local residents, environmental researchers, and other officials who oppose the construction.

An excavator and bulldozer sit near Santa Elena Canyon in Big Bend National Park, Texas, on August 13, 2026. The Trump administration is asking the Supreme Court to allow border construction to resume after a federal judge halted the work. (Photo: FangXiaNuo/Getty Images)

“If this actually occurred, I think it would destroy the tourism that Terlingua, Presidio, Marfa… all those towns depend on,” Billy Miller, a local rafting guide who takes tourists on sections of the Rio Grande river, said in the document. “I don’t think they’ll have an experience anymore. I think it will be a tour of the military-industrial complex, not a tour of the wilderness.”

The Supreme Court’s decision could determine whether construction resumes across a vast stretch of West Texas that includes Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park, and protected stretches of the Rio Grande.

The construction plans vary across the region. Inside Big Bend National Park, Customs and Border Protection has proposed access roads, surveillance technology, and roughly 17 miles of smaller vehicle barriers designed to stop cars and trucks from crossing. Elsewhere in the Big Bend Border Patrol sector, the government plans to build 30-foot walls.

Security Claims Meet Judicial Pushback

The question at the heart of the lawsuit is whether the government can bypass environmental reviews to build in Big Bend.

U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone halted construction on October 2. She noted that Border Patrol had recorded more than 89,000 apprehensions across the 517-mile sector over five years, yet Big Bend had the lowest apprehension rate per mile of any sector along the southern border.

Towering 1,500-foot cliffs, the Chisos Mountains, and waterless desert expanses make travel through Big Bend exceptionally punishing, leading Customs and Border Protection to acknowledge in May that the topography of the area naturally deters foot traffic across the border.

On October 21, the Trump administration pushed back, arguing in a filing sent to the Supreme Court that delaying construction imperils national security.

“The Sinaloa and Juarez cartels operate south of this sector across the Mexico border and seek to exploit any vulnerabilities in United States border protection to traffic humans, drugs, and weapons,” Justice Department attorneys wrote in the filing, saying that leaving a “300-plus-mile gap in border defenses” could attract criminal organizations.

In an October 30 reply, the administration maintained that the judge lacked authority to halt construction and reiterated that the injunction threatened border security.

Centuries of Impact on Land and Lives

Opponents argue that allowing work to resume before the lawsuit is resolved could cause irreversible environmental damage.

Jeffrey Renfrow, an ecologist and director of Rio Bravo Restoration who has worked in the Big Bend region for more than two decades, testified that the damage already done could outlive generations.

“My opinion is that there’s been significant harm to hydrologic function, vegetation and soils and that that harm will probably persist for decades if not centuries, and, in some cases, never recover,” Renfrow told the court.

Bulldozers and other heavy machinery can crush biological soil crusts, delicate living mats that bind the desert floor together and prevent erosion. According to a U.S. Forest Service report, some organisms in these crusts can take around 100 years to regrow.

Local rancher Bill Guerra-Addington testified that construction traffic had already increased wildlife deaths along access roads. He also described the emotional toll of watching the landscape around his home change.

“I’ve cried a river of tears seeing what they’ve done to my home,” he told the court. “I just see this kind of like a dystopian nightmare in my very home, a place that I hold sacred.”

The administration had previously used special legal exemptions to begin construction without completing certain environmental reviews and meeting other legal requirements. Cardone’s order blocked further work under those exemptions.

If the Supreme Court lifts her order, construction could resume while the lawsuit continues. If the justices leave it in place, the government must comply with applicable laws before restarting the disputed work.

Let this sink in: More than 92 percent of Québec is public land. Come winter, those millions of acres of woods, trails, and open glades get absolutely buried in snow, turning the province into a sprawling winter playground where opportunities for ski touring, Nordic skiing, and multiday snowshoeing abound.

All that wild landscape would be nothing without a trail system to match—which is why it’s a darn good thing that groomed trails and skintracks cover the province, courtesy of Québec’s adventure-minded culture and infrastructure. Cozy rustic huts dot the high country, Nordic centers pepper the lowlands, and there’s an extensive network of designated ski-touring areas across the region.

Behind Québec’s extensive network of winter trails and refuges lies a long tradition of winter travel and exploration.

Backcountry skiing in Base de plein air Air-Eau-Bois. (Photo: ©️ Base de plein air Air-Eau-Bois)

Long before winter travel became a recreational pursuit, Indigenous peoples used snowshoes and knowledge passed down through generations to navigate the territory now known as Québec. Today, these snow-covered landscapes offer countless opportunities for exploration, from Nordic skiing and multi-day snowshoeing to backcountry adventures.

Today, Québec boasts 28 national parks and dozens of Nordic and cross-country centers. It also boasts plenty of grassroots backcountry ski areas, each complete with trail maps and designated runs. There’s no avalanche mitigation at these sites—they’re wild at heart—but the effortless parking, signage, and basic infrastructure make them a perfect stepping stone between sidecountry and true backcountry. Always remember to come equipped with the appropriate training, equipment, and avalanche awareness. And if you’d rather explore with a qualified guide, you can find businesses certified through Aventure Écotourisme Québec’s Quality-Safety certification program, which sets recognized standards for risk management, guide training and service quality.

Village-to-village cross country skiing along Les Routes Blanches. (Photo: Laurentides ©️ Gouv.Qc / S. Diotte)

Looking to do a little bit of everything? Try Vallée Bras-du-Nord. This all-season adventure center maintains both groomed cross-country ski trails and designated backcountry terrain. Its varied slopes and jaw-dropping views guarantee an unforgettable outing, while a collection of yurts and cabins make it a true ski-in, ski-out getaway. You won’t find a backcountry experience like it anywhere else in North America.

Enjoy cozy yurts and cabins after a day of skiing or snowshoeing across the province. (Photo: Région de Québec © Gouv.Qc / I. Roberge)

For a more homegrown vibe, head to Sommets du Fjord, a blues-and-blacks zone just beyond the charming village of Petite-Saguenay. The town sits right on the edge of the legendary Saguenay Fjord National Park—yet another ski touring and snowshoeing epicenter—which makes it an ideal multisport base camp.

Want to turn a great day into a great week? Treat yourself to a fully immersive multiday traverse through Québec’s snow-draped winter landscapes. Robust hut systems in national parks like Mont-Tremblant and Monts-Valins make it easy to plan linkups via Nordic ski or snowshoe, and village-to-village trips in the Laurentians like those along Les Routes Blanches offer chances to discover culture and cuisine alongside the stunning landscapes. Simply choose a trail to one or more of the parks’ remote refuges and use them as stopover points to cook, relax, and connect with your adventure buddies—and recharge by a blazing woodstove before hitting the trail again the next day.

Exploring Parc régional de la Chute-à-Bull. (Photo: Parc régional de la Chute-à-Bull ©️ Tourisme Lanaudière / L. Coutu))

The adventure doesn’t end when the skis or snowshoes come off. Beyond the trails, Québec’s welcoming villages, local flavors, and cozy inns offer the perfect setting to unwind and reconnect. Share a well-earned meal, raise a glass of a local wine or brew with your fellow adventurers, and relive the day’s discoveries. Because sometimes, going further isn’t just about the distance you travel, it’s about everything you experience along the way.

If you scan articles on the best rut hunting spots, you won’t find many that highlight buck bedding areas as key locations to target. But often times in deer hunting, going against the grain can produce great results. 

However, you can only hunt buck bedding areas well if you have a firm understanding of how bucks bed, where they bed, and how they learn to survive based on their bedding area choices. 

If you are tired of hunting the same old rut stands with minimal success, consider a change. Hunting a buck bedding area during the rut could be your ticket to hanging an old bruiser on the wall. Here’s what you should know.

Defining Buck Bedding

Let’s start by agreeing on terms. Of course, bedding areas are places where deer rest and sleep. However, it’s important to know there are different types of bedding areas. Deer have daytime and nighttime bedding areas. For this article, we’ll focus on the daytime beds.

On the surface, it can be difficult to determine the difference between buck and doe bedding areas. There are distinctions, though.

Bucks tend to prioritize security over convenience. Does usually take the opposite approach. Bucks rely on themselves to detect danger (because they typically bed alone). However, does usually bed, feed, and travel within family units. When bedding together, does bed down facing different directions. This makes it easier to detect danger from all areas around them. Bucks can’t do this so they are much choosier in their bed locations.

Furthermore, bucks might or might not bed within the same patches of cover as does. When they do, bucks tend to take the best quality bedding. For example, in hill country, that might be the upper third of a ridge line. In swamps, that might be the highest dry ground available. In big woods, it could mean interiors of timber or in spots with the thickest cover and highest stem counts.

Bucks also bed in areas that offer plenty of horizontal cover. They might bed up against a boulder, log, or other object that hides their profile. Oftentimes, from a hunter’s perspective, all you might see is their head, antlers, and maybe the neck. Other times, you’ll see nothing at all (until it’s too late).

Regardless of the habitat type or geographical layout, bucks almost always bed with certain consistencies. For example, they tend to bed with the wind at their back. This allows them to smell anything upwind (behind them) and see anything downwind (in front).

This is one reason leeward ridges are so attractive to deer. The prevailing wind brings scent from over the top of the ridge. Early in the day, thermals bring scent up from below. Oftentimes, this allows deer to smell danger from multiple directions.

Without question, savvy bucks learn how to detect danger within their environment. With time, they learn typical directions that danger comes from. With that knowledge, they bed in areas that offer visibility to see hunters or predators coming from these directions. Therefore, don’t be surprised to see bucks bedding right on the edge of thick cover within some of the areas noted above. This gives them plenty of cover to hide and allows them to see danger approaching from the open areas nearby. 

It’s logical to think that once bucks become confident in certain bedding areas, they’ll go back and use the same beds over and over. In some ways, and in many places, they do. It’s especially common for bucks that live in more desolate landscapes or areas with poorer habitat quality to reuse the same beds with higher frequency.

According to the MSU Deer Lab, in areas with higher timber-to-open ratios and bigger woods settings, bucks are less loyal to specific beds. They will still bed within the same relative geographic locations. However, the absolute locations of their beds tend to vary.

Know That Old Bucks Are Different

You’ll often find buck beds in heavy cover. Photo by Honeycutt Creative

It’s been said many times, but older bucks operate completely differently than younger bucks and does. They take on vastly different habits and tendencies. 

A common thread through most mature deer includes the desire for seclusion. Older bucks don’t want a bunch of other deer bedded close to them. Additionally, it’s important to recognize that higher populations of does, and higher deer densities in general, can cause bucks to bed on the fringes. That’s especially true outside of the rut.

Because of everything noted above, it’s challenging to hunt close to buck bedding. It becomes even harder when the deer is 4.5 years old or older. These deer have learned to survive in part due to their bedding area choices. Therefore, it takes strategic planning and very deliberate approaches to thread the needle and get a big buck on the ground.

So, Should You Hunt Buck Bedding During the Rut?

While it isn’t as popular as other rut-centric stand locations, it’s certainly an option. By continuing to focus on buck bedding areas (similar to early season and pre-rut plans), hunters can find a big ol’ buck.

By digging through deer research and personal experience, it’s clear that bucks express unique habits and tendencies that culminate into perceived personalities. One example is a really old buck that barely participates in the rut. That’s the exception, of course, but it would call for a unique approach. You would have to hunt such a deer as if the rut isn’t even on. You’d focus on travel routes along bed-to-feed patterns.

Some mature bucks that participate in the rut still return to their preferred bedding areas. If they don’t find an estrus doe under cover of darkness, by dawn, they cautiously slink back to one of the advantageous beds they know. When hunting this type of deer, set up between his bed and nearby food sources. Or between his bed and nearby doe bedding.

Hunting this deer is similar to hunting the fringes of buck bedding outside of the rut. Use good entry and exit strategies, and keep wind directions in mind during the approach, hunt, and departure. Don’t be aggressive with calling, rattling, or decoying since reclusive bucks rarely respond well to those aggressive tactics.

Final Thoughts on Hunting Buck Bedding Areas During the Rut

Hunting buck bedding requires a lot of planning and consideration. Hunting buck bedding during the rut requires even more. In many cases, this isn’t the right play. 

Oftentimes, it’s less-dominant, uber-mature deer (5 ½-plus), or specific bucks that tend to be more reclusive across their entire lifespan, that require this hunting tactic.

Once you’ve determined you’re hunting a specific buck like this, you must hunt him carefully. Use the hunting strategies you’d typically use to hunt a whitetail bedding area with the immediacy of the rut in the front of your mind.

On a fall afternoon in Halachó, a small town in the Mexican state of Yucatán, Wilson Alonzo stood before a smoldering mound of dirt and began digging. Slowly clearing away branches, leaves, and soil, the chef unearthed a large disc enclosed in banana leaves. He peeled the wrapping back and examined the golden edges of the round tamale inside. He nodded. It was ready for the altar.

Every October and November, Yucatecans like Alonzo gather at the edges of underground ovens and cook mukbil pollo (“buried chicken”), a sacred tamale made only for the Yucatán Peninsula’s Day of the Dead. Known as Janal Pixán (“Food for the Souls” in Yucatec Maya) or Los Finados, the holiday welcomes returning spirits: children on October 31, adults on November 1, and all spirits on November 2, followed by a farewell feast eight—or in some cases, 30—days later. Above anything else that might grace an ofrenda, which could include anything from hot chocolate to relleno negro to beer, mukbil pollo is the star that occupies every altar’s center.

“It is the absolute highlight of the offering,” Alonzo said. One bite confirms why: The crispy, caramelized crust of lard-enriched masa gives way to a smoky, savory mouthful of chicken bathed in a brothy crimson sauce of achiote, tomato, sweet and spicy peppers, epazote, onions, and garlic.

Sam O’Brien

But Alonzo—whose restaurant, Ya’axche, specializes in traditional Yucatecan dishes—said it’s not mukbil pollo’s flavor that nourishes spirits; instead of eating it, they consume the tamale through its aroma. And a crucial part of that aroma, he added, comes from the píib. Dating back to the ancient Maya, this underground cooking technique uses wood, stones, and leaves to impart a slightly herbaceous, smoky succulence to dishes like mukbil pollo—also known as pibipollo or pib—and cochinita pibil.

“Cooking it in a gas oven simply isn’t the same,” Alonzo said. “You lose the unique aromas and flavors imparted by the earth itself.”

Not everyone sees the píib as essential. In cities, where outdoor space is limited, most tamales are baked indoors. When Steffan Igor Ayora-Díaz, a retired anthropologist, was growing up in the city of Valladolid, his mother made mukbil pollo at home, then brought it to a local bakery to cook in an industrial oven. “Nobody said, ‘Oh, it has to be in a píib,’ ” he explained.

Ayora-Díaz now lives in the Yucatán state capital of Mérida, where he said beliefs surrounding Janal Pixán lean more secular. Not only do most eschew the píib, but he and many of his friends no longer build ofrendas, instead celebrating the holiday by gathering with family over a meal. Mukbil pollo is still important, but it plays a role more analogous to turkey on Thanksgiving. “We’re still going to eat it because that’s the food that you eat on these days, but you’re not really thinking of dead people when you’re eating it,” he said.

While purists decry these nontraditional approaches to the holiday, Ayora-Díaz points out that mukbil pollo is a blend of ancient and modern. “It’s a ‘traditional’ dish, but I’m putting ‘traditional’ in quotes because it’s relatively new,” he said. The Maya have been cooking in píibs and eating tamales for millennia, but ingredients like garlic, onions, and lard arrived with the Spanish conquistadors in the 1500s. And, according to Ayora-Díaz, mukbil pollo only started appearing in written records in the 1900s.

Janal Pixán, too, is a hybrid of sorts, blending Christian rituals for All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day with Maya beliefs surrounding death and ancestor veneration. In Xocén, a small village of largely Yucatec Maya residents about 20 minutes outside Valladolid, honoring the dead can mean not only praying for their souls at Catholic mass but welcoming them into one’s home as guests and offering them the same hospitality as the living.

“We are dealing with spirits who require respect and specific observances, and who actually come to visit us,” Miguel Tun, a Yucatec Maya tour guide, said. “Even the yards look clean during this time. You walk past someone’s house and see that everything is spotless because visitors are coming.”

Tun lives in a modern home next to his parents’ palapa in Xocén. The Tuns, like many Yucatec Maya, make their tamales only for the biix, or eighth day of Janal Pixán, when souls are believed to return to purgatory. In Xocén, the local version of mukbil pollo is perfectly suited for this journey. Called chachak waaj (“red tamale” in Yucatec Maya), it’s like a small hand pie.

“The idea is that the souls can easily carry the food with them,” Tun said. “It’s about seeing them off. It’s like when people travel—say, from abroad—to visit, and we give them a gift.” 

This gift isn’t just the tamale itself but the time spent creating it. I visited the Tuns to learn how to make chachak waaj from scratch. We began in the morning. After shredding chicken, slow-cooking sauce, mixing manteca (rendered pork lard) into masa, assembling each tamale, and wrapping them in banana leaves, we needed headlamps to navigate the píib in the darkness. 

Sam O’Brien

“If you are going to make an offering as part of a spiritual practice, it has to be something into which you have poured your effort, you have sacrificed your time,” he said. “That holds significant meaning within the Maya worldview.”

The hours spent making chachak waaj also provide an opportunity to reminisce about the dead. “It’s a moment where the whole family is together, remembering things while cooking, making preparations, or kneading the dough,” Tun said. “Sometimes the conversation is about what’s happening in the community, but there are moments where someone makes a comment about something the deceased person used to do or say.”

At 9:30 p.m., Tun and his father returned from the backyard píib carrying several trays of tamales. It was time for the final step. The Tuns, several cousins, and an aunt gathered on plastic chairs and tree stumps and started unwrapping. They inspected each crust, making sure it was sufficiently crispy (the ones that weren’t would head back to the píib). As they worked, scattering charred leaves at their feet, they talked about an upcoming dance recital for one of their cousins.

Later Tun told me this is, for him, the most important part of Janal Pixán. “Even though a loved one is gone, you demonstrate strength, you show that the family remains united,” he said. “You want that person, when they arrive, to see everyone together, to see that bond. That’s what matters most. I believe that’s the true essence of the Day of the Dead.”

There was the couple in Texas who kindly housed me but warned about their vicious late-night, door-opening, biting cat, and the time a prominent politician’s son asked me to edit his “fictional novel,” a thinly-veiled story about his father’s real-life exploits. I’d recently met the fabulous writer Stephanie Storey while she was touring her beautiful debut novel Oil and Marble. Stephanie grew up in Arkansas, and I’m pretty sure she told the director of the literary festival, Books in Bloom, about me. I received an invitation to be a featured author at the festival in the spring of 2018. I’d never been to Arkansas and was excited to experience both the town and this celebration of literature. My communication from the director stated I’d be staying at the Crescent, a historic hotel founded in 1866. The town was as adorable and welcoming as advertised, a Victorian village with rumored healing springs, and everyone I met exuded warmth and hospitality. The thriving literary community radiated kindness and engagement.

My room was comfy and oddly shaped, the kind of space that had clearly remained much as it was in the 1800s. The quirks of the layout only added to its charm. It wasn’t until I checked in that day that I noticed a sign proclaiming the hotel to be “America’s Most Haunted Hotel.” I’m not easily spooked, but I do have a healthy curiosity and respect for possibilities.

That evening, after going out for dinner with several other authors from the festival, I took the stairs rather than the elevator to get some exercise as I made my way back to my room. When I reached my floor, I noticed a throng of people blocking the hallway. As I got closer, I realized they were listening with rapt attention to a hotel employee leading them on the nightly ghost tour. And then it hit me: they were standing directly outside my room. The tour guide was calling my room the most haunted room in the hotel.

I looked around, half-expecting someone to jump out and shout, Gotcha! You shoulda seen your face! But no. I eased closer to listen, catching fragments of the guide’s narrative: something about a secret pregnancy, a woman who had mysteriously fallen—or perhaps jumped—from the window of the very room where I’d be sleeping that night. I nestled into the sheets, closed my eyes, and drifted off.

Then, almost immediately, I sensed a cat hopping onto the bed. Now, I know that sounds wacky. And I can’t exactly articulate the sensation. Still, if you’ve ever had a cat, you know that particular feeling—the gentle padding of paws, the slight shifting of the comforter, barely perceptible but unmistakable. This felt so real, so present, that I jumped up and turned on the lamp, sending my reading glasses flying off the nightstand. There was no cat on my bed. I looked underneath, checked the bathroom, searched the corners of the room, all the while calling Here, kitty kitty, my suspension of disbelief tested. Absolutely nothing.

I got back into bed, turned off the light, and lay there, mind racing, acutely aware of every shadow and sound. I thought of my youngest child, a seventh grader, back home in South Carolina. He had a big math test the next day. I turned the lamp back on and grabbed my phone to text him a good luck, good night text. Not eager to get back out of bed to retrieve my glasses, I squinted and pecked out my message.

“Night, buddy. Proud of you. You got this! Love you so much.”

I hit send, turned off the lamp, then, oddly enough, slept soundly. The next morning, I awoke early and absentmindedly picked up my glasses, only to realize, with great embarrassment, that my late-night text had not been to my son Will, but to my longtime OB-GYN, Dr. Robert. Mortified, I typed a quick explanation. I kept it simple: “Oops. Wrong recipient. Meant for my son.” He replied with a crying-laughing emoji. Thank goodness my gynecologist had a sense of humor.

I opened my laptop and prepped for my session at the festival later that morning. Sipping my coffee, curiosity got the better of me. On a whim, I googled “ghost cat of Crescent Hotel, Eureka Springs,” not really expecting to find anything.

Holy moly.

There was, in fact, a ghost cat at the Crescent Hotel—and it had a name: Morris. He was a real orange tabby who had lived at the hotel for twenty-one years, supposedly serving as its unofficial general manager. He had an office with his title on the door and a custom cat-door cut into the lobby so he could move freely through the hotel. When Morris died in 1994, over three hundred people attended his funeral. He was buried on the hotel’s East Lawn with a headstone.

Are you kidding me!

According to what I found, Morris apparently loved his job too much to leave. Guests and staff continued to report his presence—a cat brushing past ankles, faint meows in empty hallways, and, you guessed it: a gentle weight on the bed.

I hadn’t experienced the ghost of a tragic woman in the most haunted room, but I may have experienced Morris.

As I left the hotel that morning to start my day at the festival, I stepped outside and visited Morris’s grave. Standing there, I thought about what it means to be a writer—to remain open to possibilities, to imagine. Morris’s late-night visit had broadened my lens. And as unexpected situations tend to do, the experience made me consider a new what-if. A cat so devoted to his purpose that he transcended death itself to continue his work? There was a story in that. 

There always is, if you’re willing to see it.

Your partner is your person, your safe place, your rock — so when they betray you unexpectedly, it can feel like the ground split open beneath you, and there’s no more safety net. Maybe you’re not sure what to do next. Maybe you’re thinking of ending things, or you want to try and work on it first. Whatever happened and wherever you are, there are certain steps couples take after a betrayal that relationship experts say predict whether or not they’ll move forward together or fall apart.

For starters, a betrayal doesn’t just mean someone cheated. Any breach of trust can feel like betrayal, says Blythe Landry, M.Ed., licensed clinical social worker, author of Trauma Intelligence, and founder of Grief to Purpose. Quitting a job without letting your partner know, buying a car or adopting a pet without discussing it first, or parenting in a way your partner is opposed to behind their back — these are all betrayals. So too are having affairs and endangering your kids, like drinking and driving with them in the car, Landry explains.

Those first six months after a betrayal are when the pain is most acute, Landry says. And following any type of betrayal, each partner is going to face their own unique hurdles.

“Oftentimes, the betrayed partner has difficulty determining if things are safe emotionally, as well as being uncertain of what they can believe about their relationship,” Suzanne Koch Eckenrode, licensed marriage and family therapist at Carlsbad Beach Recovery. “The person responsible for breaking the trust may be experiencing feelings of guilt, shame, or defensiveness. One of the biggest obstacles to healing is the fact that both parties will heal at a different rate. It can be very challenging to begin the process of healing when one feels ready to proceed while the other does not feel ready.”

So, how do you begin to move forward at all? Here’s what the experts advise.

What Couples Who Survive A Betrayal Do In The First Six Months

1. They focus on consistency.

Instead of trying to immediately forgive and move on, couples who stick together put their energy toward being consistent with one another, says Koch Eckenrode. This is what rebuilds trust in the long run.

“The couple establishes clear boundaries, communicates in an open manner when they feel uncomfortable or angry, and takes responsibility without blaming the other person,” she says. “Typically, rebuilding a relationship is not achieved by a single grand action but through many actions, to create enough positive interactions so the two people begin to trust each other again gradually.”

2. They both take accountability for their roles in the relationship.

In most cases, the “perpetrator” has not suddenly turned evil and betrayed their partner. These things don’t happen in a vacuum.

“There is often a history of communication differences and things that are going on that lead one party to do something,” Landry says. “Now, that does not make the other person responsible for their choice to betray. But for example, if someone is constantly criticized and they are afraid to tell the truth because [of it], they may be much more likely to do something that they don’t tell their partner about. So one very positive sign is if both parties can look at their accountability.”

One partner may feel chronically unheard, rejected, or disrespected, which ultimately leads to a betrayal of some kind, Landry says. While it doesn’t excuse them, it does explain the behavior — and it reveals what needs fixing to move forward in your relationship together.

3. The betrayer genuinely strives to understand why they did it.

When a normally good, moral person betrays their partner, typically something inside of them is not OK. Either because they don’t want to or don’t know how to process those feelings, they look for something external to help. Maybe it’s an affair partner because their spouse no longer seems sexually interested in them. Maybe it’s drinking too much because it takes the edge off the bad feelings. But their commitment to understanding their own behavior plays a major role in healing and rebuilding trust.

And, importantly, the other partner is patient while they suss out what led them to stray.

“It’s always easy to see when it’s not you,” Landry says. “That’s what I would say to anyone going to couples counseling or even going to a pastor or whoever you go to for help. When it’s inside of you, it’s so evasive sometimes.”

4. They both commit to working on the relationship, even if it seems like only one partner is the problem.

Thinking back to her own clients, Landry says the couples in which both partners were genuinely invested in fixing things — those were the ones who repaired their relationships. That meant both showing up for counseling, both taking accountability, and both being vulnerable throughout the process.

5. They communicate, communicate, communicate until that becomes the norm.

Let’s say one partner travels often for work, and has also used that time to gamble away large sums of money in secret before. Short of quitting that job and never traveling again, how can the couple handle this scenario in a way that rebuilds trust?

“Discuss your travels beforehand and establish reasonable times for checking in with one another, then immediately inform each other of any changes from the plan,” says Koch Eckenrode. “The person doing the travel will be expected to follow through and do as agreed upon without having to constantly be reminded. The purpose here is not to provide some sort of long-term surveillance system; it’s to create an environment where each person feels safe enough that they are transparent about their actions naturally because it has become a part of who they are as opposed to being something they need to do.”

If you had good communication before the betrayal, that’s usually the biggest indicator that you two will successfully get through this as a couple, Landry says.

6. They hold each other in high regard, even when it’s hard.

This has more to do with your personalities than the relationship itself, Landry notes, but the ability to see each other in a positive light will get you far. When the betrayed party has the capacity to look at their partner and say, “This is a good person who did a bad thing,” that’s a really good sign.

Of course, there are those people who don’t deserve this level of grace. “There’s a difference between giving someone another chance and holding them in high regard and letting someone abuse you. Again, it’s impossible to see yourself and your behaviors clearly all the time, Landry says. This is where the support of a therapist, family member, religious leader, or other non-biased third party can be helpful.

The best way to survive a winter storm is to not get caught in one. True as that may be, however, it’s not a very useful tip when the snow has already started falling. As weather grows more unpredictable, getting stuck on the trail, even when you’ve dutifully checked the forecasts, could happen to any of us. The key to surviving is always being prepared for the worst by packing the 10 essentials and reading articles like this, even if you’re just planning to go on a day hike.

“If you ask me what’s the highest risk, it’s those short hikes because they’re the ones most people aren’t prepared for the longer term,” says Dan Wowak of Coalcracker Bushcraft, a wilderness school and YouTube channel based in Pennsylvania. “A two-mile hike can turn into a devastating emergency situation. You roll an ankle, a storm comes in, it gets dark, you have no equipment, and you don’t know what to do.”

We talked to Wowak and two other experienced hikers about what to do when bad weather rolls in with no warning. The first step: They all suggest packing the right things for winter, including an emergency shelter, communication device, extra layers, navigation, and a fire starter.

1. Stop and Evaluate

You might think that if you’re only a mile from the trailhead, you’re home free. But as snow starts to cover the trail and any tracks you may have left, you might as well be days away. “When you’re out and the weather is turning, you might be inclined to go faster,” says Danny Giovale, the founder of Kahtoola. “I would strongly suggest stopping for a sec.”

Knowing when to stop and wait out the storm will save you from precarious situations. Giovale and Wowak suggest considering how far you have left to go and the time of day. If the storm is moving in quickly and it’s getting dark, it’s best to stop and prioritize survival. Nighttime, Wowak says, is one of the worst times to travel because both visibility and temperatures are at their lowest. So if dusk approaches and you’re not prepared with the equipment and knowledge to travel in the dark, settle in to spend the night out there.

2. Set Up—or Find—Shelter

If you packed an emergency bivy or a tent, it’s crucial to set that up immediately to protect yourself from the elements, like snow, rain, and wind. Blocking the weather is going to prolong your survival timeline. “If you’re not dry, you’re in trouble really fast,” says Jon King, who hikes daily and sends the trail report for 10,000-footer San Jacinto Peak in California.

If you don’t have shelter, Giovale suggests looking for a cave or building your own in the snow. This, of course, requires prior knowledge, but the idea is to create a big pile of snow. Let it set up into a dome and then dig out a space for your body. Don’t forget to poke an air vent.

Then there are trails with man-made shelters, like on San Jacinto Peak, says King. Part of your preparation is knowing where these huts and structures are located in case you ever need them.

3. Keep Warm

As your hands grow cold, they become less functional. As your body loses heat, you become at risk for developing hypothermia and making rash decisions. In addition to a shelter, Giovale always brings a lightweight shell jacket with a hood. It’s “one of the ounce-per-ounce most impactful pieces of gear,” he says. Staying as dry as possible with extra layers will keep you warm. Keep exercising and moving to stimulate blood flow to your fingers and toes.

Wowak also recommends building a fire. “Getting a campfire started is a very relaxing and primal thing that makes people feel safe in just about any situation,” he says. Some caveats apply. It’s harder to do in a torrential downpour than in a snowstorm. It’s also not always advised in fire-prone areas or during a fire ban unless it’s a life-or-death situation.

Should you spend the night outside, make sure to insulate your body from the ground so you don’t lose body heat, he adds. A foam mat will work, but in lieu of that, so will pine boughs. In a pinch, you can sit on your backpack.

4. Stay Hydrated 

Ration your water reserves to stay hydrated, or use the campfire to melt snow and warm up a drink. Drinking cold water can drain your energy, Wowak says, so warm water is better.

Unless you brought snacks, you might start to feel hunger pangs. Hydration can help there, too. “,Even if we don’t have a lot of food, if we were only planning on a day hike and a storm comes in, and we’re stuck there, being hydrated will trick your body and you won’t feel as hungry because you’re full of fluid,” Wowak says.

5. Stay Positive

The worst thing you can do in any kind of survival scenario is dwell on the situation, Wowak says. Keep your mind engaged with survival tasks like making your camp bed, boiling water, organizing your gear, moving your toes and fingers, etcetera.

Giovale says to trust your prior training to get you through the storm.

“My experience with being in serious situations outside is that whatever you have learned or been exposed to, you’ll probably remember,” he says.

Wind and wet keep them on the roost. Hunt returning birds in the last 45 minutes of light. Don't write off pressured public land after opening day.

  • Hunt roost cover in the last 45 minutes of light as birds return from grainfields.
  • On bitter cold mornings, pheasants go to grain fields early. Hunt food, not roosts.
  • Wind and wet keep birds on the roost. Hunt heavy cover when conditions are nasty.
  • Public land rebounds after nearby private corn is picked. Watch agricultural activity.
  • Close-flushing roosters are harder to shoot than they look. Be ready to execute.

“We’ll flush about 150 birds today,” I told my new friend M.D. as we loaded our guns by his truck. A recent transplant to pheasant country, M.D. raptly surveyed the wide fields, trying to picture 150 flying pheasants where he could see only a wide expanse of snow. “But only one of them will get up within gun range,” I predicted. “Don’t miss.”

The pheasants looked like tiny black checkmarks against the whiteness as they scooted in and out under the sparse cover of bushes and brushpiles. With the long grasses buried under the snow, the birds had nowhere to hide. They flushed in large, nervous flocks before we could close within 100 yards. Finally, we found the one bird we were looking for, holding tight in a lone patch of grass tufting up through the white crust.

M.D. missed, proving that when it comes to pheasants, knowledge is power only if you shoot straight. Muffing deceptively difficult shots at close-flushing roosters is, perhaps, the most common error pheasant hunters make. But it’s not the only one. Here are ten lessons I’ve learned the hard way.

1. Don’t hunt pheasants in the wrong place at the wrong time.

Pheasants roost in thick, heavy grasses, then walk or fly out to grainfields to feed in the morning. At midday, they hang around in light cover near food, then hit the fields again in the afternoon. Late in the day, birds will return to heavy roosting cover.

Many people believe pheasants linger on the roost on freezing cold winter days. Over the years, I wasted a lot of time getting up early on subzero mornings trying to kick-start sleepy pheasants out of their beds. In fact, when it’s 20 below zero and days are short, pheasants head for the fields at first light to load up on high-energy grain, so hunting their bedroom on a bitter cold morning is a waste of time. A nasty, windy, wet morning is a different story: Hunt the roost early when it’s spitting and blowing, and you may well catch pheasants reluctant to leave their warm, weedy roosts.

By the same token, hunting good roosting cover at midday when the birds are still feeding yields poor results. Be there in the last 45 minutes of daylight, however, as pheasants return from the grainfields, and the hunting can be absolutely spectacular.

2. Don’t assume all public lands were “shot out” after opening day.

Early in the season, when hunter after hunter tramples public land, birds move out to nearby private acres where the pressure isn’t as intense. Gunwise birds sometimes use the public area as a bedroom, returning around dark to roost, then leaving at the crack of dawn.

Hunt a public area the day the corn is picked on the private land across the road, and you’ll be amazed. Last fall, I walked a small, heavily used county area on a whim, thinking I’d give the dog a good run if nothing else. I didn’t know then that the corn had been picked across the road the day before, and without a roof of cornstalks over their heads, the pheasants had retreated in confusion to the marsh grasses of the public land. What I thought would be a quiet dog walk turned into one of the noisiest hunts of the year. Late in the season, when heavy snows have flattened sparser cover on private lands, public areas (especially wetlands) often hold birds again.

3. Don’t go over- or under-gunned.

A pair of winter roosters taken by the author with a 12-gauge Blaser F16 over/under. (Photo/Phil Bourjaily)

Pheasants don’t follow those neat rules of behavior printed on ammo boxes: “early season/close flushes” or “late season/wild flushes.” I’ve seen opening weekends where the wind blew and birds turned skittish, offering no shots closer than 35 yards. And wild-flushing late-season pheasants usually jump up a good 100 yards out of gun range; the birds you can actually shoot are the few that sit tight until you’re right on top of them.

Rather than guessing day by day what the birds will do, I shoot the same Improved Cylinder/Modified-choked 12-gauge double, or Modified-choked 12-gauge pump, from day one to season’s end.

4. Always block the exits where pheasants will escape.

This is one of the errors I commit regularly; I’m usually too eager to start chasing birds to wait while a blocker moves into position at the far end of a field. Every once in a while I hunt with someone who firmly believes in blocking, and I’m always impressed at how well it works. If you hunt any kind of strip cover, pheasants will run to the far end and flush. When pheasants know someone is watching the back door, they don’t run or flush wild; they hunker down and take their chances with the hunters working the cover. One solo hunter I know actually walks noisily to one end of a field and pretends to stand there blocking, then sneaks around to the other side and hunts towards, well, himself.

5. Know when to be quiet.

A slammed car door or a slide-action smacking shut can put pheasants to flight before the hunt even begins. Sometimes you’ll be treated to the impressive sight of pheasant flocks taking off in sympathetic waves across a field, each mass flush triggering the next, all the birds leaving the field you’d intended to walk.

Always approach pheasant cover quietly, especially late in the season when cover is sparse, and birds have grouped up. Once actually hunting, however, I’m not a stickler for silence. I believe pheasants can almost always hear you coming, no matter how quiet you try to be. They’re out there in the grass, monitoring your approach by the crunch of your footsteps and deciding to sit, run, or fly based on exactly where everyone in your party is at all times. Pause every once in a while; when ringnecks suddenly can’t hear you coming, they sometimes lose their nerve and flush.

6. Don’t overdress for pheasant hunting.

Too many pheasant hunters bundle up in heavy clothes, then overheat after an hour of chasing the dogs chasing the birds. You want to feel a little chilly while you’re standing by the truck getting ready; you’ll warm up soon enough.

Pheasant hunting rarely requires heavy boots or brush pants. It’s better to wear something lightweight and easy to move around in. Wear several lighter, thinner layers up top rather than a heavy coat. You can add and subtract layers as you cool down and warm up, tucking unworn clothes into your gamebag. I choose a lightweight ballcap and keep a warm knit cap in my pocket should the weather turn colder.

Any color hunting clothes are fine, so long as they’re fluorescent orange. I glow like a Chernobyl pumpkin when pheasant hunting; I want the people I hunt with to do the same. Besides preventing accidents, this allows you to quickly verify everyone’s location when a bird’s on the wing and you want to be sure you’re taking a safe shot.

7. Don’t plan a big trip for opening day.

If you travel to hunt pheasants, make your trip two or three weeks after opening day (unless of course you’ve already leased a section of South Dakota pheasant country or married into an Iowa farm family, in which case you don’t need my advice). Too many people think opening weekend, with its uneducated, unhunted birds of the year, is the best time to hunt pheasants. In fact, the weather can be hot, cutting your dog’s endurance and scenting ability. Many years, the fields aren’t harvested by opening day, either. Pheasants will hide among the standing crops where you can’t go after them. If you don’t have a place to hunt lined up long before opening day, you might not find one, and public lands will be very crowded.

Two or three weeks after the season begins, the crops will be in, the weather will be cooler, and the crowds will be gone. There will be lots of birds left, too.

8. Don’t ignore the woods for pheasants.

A rooster takes refuge in a patch of woods off a field edge. (Photo/Juha Suhonen from Pixabay)

We all know pheasants are birds of grasslands and marshes, not timber. Watch a rooster batter upwards through the branches on stubby wings, his long, graceful tail totally useless as a rudder in close quarters, and you’ll see why ringnecks usually avoid the woods.

Nonetheless, pheasants will hide in the woods if they can’t go anywhere else. When heavy snow flattens the grassy fields pheasants prefer, look for birds in the woodlots. The best pheasant woods have creek bottoms, bushes, or brush piles to hide in. Years ago, I read somewhere that when you find pheasants in the woods, they will usually be roosters. I’ve paid attention ever since and found it to be true, although I have no idea why.

9. Don’t shoot at pheasants too soon.

Most pheasants are shot within 15 to 20 yards of the muzzle, and many more are missed at even closer range. Stifle the urge to shoot immediately. At 10 yards, your pattern is the size of a fist, and you don’t really know where the bird is going yet. Three feet of squawking, psychedelic gamebird blowing up at your feet is always startling; if you’re not paying attention, it’s downright scary. If you jump back and shoot in self-defense whenever a pheasant flushes, you’ll miss a lot. Take a second to read the pheasant’s angle, swing the gun at the white neck ring, and shoot. Trapshooting, with its varying, going-away angles, is wonderful training for pheasant-type shooting.

While you’re at it, give up trying to beat your buddies to the shot. Take turns; everyone will shoot better, you’ll lose fewer birds, and those you bag won’t be mangled.

10. Don’t take pheasants for granted.

The real trick to successful pheasant hunting isn’t bagging more birds, but getting more satisfaction from the ones you do shoot. Even those of us who live in the best pheasant country don’t get ’em every day, which is exactly as it should be. When my own focus narrows to encompass nothing more than the tail end of a bird dog in the weeds, I try to stop at the top of a hill, take a breather, look around, and ponder how lucky we are—after having plowed up the prairies and run off so many native birds—to have pheasants around. Think about it that way, and every single bird becomes cause for celebration.

Rut Phase: Immediate Pre-Rut

Daylight movement should be on the rise this week. (Photo/John Hafner Photography)

Buck testosterone levels are close to their peak in much of the whitetail’s range by the end of October, and that pushes many night-lurking bucks into making daylight appearances for the first time, seeking the growing number of does nearing estrus.

The majority of does are still not ready to breed yet during this part of the pre-rut, but many are getting close and bucks are well aware. Keep in mind that weather events can greatly influence the intensity of action you see. Warm weather can throw a wet blanket on things for sure, but a few chilly mornings, or better yet, a full blown cold front can trigger a seeming explosion in daylight buck activity.

Our pick falls on Saturday, a day most hunters don’t have to answer to the bossman and can carefully plan a precise hunt, both morning and evening, that could pay off big. October 31 Morning Hunt Plan: Stake Out a Scrape Line

Stay alert. Bucks should stay active later into your morning hunt on Saturday. (Photo/Hoyt)

Mississippi State University collared-buck research reveals buck activity trends later into daylight in the morning hours, allowing savvy hunters opportunity that evenings do not offer. Once in security cover and bedding areas, bucks do not immediately bed down, especially now. Falling temperatures and rising testosterone extends this activity window as bucks continue their nighttime pre-rut activities of scrape-making, rubbing, and checking local does well into daylight.

Your play on this late October morning is to use your scouting intel to set up on a hot scrape or scrape line in transition cover near a known bedding area. And by “hot” I mean a location where a buck has proven he is willing to visit scrapes repeatedly at or near daylight. If you do not have this information, there’s still time before the weekend for some midday speed-scouting, as long as you’re confident that you can scout specific transition areas without alerting bedding bucks. Entry and exit are key on your morning hunt. I try to plan approaches away from destination food sources and at a 90-degree angle to the buck’s known travel pattern or scrape line to minimize the chance of bumping him or any other deer, because if this play doesn’t work, it could be a great setup for your evening sit or our next best day of the rut as long as deer don’t catch on.  

An alternate setup in hilly terrain that’s a favorite of mine is a ridge-top lookout. October 31 Evening Hunt Plan: Stay Put or Meet the Does for Dinner

If you didn’t seal the deal Halloween morning and aren’t stuck with candy duty for the evening, a good play could be repeating the morning setup for your evening hunt. One of the strengths of hunting these transition areas is that you can get in and out undetected and potentially catch bucks lingering both morning and evening. This is where your visual observations from the A.M., and fresh scouting or trail camera data secured on the way out from your hunt can help your decision. If you do decide to hunt the same location as the morning, get set plenty early. This time of year is when hunters occasionally get those early afternoon trail camera photos which leave them sputtering, “If I’d only been there 15 minutes earlier.”

If you want to change it up for your evening sit, try slipping into an evening food source you’ve been saving for such a time as this. Difficult to hunt in the morning due to the potential for spooking deer, these doe hangouts now become more attractive to bucks than that house on the block giving out full-sized candy bars is to trick-or-treaters. New scrapes have probably shown up along these plot edges, and if a target buck has only been hitting them in night hours, this could be the day his script flips. Take extra time to make a slow, silent approach, and if you think there’ll be deer lingering after dark, arrange for someone in a vehicle to pick you up and clear the field before you leave. This will keep the plot fresh, allow you to check or put up a camera on any new active scrapes for monitoring, and set you up nicely for our next day of the rut just days away.

Bonus Tip: Read a Buck Like a Book

Should you use a buck decoy? Let your target buck’s behavior answer that question. (Photo/John Hafner Photography)

So how aggressively should you be using decoys, rattling, or calling during your pre-rut hunts? It’s a good question, and from my experience it depends on deer in your area, the hunting pressure there, and specific buck personalities. The goal is to incite a reaction, not intimidate. In high-pressure areas, where deer may be call-shy from run-ins with hunters, or in areas with subordinate age classes of bucks, consider a softer approach.

In areas with less pressure and a more mature age class of deer, more aggressive approaches can be used successfully. By far the best way to fire up a buck is to play off his personality. Jared Scheffler of Whitetail Adrenaline has spent decades reading bucks and judging their temperament, allowing this to dictate his approach. And getting a visual is the key. If he sees a buck pushing others around, he knows he can likely antagonize him by mirroring this with aggressive calling, rattling, or even a buck decoy. You can do the same. Here’s what that looks like inside real coaching conversations.

When most people picture “accountability,” they picture a drill sergeant.

Someone standing over you going “What were you thinking? Why did you skip that? Do you even want this?“

(And if there’s no drill sergeant handy, we do a pretty good job of beating ourselves up all on our own.)

Here’s the thing: if being hard on yourself worked, it would have worked by now.

Great accountability isn’t built on being a hard-ass.

It’s built on forward momentum and honest feedback.

The drill sergeant questions YOU. Your motivation. How much you want it.

Real accountability helps you try again quickly.

Then helps you see what you’re doing well and what can improve.

Here’s what that looks like:

Forward momentum

A client of mine had a hectic week. Travel, out of their routine, workouts missed.

The drill sergeant move is “why didn’t you do your workouts?!” at the exact moment they were already struggling.

Here’s what I sent instead:

“Hey! I know this has been a hectic week. Let’s do some push-ups together. Do a set next chance you get and shoot me a . I’ll do one too.”

This got a and a “Thanks for the encouragement! This week has been so hectic my schedule got away from me.”

That win got them back on the routine.

Then we build off of that by looking ahead to the next few days.

“How’s the rest of this week looking for strength training? I went ahead and put together a few variations in case you’re short on time.”

When you’re already struggling, the question isn’t “hey, why didn’t you do that?”

It’s getting the next available win.

Then looking ahead to what’s coming up after that.

Honest feedback

But forward momentum is only the first part of the equation.

The second step is giving honest feedback on both the wins AND the challenges.

That’s my job as a coach.

To be a neutral observer. To point out what’s going well, what’s not, and suggest how we can improve it moving forward.

Based on the actual human in front of me: their unique goals, motivations, and whatever flavor of chaos they have going on in their own life (work, family, travel, illness, injury, etc.)

Here’s a recent example:

“Hey! I reviewed the strength workout on Friday. Solid work all around here. I can see from your form check video that you’re doing a better job staying balanced across your whole foot on the squat.

I also noticed that the middle of the week has been tougher to get consistent workouts in. You’ve hit 2 out of the last 4 midweek workouts. I know work has been crazy with the project deadline you have coming up, so my guess is you’re getting buried and it makes it hard to get to the gym. Am I on the right track? I have some ideas for how we tackle that next week that I’ll drop down below in a video for you.”

This is the honest part. It didn’t go anywhere!

But instead of berating you for missing a workout, the goal is to look at all the evidence and suggest next steps.

Including if the evidence says that something BIG needs to change. It’s all on the table.

Accountability isn’t punishment for what you didn’t do.

It’s helping you decide what you do next.

Here’s how you can try it on your own

If your week went haywire, before you beat yourself up, take this approach:

  • First, get your next win. A set of push-ups. A 10-minute walk. The short version of your workout. Give yourself the . Then ask “how’s the rest of the week looking?” Plan ahead instead of fixating on what you missed.

High chair FAQs

What is a highchair?

A highchair is a seat that elevates a baby during mealtimes. This allows an infant to sit at the table with the rest of the family and engage in the social element of meals. It also provides a designated eating spot, so a baby isn’t crawling around getting food all over the house.

When do you start to feed your baby in a high chair?

You should start introducing your baby to solid foods at about six months of age.

To learn more about the signs that your baby is ready to start solids, look at BellyBelly’s article Babies Start Eating Baby Food | 6 Signs Ready For Solids.

When do you stop using a high chair?

The age at which your older toddler stops using a high chair will depend on various factors. Some toddlers outgrow their seats faster than others. Others are good at sitting down during meals and can move to a regular dining seat, with booster seat, sooner. This all depends on your setup, your priorities, and your child. Your toddler will probably stop using a high chair somewhere between the ages of 18 months and five years.

What is the 90 rule for high chairs?

The 90 rule (or 90-90-90 rule, as it is sometimes known) refers to the optimal sitting position for safe eating. Your child should sit with 90 degree angles at hips, knees and ankles. Back support and a foot rest are needed to achieve this position.

What are the pros and cons of using a high chair?

Pros of using a baby high chair:

  • Encourages safe posture for eating
  • Contains the mess at mealtimes
  • Allows your child to be part of family mealtimes
  • Leaves your hands free to eat your own food
  • Promotes independence.

Cons of using a baby high chair:

  • Takes up space in your home
  • Requires cleaning
  • Costs money
  • Can be bulky.

What are the alternatives to using a highchair in 2025?

Alternatives to using a baby high chair include using a booster seat, sitting the baby on your lap, and having your child sit at a toddler chair and table. Booster seats are ideal for taking out and about but they lack the safety and some of the functions of a high chair. Sitting a baby on your lap works occasionally, but it will prove tricky if it’s your regular setup; toddler’s hands in your dinner, anyone?

Although a toddler table and chair might seem like a winning solution, your child will be at a totally different height and won’t be able to be involved in family mealtimes. A baby high chair puts an infant at the heart of the action, allowing the whole family to enjoy the social aspect of meals.

Are high chairs safe?

Yes! A well made high chair is designed with safety in mind. Look for chairs that encourage the 90-90-90 rule position to make sure your child is safely positioned during mealtimes. Choose a chair with a decent harness that will keep your child secure during mealtimes.

Although accidents can happen, using the high chair correctly will reduce the risk of accidents. A 2014 study published in Clinical Pediatrics found that falling is the most common high chair related injury. This can be avoided by using safety straps and harnesses.

What are some high chair accessories to consider?

If it doesn’t have a built-in footrest, make sure you purchase a footrest for the chair so your child can sit in the 90-90-90 position to encourage safe eating. Some highchairs also sell toy accessories to keep your child happy during long mealtimes. It’s also worth investing in suction bowls and plates so your babies can’t immediately tip an entire dinner onto the floor (or their own head).
