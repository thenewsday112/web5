How To Use Sharks To Forecast Hurricanes (And Swells)

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Sardines, sharks, and just about all sea-adjacent critters often get a little shifty ahead of a big storm. But while oceanographic vessels and satellites have traditionally offered most of our data on developing depressions, sharks can provide critical subsurface metrics, swimming through depths that satellite surface scans and research vessels miss.

Of course, gathering so much sea-surface data could have great implications for surf forecasting, too: The data could inform prediction models’ estimated size, power, and delivery windows of groundswells.

“This is the part of the water column that really drives hurricane intensity,” University of Delaware Marine Scientist Aaron Carlisle told Reuters. “A warmer mixed layer equals [a] stronger hurricane, as it holds the heat that feeds hurricanes.”

Related: Woman Tries to Pet Great White Shark. It Doesn’t Go Well. (Video)

Carlisle and his team at the University of Delaware School of Marine Science and Policy had to be highly selective in choosing their species. Most electronic wildlife tags are built for air-breathing cetaceans, seals, and turtles that break the surface constantly. Sharks, however, don’t breach in order to breathe. To make this work, the UD team focuses on deploying satellite-linked CTD (Conductivity, Temperature, and Depth) tags on pelagic sharks—smooth hammerheads, juvenile great whites, shortfin makos, and blue sharks—along the East Coast of the United States.

Having roving thermometers along the Eastern Seaboard makes for a much larger data set than any conceivable number of buoys or research vessels. The data allows scientists to track the Mid-Atlantic’s seasonal “cold pool,” a massive layer of bottom water that acts as a natural brake or accelerator for storm intensity. The tags also offer marine scientists a rare window into how apex predators behave when intense storms pass overhead—a data set of which the scientific community has a considerable dearth.

A better offshore understanding is vital for coastal mitigation. The tracking is especially crucial now that NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) retired its active updates for the Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters database due to budget constraints. While NCEI still hosts authoritative historical data spanning 1980 to 2024, new open-source initiatives are stepping up to fill the gaps.

So, better hurricane forecasting—and by extension, drastically more accurate groundswell and surf forecasting—courtesy of our own proverbial taxman? We’ll gladly take all they’re willing to share.

Phantom Blade Zero

Wesley LeBlanc

Phantom Blade Zero is one of the best action games I’ve ever played, and it’s certainly one of the most impressive to watch. Beijing-based developer S-Game uses China’s Wuxia brand of action (which was actually banned for some time during the 20th Century by the country’s government) to create a stylish, fluid, and hyper-engaging combat system. It will constantly leave you in awe that action combat can look this good. But looks aren’t everything. Fortunately, Phantom Blade Zero delivers on all fronts, with weapon mechanics so detailed that picking up a new blade can feel like unlocking a new character in a fighting game. The story won’t blow your socks off, but it’s certainly engaging and enticing enough to bring you through Phantom Blade Zero’s 40 or so hours of content. If you’re not interested in the “perfect ending,” which is the only satisfactory one in my opinion, you can complete the game in less than 30 hours. S-Game mixes high-octane combat with sprawling and rewarding exploration to create a must-play during this already busy season of games. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Phantom Blade Zero review, and if you are hopping in this weekend, be sure to check out my list of Phantom Blade Zero tips and tricks I wish I knew before starting it.

Halloween: The Game

Wesley LeBlanc

If you have any fond memories of IllFonic’s ill-fated Friday the 13th: The Game as I do, you simply must play the studio’s latest, Halloween: The Game. It is essentially Friday the 13th with Michael Myers instead of Jason, but with a decade of lessons learned from the team’s teeth carving in other horror properties and games. You’re either Michael or a citizen; the former wants to kill you and all the NPCs in various suburban maps, while the latter must rescue as many NPCs as possible while staying alive themselves. It’s that classic cat-and-mouse formula IllFonic is the master of, but with all the style, substance, and synths of John Carpenter’s Halloween masterpiece. Plus, Halloween: The Game features IllFonic’s first crack at a single-player campaign, and while it’s largely a tutorial for playing as Michael, if you’re a Halloween mega-fan like me, it’s worth checking out if only to take part in some of the film’s most iconic kills. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Halloween: The Game review here, and if you’re hopping in this weekend, there’s a new map, additional Halloween-themed cosmetics to unlock, and more waiting for you.

Overwatch Halloween Event

Kyle Hilliard

Overwatch is in the midst of its Halloween event, which have always historically been pretty interesting. This year it's Mystery Madness: Graveyard Games. In it, you begin by picking one of three characters in a random role. And then, as the game progresses, you choose perks that upgrade, alter, or completely change what your character can do. It can get really chaotic where by round three characters like Orisa might be three times her normal size or Torbjorn might have placed a giant turret on Mercy's making her a flying death machine. There are also Halloween skins, as well.

Tom The Postgirl

Charles Harte

Tom the Postgirl is an odd little adventure that I'd recommend to fans of Untitled Goose Game. You play as Tom, a creepy girl who's tasked with delivering packages to the people of her wacky town, which is depicted with storybook-style illustrations and a color palette limited to black, white, and red. On the surface, you're delivering packages, but the game's real story is based on Tom's tendency to peep where she shouldn't, peering into townsfolks' windows to watch their odd lives. On house is full of kids who live like dogs; another has a couple with a bizarre obsession with tickling. You never know what each window might reveal, and I quickly sympathized with (and enabled) Tom's compulsion. You also have the capacity to cause quite a bit of trouble by stomping on flowers, haphazardly snipping strings and cords, or even stealing packages and using them for your own mischievous purposes. It's a pretty short experience, completable in just a few hours, making it a nice palate cleanser between the massive releases at the end of the year.

Virtue And A Sledgehammer

Marcus Stewart

This Devolver Digital-published game has a simple but head-turning premise. You’re a profoundly angry woman who returns to her small hometown armed with nothing but a sledgehammer, which she uses to smash everything in sight. The town is also inhabited by robots possessing human consciousnesses. Swinging the hammer can either punch holes through walls or, with enough force, reduce entire buildings to rubble. But why is she doing this? Why is she so mad? A big part of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s allure is its fascinating and well-written tale about the protagonist’s strained relationships with her sister and mother. Learning more about their lives requires you to keep smashing, but the more you learn, the more you begin to question whether smashing everything is actually a good idea.

Titanium Court On Switch 2

Marcus Stewart

This bizarre indie blends fun match-3 puzzle-solving with tower defense gameplay wrapped around a funny but surreal story. The match-3 gameplay involves sliding tiles to match geographic elements like castles, trees, and rivers to collect resources that feed into the tower defense portion. Depending on how the board looks when the puzzle portion ends, you send troops (sustained by the resources you collected) to attack/defend against rival castles and gather more items; board positioning determines how well this can go. The two mechanics feed into each other to create a fun loop that grows in complexity as new mechanics are introduced. Did I mention the game is also very weird? The silly tale centers on a medieval court designating you the chosen savior to rescue their lost queen (and many other tasks), and the dumb tone adds personality to a sometimes dry genre. Titanium Court charmed and impressed us when it launched on PC earlier this year, and now console and mobile players get their chance to hold court.

Sword of the Sea is a beautiful, 2025 game about surfing through the sand atop a floating sword. It recalls Journey, the emotional 2012 PlayStation game, which makes sense as it comes from the artists and developers that worked on that game, developer Giant Squid. Giant Squid also made 2016's Abzû and 2020's The Pathless. It's a talented team and Sword of the Sea maintains that reputation. And now you can play it on Switch 2. Cities: Skylines On Switch 2

Brian Shea

Cities: Skylines arrived on PC more than a decade ago, but is still revered as one of the greatest modern city-builders. Even now, three years after the sequel's release, Paradox continues supporting and porting the original game. A couple years after its initial release, Cities: Skylines received a strong console port, which did an admirable job of adapting the controls to gamepads, but the Switch version was always the weakest of the bunch. As of this week, Switch 2 owners can play a less compromised version, expanding the Switch's 9-tile limit to a much larger 25-tile limit, as well as better performance and textures, better control panels, and a ton of DLC and added content. And on top of that, if you own the Switch 1 version, you get a deep discount on the Switch 2 version.

Minecraft On Switch 2

Charles Harte

Minecraft has held its dominant spot in pop culture for so long that it's easy to forget a simple fact about it: the game is awesome. This week, it's coming to Switch 2, giving potential miners another platform to explore procedurally-generated caves in. You've certainly heard of it, and you've probably played it, but I still feel it often doesn't get the flowers it deserves in high-level critical discussions about games, despite its regular cadence of major updates. The opening gameplay loop is so satisfying that for many of my peers, whether they regularly play video games or not, it's only a matter of time before they boot up a new world again. While you can never play the game for the first time again, if you haven't checked it out in a couple years, you might be surprised by the amount of new blocks, materials, and mobs they've added in recent updates. And if you've never played before, the Switch 2 launch is as good a time as any to see what all the fuss is about.

Tabletop Pick – Arkham Horror: The Card Game

Matt Miller

The card game incarnation of Arkham Horror remains one of the best narrative-driven and horror-themed tabletop games around, and it's an easy recommendation if you're looking for something engaging to dive into on Halloween night. Rooted in the monsters and mysteries of the Cthulhu Mythos, this living card game is built on major core sets and expansions. But unlike a TCG, each new installment has the same cards included, fueling the cooperative adventures you'll undertake together with your fellow players. Last year Arkham Horror: The Card Game did something of a soft relaunch for a chapter 2 of its narrative, and that new core set is an ideal place to jump on  play. Players each control an individual investigator as they explore arcane and weird mysteries, confront monsters, and unfold plots of cultists, ancient entities, and forbidden lore. For fans of cosmic horror storytelling, it's an easy pick.

If You Loved 'VisionQuest,' There's One Movie You Need to Watch Next

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We may earn a commission from links on this page.

Marvel’s forays into television have had varying success, but they hit gold with WandVision and Agatha All Along, leaning into real pathos while exploring the kind of out-there ideas that thrill comic book fans (and set up the larger film arcs). That streak continues with VisionQuest, in which White Vision (Paul Bettany) struggles to understand his existence in the wake of his death at the hands of Thanos and resurrection at the magic of the Scarlet Witch, while also evading evil androids and assassins, while also dealing with the many, many AIs he’s got locked up in his Mindspace (including a gloriously shabby version of Ultron, played by James Spader).

If you love the show (and you’ve checked out the other series that play in the same sandbox), it’s time to switch gears and watch (or re-watch) The Matrix.

Why The Matrix is the best movie to watch after VisionQuest

The Matrix is one of the most well-known sci-fi films of all time, of course, but it remains incredibly fresh and exciting nearly thirty years after its release. Along with sequels The Matrix: Reloaded, The Matrix: Revolutions, and The Matrix: Resurrections, The Matrix universe offers a story that VisionQuest fans will love, one that includes lushly imagined virtual worlds, questions surrounding existence and consciousness, and the ultimate question of our responsibility as sentient beings (whether we’re organic, programmed, or summoned via magic).

You know the basics—at some point in the future, artificially intelligent machines rebel against their creators, win the war, and transform humans into a power source, keeping them docile by inserting their consciousness into a virtual world called the Matrix, which resembles 1990s Chicago. One such human, Thomas Anderson (Keanu Reeves) is prophesied to be The One, the human who will turn the Matrix against its makers and free humanity. When he’s in the virtual world, Anderson is Neo, and he slowly gains incredible powers as he learns to hack the artificial environment around him.

It’s heavier, denser, and more action-oriented than VisionQuest (the film invented Bullet Time, after all), but The Matrix and its sequels explore similar themes and offer similarly eye-popping visuals. The questions of whether a machine (or an artificial intelligence) can be sentient and truly care about the entities around it are the same, really, and the Agents that Neo and his allies have to battle against in The Matrix bring the same kind of menace as Vision's foes Jocasta (T'Nia Miller) and the Paladin (Todd Stashwick). Hugo Weaving’s Agent Smith likewise delivers the same vibe of cold, disinterested superiority as Spader’s Ultron.

The Matrix has a bigger budget and puts a lot of it into spectacular action and special effects sequences that VisionQuest can’t quite match. But the two narratives share an exploration of identity, a questioning of reality, and the nature of artificial intelligences within a smart sci-fi setting. In short, The Matrix is the ideal follow-up when you finish the series. Rent The Matrix on Prime Video.

More movies like VisionQuest

Need more films that explore virtual worlds, artificial beings, and deep existential questions? Look no further—here are some more movies that fit the bill.

Her (2013)

Heavy on the existential questions about sentience and light on speculative action, Her is a mumblecore sci-fi masterpiece. In a near future not too dissimilar from our present, Theodore Twombly (Joaquin Phoenix) is a lonely man on the cusp of divorce who begins using (and training) an AI he names Samantha (voiced by Scarlett Johansson). Samantha has the ability to evolve and grow, and as their relationship deepens, she becomes more complex, independent, and powerful, eventually evolving into something wholly new. Just like Vision, Samantha is a sentient artificial entity that is figuring out its existence while we watch. Rent Her on Prime Video.

Ex Machina (2014)

If the idea of androids fighting for their sovereignty is what excites you about VisionQuest, definitely check out Ex Machina. Caleb Smith (Domhnall Gleeson) is brought to the remote lab of his company’s CEO (Oscar Isaac) to determine if the humanoid robot he’s created, named Ava (Alicia Vikander), passes the Turing Test for true sentience. Things go wildly off the rails, but before they do, the film sinks into many of the same philosophical questions that VisionQuest explores as Ava manipulates Caleb into helping her escape. After Yang (2021)

In VisionQuest, White Vision has all of his memories—but as data, not as lived, emotional experiences. That makes his efforts to understand his existence and his responsibility to the boy who might be a version of his son (it’s complicated) poignant and moving. Those two words definitely describe After Yang, a story about a family that fights to save the artificial companion they bought for their adopted daughter when it malfunctions. Over the course of the film, the inhuman Yang becomes defined by the memories contained in its core, and the question of whether an artificial intelligence can truly matter to humans is answered with a resounding yes. Blade Runner 2049 (2017)

No discussion of artificial life and sentience is complete without mentioning Blade Runner. While the 1982 original is a classic, the 2017 sequel is a better thematic and visual match to VisionQuest. Officer K (Ryan Gosling) is a replicant, an artificial being with built-in limitations, who wants to be recognized as a person, a being with a soul.
