The DC Universe is getting weird.

The People v. Gorilla Grodd, the bizarre Superman spinoff starring Skyler Gisondo as Jimmy Olsen, has officially wrapped filming and is now moving into post-production.

Collider reported that Gisondo confirmed production had finished, while also teasing that the series takes an approach to the DC Universe that audiences haven’t seen before.

That’s fitting for a show whose premise sounds unlike anything else currently coming out of DC.

Jimmy Olsen Is Investigating Gorilla Grodd

The eight-episode HBO Max comedy is structured like a true-crime documentary, with Jimmy Olsen investigating one of Metropolis’ strangest murder cases.

Gorilla Grodd, the superintelligent ape, has been convicted of murdering his father, the King of Gorilla City. Jimmy isn’t convinced the case is that simple, so he reopens the investigation to determine whether Grodd was actually responsible.

The series comes from Tony Yacenda and Dan Perrault, the creators behind American Vandal, which should give viewers a pretty good idea of the mockumentary style they’re going for. Yacenda is directing all eight episodes.

Gisondo is returning as Jimmy after playing the character in James Gunn’s Superman. Jimmy Tatro will play Gorilla Grodd, while Beck Bennett, Mikaela Hoover and Wendell Pierce are also returning as Steve Lombard, Cat Grant and Perry White, respectively.

It Could Be Very Different From Superman

That’s part of what makes the project so interesting.

Rather than simply giving Jimmy Olsen another traditional superhero adventure, The People v. Gorilla Grodd is being presented as a documentary that exists within the DC Universe itself. The filmmakers have described it as something that should feel like an actual Daily Planet production made for the people of Metropolis.

Gisondo previously told Collider that the mockumentary format was one of the things that attracted him to the project. He said he had never seen anything quite like a true-crime mockumentary set inside a superhero world.

And now that filming is finished, DC fans are one step closer to seeing whether that unusual idea actually works.

When Will The People v. Gorilla Grodd Be Released?

There’s still no exact premiere date for the series.

HBO Max ordered the show straight to series in August, with eight half-hour episodes planned. James Gunn and Peter Safran are executive producers alongside Yacenda and Perrault.

For now, The People v. Gorilla Grodd is simply another sign that Gunn’s DC Universe isn’t afraid to take some very unexpected swings.

A true-crime comedy about Jimmy Olsen investigating whether a superpowered gorilla murdered his own father certainly qualifies.

I made a Rogue Skyborne as my character of choice. Image via Blizzard

President Donald Trump called Norway a “shithouse country” after failing to win the Nobel Peace Prize. Norway’s tourism officials decided to take that as a compliment.

Visit Norway, the Scandinavian country’s tourism agency, responded to Trump’s insult with a cheeky campaign celebrating its spectacular public restrooms, declaring that Norway makes “shithouses great again.”

The campaign features some of the country’s most architecturally impressive toilets, many situated along scenic routes overlooking fjords, mountains and waterfalls. Some have even won international design awards.

Trump delivered the insult last Sunday during a campaign rally in Port Huron, Michigan, while complaining about the Nobel Peace Prize, which was awarded earlier this month to international jurist Navi Pillay.

“Norway has become a shithouse country, actually,” Trump told supporters. He also suggested he no longer wanted Norwegian immigrants coming to the United States because he had not received the prize.

The Nobel Peace Prize is awarded by an independent Norwegian committee, not the country’s government.

Rather than engage in a diplomatic spat, Visit Norway embraced the president’s characterization, promoting restrooms that include the award-winning Stegastein viewpoint toilet overlooking Aurlandsfjord and the triangular Golden Toilet on Senja Island.

The agency repurposed material originally developed for World Toilet Day, which falls on Nov. 19.

The response has been an unexpected tourism success.

According to travel industry publication Skift, Visit Norway’s toilet webpage attracted more than 80,000 views from over 170 countries in five days, with traffic surging to more than 540 times the previous week’s level. Roughly a quarter of visitors were American.

The tourism agency spent less than $10,000 promoting the campaign, with approximately 90% of its traffic arriving organically. A Times Square advertisement was also planned for this weekend.

The new situation is kind of dry, but still good to have. Image via Paradox Interactive

Warming seas are giving hungry fish more time to destroy kelp forests

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Warming oceans are destroying kelp forests around the world, but along one stretch of Japan’s coast, rising temperatures have delivered a double blow.

Summer heat has become intense enough to kill the kelp outright, while warmer waters have given plant-eating fish up to 86 additional days each year to devour whatever grows back.

The kelp forest collapsed from covering three-quarters of the reef to covering none of it in about a decade.

Divers who re-surveyed old kelp beds between 2019 and 2024 found the forests gone from bay after bay.

Ben Harvey and colleagues at the Shimoda Marine Research Center, part of the University of Tsukuba, led the study. Co-authors came from the Marine Biological Association and the University of Plymouth in the United Kingdom.

It is observational work, built from four decades of field records plus lab tests, and the authors wrote that their data can’t separate exactly how much each driver contributed.

Forty years of measurements

The kelp is Ecklonia cava, a brown seaweed known in Japan as kajime, which grows new blades in winter and reaches its full size in summer.

Around the town of Shimoda, on the Izu Peninsula southwest of Tokyo, it once formed dense kelp forests. They grew on rocky reefs down to about 65 to 80 feet (20 to 25 meters).

Researchers at the marine station have weighed the kelp on those reefs four times since the station’s first survey.

The earliest count found roughly half a pound of dried kelp for every square foot of reef at the summer peak (2.37 kilograms per square meter).

By the mid-1990s the peak was down about a third, and by the mid-2010s it was down more than 80%.

In the 2023 and 2024 surveys, the divers found no kelp at all. Every sampling square came up empty, and a forest the station had tracked for four decades was gone.

A separate government monitoring program, which tracks canopy cover, told the same story.

In 2009 and 2010, the kelp canopy covered 75% to 80% of the survey plots. By 2020 it covered less than 5%, and from 2022 it was absent.

A shifted current heated the bays

For most of the record, the sea around Izu warmed slowly, about 0.27°F (0.15°C) per decade from 1982 to 2016.

Then the Kuroshio Current, the warm current that flows north past Japan, swung into a large meander that lasted from 2017 to 2025. The bend pushed warm offshore water into the coastal bays.

During the meander, the observed warming rate climbed to about 5.4°F (2.98°C) per decade, nearly 20 times the earlier rate. However, that figure reflects a much shorter period of unusually rapid warming.

Marine heatwaves, stretches of days far above the normal temperature for the date, became more frequent, longer and more intense, with record totals during the meander years.

Across the peninsula, the coast picked up about 34 extra days above 84°F (29°C) over 2017 to 2024 compared with 2009 to 2016.

That temperature matters because laboratory experiments showed that kelp begins to struggle as water approaches 85°F (29.5°C).

Hot summers kill the kelp

In the lab, the team warmed young kelp step by step from 68°F to 91°F (20°C to 33°C) and tracked how well it photosynthesized.

The kelp held up to about 85°F (29.5°C). Above that it declined fast, and at 90°F to 91°F (32°C to 33°C) every plant had stopped functioning.

Heat well short of lethal still did damage. Over 35 days at 72°F (22°C), an early summer temperature, young kelp grew about 45% in surface area. At 82°F (28°C), a peak summer temperature, it grew about 12%.

All of the plants survived the warmer tank, but they barely grew.

Summer water off Shimoda now reaches the lethal range. The authors wrote that ambient conditions alone can kill the kelp, and that the warmer autumns and winters leave less time for new blades to form before the next summer.

Parrotfish ate the regrowth

Heat alone can kill the kelp. The fish may be why it never came back.

From May 2021 to March 2022, the team surveyed herbivorous fish every month at three reefs near Shimoda: one still forested, one patchy and one already bare. They also filmed the kelp for an hour at a time to count bites.

The forested site went from healthy canopy in early summer to sparse, damaged plants by October and lost its canopy entirely afterward. New seedlings appeared, but they didn’t grow into a forest.

One species, the Japanese parrotfish Calotomus japonicus, accounted for roughly 70% to 95% of the bites on kelp, depending on the site. It bites through the stalk and removes whole plants.

The tropical rabbitfish Siganus fuscescens, one of the southern newcomers in Japanese waters, punches holes in the blades instead.

The fish become more active grazers when water temperatures exceed certain thresholds.

Parrotfish and other subtropical species can graze in water as cool as 59°F (15°C), while rabbitfish generally require temperatures above 77°F (25°C).

Grazing seasons grew longer

Because feeding tracks temperature, a warmer year means a longer feeding season. The team compared the years before the meander with the years during it.

For the parrotfish and the other subtropical species, warm enough water now arrives about 71 days earlier and lingers about 10 days later.

The rabbitfish gained about 35 days. Across the peninsula’s former kelp beds, the suitable window for subtropical grazers grew by about 45 days a year.

The longer season overlaps the months when the kelp used to rebuild after summer die-back. In the authors’ description, fish thin the blades through summer and autumn, then scrape off what is left in winter.

The cool-season break the kelp once relied on no longer exists.

Where kelp disappeared, low-growing turf algae covered the rocks, and the towering underwater forests failed to return.

Can kelp recover?

The records cannot establish exactly how much of the kelp forest collapse resulted from heat and how much from grazing.

But the continued appearance of kelp seedlings through at least 2022 suggests that new plants were still trying to take hold.

The researchers propose combining kelp restoration with measures to reduce grazing pressure during cooler periods.

Similar losses in other warm-current regions, including eastern Australia and South Africa, suggest the problem extends far beyond Japan.

The challenge is that the cooler conditions kelp needs to recover may become increasingly rare. As oceans continue warming, surviving a hot summer may be only half the battle.

Young kelp must also escape the hungry fish waiting for it when temperatures finally fall.

The full study was published in the journal Global Change Biology.

What an astrophysicist might have the perspective to eulogize as “the incredibly improbable trip that we’re on” the rest of us might, and often do, experience as simply and maddeningly absurd — so uncontrollable and incomprehensible as to barely make sense. What are we to make of, and do with, the absurdity of life that swarms us daily? Oliver Sacks believed that “the most we can do is to write — intelligently, creatively, evocatively — about what it is like living in the world at this time.” And yet parsing the what-it-is-like can itself drive us to despair. Still, parse we must.

More than a decade before Albert Camus (November 7, 1913–January 4, 1960) became the second-youngest laureate of the Nobel Prize in Literature, awarded him for work that “with clear-sighted earnestness illuminates the problems of the human conscience in our times,” he contemplated the relationship between absurdity and redemption in a 1945 interview by the French journalist Jeanine Delpech, included at the end of his Lyrical and Critical Essays (public library) — the superb posthumous collection that gave us Camus on how to strengthen our character in difficult times and happiness, despair, and the love of life.

Albert Camus

Three years before the interview, twenty-eight-year-old Camus had stunned the world with his revolutionary philosophical essay The Myth of Sisyphus, which begins with one of the most powerful opening sentences in all of literature and explores the paradox of the absurd in life. “I draw from the absurd three consequences, which are my revolt, my freedom, and my passion,” he writes — something that prompted his interviewer to ask whether a philosophy predicated on absurdity might incline people to despair.

Camus — who years earlier had asserted that “there is no love of life without despair of life” — answers:

All I can do is reply on my own behalf, realizing that what I say is relative. Accepting the absurdity of everything around us is one step, a necessary experience: it should not become a dead end. It arouses a revolt that can become fruitful. An analysis of the idea of revolt could help us to discover ideas capable of restoring a relative meaning to existence, although a meaning that would always be in danger.

Speaking at the close of the meaningless brutality of World War II, six years before he formulated his ideas on solidarity and what it really means to be a rebel, Camus considers the only act of courage and rebellion worth undertaking:

In a world whose absurdity appears to be so impenetrable, we simply must reach a greater degree of understanding among men, a greater sincerity. We must achieve this or perish. How Moscow broke with the West—and unleashed a more chaotic world.
📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Last fall, a tall 8-point Michigan buck showed me just how good—and lasting—the action can be during the first few days of November. The buck hit my mock scrape twice during daylight, which tipped me off that it was time for a strategic hunt where a small woodlot met standing corn. Between my hunt that evening and another the next morning, I had nearly a dozen encounters with the buck as he zig-zagged his way through the broken acres of timber and ag fields, feverishly seeking a receptive doe. The flurry of activity finally ended when he stepped into one of my shooting lanes at 11:10 am.

Bucks are transitioning into full-blown seeking mode right now, and I’m betting that this coming Tuesday will be one of the best days of the year to be on stand. Of course, it is a big election day across the country, so hopefully you voted early or sent in that absentee ballot, because this is a day you’d probably rather be hunting over a scrape line than standing in line waiting to cast a ballot. Here’s what to expect and how to proceed for your best shot at tagging out on Tuesday.

Rut Phase: Late Pre-Rut/Seeking

Expect bucks to be checking just about every doe they see right now. (Photo/John Hafner Photography)

Rut hormones are starting to peak in bucks across much of whitetail range, and more and more does are showing signs that they are ready or nearly ready to breed. It should all add up to great hunting. Bucks, young and old alike, are in seeking mode now, often visible in daylight hours and moving more with each passing day.

This is the height of what I’d call the late pre-rut, with bucks largely still hitting scrapes, but possibly your last chance to catch up to a homebody buck before he abandons those scrapes and gets wildly unpredictable in the all-out chasing phase of the rut. Now, more than ever, bucks are susceptible to a wide range of calling and decoying tactics, as their default safety mode is being overridden by their urge to reproduce. This makes setting up “close enough” a viable strategy for bowhunters with a grunt call in pocket and a set of rattling antlers in hand. So, on this Best Day of the Rut, elect to pack a lunch, sit long, and be ready to shoot on a moment’s notice, because bucks are sure to be on the move.

November 3 Morning Hunt Plan: Sit a Junction Near Doe Bedding

Get tight to doe bedding for the morning hunt, and hang in there as long as you can.

Things change fast in the whitetail world, and staying on top of current sign can make or break your pre-rut hunting. Hopefully you’ve been scouting as much as hunting since our last Best Day. A big mistake many hunters make is thinking more time on stand will mean more opportunities, while ignoring the scouting that actually leads to high-quality sits. At this time of year, a scrape that was smoking hot last week may be stone cold when you show up, so use a weather event or midday break in your schedule to speed-scout for the latest sign and use that info to direct your hunting decisions. This could be checking a low-intrusion camera or cruising those scrape lines to confirm recent activity. Just try to minimize your impact and keep it as low key as possible.

For your morning hunt, think like a buck and focus on the does. Look for a transition area near known doe bedding where you can catch them returning in the morning hours. Bucks will be close behind. Focus on zones with numerous attraction factors or lines of travel to increases your chances. These factors could include a scrape line, convergence of well-used trails, a fruit tree, or multiple edges of cover necking down to one location. These things will steer deer traffic right past a carefully placed stand and multiply your chances of encountering a doe-seeking buck.

With my 2025 Michigan 8-point, the only standing corn in the area, just 30 yards from my stand, was prime doe bedding. This kept the tall 8 in the area and constantly moving, attempting to see if a doe was just behind the next stalk. The block of woods created four edges where it met a neighboring chunk of timber, encouraging deer to naturally move past my stand as they followed these edges. The last factor was a mock scrape the buck had taken over, which I set in August with just this time frame in mind.

Find a similar transition zone near does and stick it out. Even if a buck happens to sneak by you, there’s a good chance he’ll be back soon to re-check the area. Be sure to avoid areas where the deer will travel as you enter. For this killer morning ambush to work, you’ll need get settled early—like and hour early—to make sure you beat the deer back to their bedding transition. And don’t be in a hurry to leave either. Remember, I shot that tall 8-point at 11:10 am last year.

November 3 Evening Hunt Plan: Try a Food Plot Stake-Out

Let real does be your decoys at a prime feeding area in the evening now. (Photo/George Schmiesing via Adobe Stock)

You can’t go wrong with an evening sit at a food-plot, ag field, or other prime feeding area that you know does are hitting consistently. They will essentially be your real-life decoys. Nearby bucks will have a hard time resisting coming into the open to check on any mature doe they see, even if they aren’t quite in estrus, giving you an opportunity if you’re ready. And speaking of being ready, it’s a good idea between and just before hunts to practice getting drawn quickly, fast target acquisition, judging distance at a glance, and stopping a buck with a mouth grunt or bleat.

As for the evening hunt itself, this is prime time to employ a 3D decoy and deep-sounding grunt tube. I say 3D because, in my experience, 2D decoys at close range can spook deer that can’t see them from certain angles. A realistic 3D decoy can change this, signaling to a passing buck that something has this strange buck’s interest which requires his investigation. Bottom line, if a buck is seeking does in daylight, he’s probably sufficiently amped up to be vulnerable to these ploys.

What about scent? Using a fresh estrus scent now (where legal) could add the realism you need and make decent setup irresistible. Use a drag rope but add this trick: Don’t drag but dab your scent-filled rag on the ground like deer footsteps, exactly what a buck would be tracking. Laura D’Allaird, chief of the SEC Enforcement Division’s Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit, described the operation in a statement.

When considering cartridges like 6.5 Creedmoor vs. .300 Win. Mag., the .300 has more energy, drives a bigger bullet faster, and, we assume, will do more damage than the 6.5. That means that if we don’t hit exactly where we are aiming, we are more likely to still kill the animal cleanly by choosing the magnum. At face value, the logic is sound. But how does it hold up when we start examining what the bullets actually do downrange? You might be surprised.

Smaller Cartridges Vs. Magnums

Magnums like the .300 Win. Mag. are popular, but do they really give you margin for error? Tyler Freel

There are two schools of thought that commonly divide hunters when it comes to choosing cartridges. One opts for milder cartridges that have lower recoil and are generally easier and more pleasant to shoot well. The other wants the extra power to cover their bases. Everyone, of course, falls somewhere on a spectrum, and the categories aren’t mutually exclusive. 

Small Cartridge Theory

I’ve written a lot about why I think smaller cartridges are often the right choice for big game, and it generally boils down to two observations. First, with thoughtful bullet selection, I haven’t noticed a dramatic difference between the wound characteristics of smaller and larger cartridges — and the smaller rounds have produced consistently satisfactory results. Second, milder cartridges are more pleasant to shoot and, despite having no real limitations on the cartridges I have access to, that’s what I reach for consistently. I have the rifles and ammo to torch a hundred rounds of .300 PRC in a range session practicing positional shooting but, realistically, I’m going to pick a 6.5 Creedmoor, 6 Creed, or 22 ARC to do that. Practice with smaller cartridges can also benefit how you’ll shoot magnums, but there’s a lot of value in hunting with the cartridge that you train with.

Proponents of smaller cartridges will point out that they deliver enough terminal performance to get the job done but are, generally, much easier to shoot well. Tyler Freel

Large Cartridge Theory

You’ll commonly hear hunters argue that you should choose the largest cartridge that you can handle without flinching — especially for larger game. Lots of hunters consider this basic conventional wisdom, and it’s rooted in the belief that a larger cartridge, bullet, or more kinetic energy will result in a larger wound and will kill the animal more quickly. This makes up for some degree of shooting error, or will allow more forgiveness on less-than-ideal shot angles. Essentially, choosing a more powerful cartridge is viewed as cheap insurance. 

The .300 Win. Mag. works very well on big game like moose, even beyond 300 or 400 yards, so it’s easy to understand why people like it. Tyler Freel

Real-World Results

There are many ways that bigger cartridges like the .300 Win. Mag. outperform smaller ones like the 6.5 Creedmoor. When choosing something for yourself, you need to know what the real benefits are, and decide whether they actually apply to you and your hunting. What does that look like in terms of margin for error? That’s exactly what we took a look at in the video above. 

I took a 6.5 Creedmoor and .300 Win. Mag. to the range and fired the same line of factory ammo — Federal Fusion Tipped — from each into identical blocks of Clear Ballistics gel. This gel isn’t a one-to-one representation of flesh, but it is a homogeneous, consistent medium in which we can get a side-by-side comparison of two bullets. The blocks were placed at 200 yards with a Garmin chronograph positioned about 15 yards in front of the target to record impact velocity. The Fusion Tipped bullets were 140 grains and 180 grains for the 6.5 CM and .300 Win. Mag., respectively. The rifles were the same except for different barrel lengths — 18 inches for the 6.5 CM and 22 inches for the .300. Impact velocities were recorded as 2,233 fps and 2,526 fps, with the advantage going to the .300.

Takeaways

This demonstration shouldn’t be taken to mean that the 6.5 CM and .300 Win. Mag. are completely equal. They each have their advantages and disadvantages. The results, however, do track pretty well with other gel testing I’ve done, and what I’ve observed on animals. Those are that, with the same bullet construction, results don’t vary too much across most common hunting cartridges. In fact, things like penetration and wound cavity size can be more greatly influenced by bullet selection than simply picking a bigger or smaller cartridge. 

Some hunters place a lot of weight in having a cartridge that “hits harder.” While we can see some of the effects in the movement of the gel blocks during this demonstration, and that delta in impact force could possibly offer increased effect to the central nervous system, it’s not a reliable predictor of how fast an animal will die, or how much room for error you have. What we can rely on is the damage the bullet does.

One of the real advantages of a magnum cartridge, with a thoughtfully chosen bullet, is that it can deliver effective velocities over longer distances. A disadvantage of a 6.5 Creedmoor, in common shorter barrel configurations, is that it will produce lower muzzle velocities and result in a shorter effective velocity window. Ensuring that your impact speeds are high enough to make the bullet work properly is critical. 

Our field shooting exercise did demonstrate that you might be able to shoot a magnum just as well as the 6.5. If you can, then you are better poised to take advantage of a magnum’s real benefits. Over time, most people will find that they shoot milder cartridges more consistently. It is most important to choose a cartridge that you can shoot well and will practice with.

Along with better equipment and more refined shooting technique, we are constantly learning more about terminal ballistics. Many of our long-held beliefs are simply a product of lore and oversimplified logic. If you’re a successful hunter and your favorite cartridge works well for you, we’re not asking you to change. But if you’re feeling the pressure to choose a cartridge that you don’t think you’ll shoot as well, because you feel that it’s powerful enough to make up the difference, you’re barking up the wrong tree. The smaller cartridges will work just as well on the good shots, and the big ones will do just as poorly on the bad shots.

“Neighbor down the road had a squirrel dog,” said Larry White as he led a cast of dogs and hunters to the woods. “Got its legs cut off by a mowing machine. Had to hunt that dog from a wheelbarrow.

“One day a friend asked to borrow the dog, so the owner explained that you had to push the wheelbarrow in whatever direction the dog pointed his nose. If he barked, the track was hot, so you pushed faster. When he looked up and barked, he had the squirrel treed and you got a shot. The friend came back late that night with no squirrels. His clothes were torn to shreds and his skin was scratched up and looked like a road map. ‘What in the world happened to you?’ the hound’s owner asked.

“‘What’s it look like?’ the friend snapped. ‘Your fool squirrel dog hit a deer and we ran that thing all day long!'”

Larry White was judging a cast in the 1981 World Champion Squirrel Hunt. His tale got a laugh, but it also emphasized the importance of handler/dog teamwork in any form of hunting.

Outdoor Life

A good squirrel dog works for the hunter by locating squirrels through scent on the ground and on trees as well as by seeing and following bushytails moving through the treetops. The dog’s manner of barking signals its degree of progress to the hunter. A good hunter works for the dog by staying keenly and constantly aware of what the barking means and by following almost within sight so he can get to the tree quickly and shoot the squirrel before it escapes.

Good competition squirrel dogs are more decisive, accurate and consistent than average squirrel dogs. They do the job with less wasted time and motion.

Squirrel-treeing talent always has been wherever you found it, but these days some unusual dogs are showing up in the woods. Chuck Trehern’s purebred Doberman pinscher learned to bark up a tree by watching the family coonhounds. Ronnie Liles’ 6-year-old Australian shepherd, Smokey, was never taught: he just started treeing squirrels. T.C. Jones won the 1980 Championship with a registered liver and white English pointer! Trophies similar to bowling trophies are awarded. The statuettes portray a dog barking treed.

Most squirrel dogs are not pedigreed, however. Two Illinois dogs were described as terrier and feist crosses. Randy Pannell, who trains 15 to 20 squirrel dogs every year, came over from Blue Springs, Mississippi, with a redtick coonhound/mountain cur cross. Fola Hart brought a Norwegian elkhound/feist cross from Virginia. And the 1981 winner was Orville Hart’s “Whiskey,” a 4-year-old Norwegian elkhound/feist from Leesburg, Ohio.

Feists, those wiry little mongrels that are intelligent enough to learn any job on the farm, are becoming rare because most people prefer to keep purebred dogs. As a result, many squirrel hunters are making their own mongrels by crossing two different purebreds. There’s a constant search for the best dog, and that’s one reason why Dick Sheets thought up the World Champion Squirrel Hunt.

Sheets reasoned that squirrel-dog competition would not only preserve and enlarge the sport, but that the dogs would improve through breeding. Perhaps breeds of purebred squirrel dogs of consistent ability would emerge from such an effort. The Yockanookany Coon Hunters Association of Ethel, Mississippi, agreed with Sheets and sponsored the first championship in 1980.

Yockanookany is Choctaw for “crawls on belly like snake” and also applies to the way hunters must cross a spongy swamp. The 100-mile-long Yockanookany Swamp is just two miles from the club grounds.

By 1981, competition had already influenced squirrel-dog breeding. L.E. Goutierez came over from Louisiana with two pups out of the 1980 second-place winner. The pups’ dam was Spot, his own small Chihuahua/feist cross.

Goutierez arrived a day early, pitched his tent and drove his dogs down the old Natchez Trace to the nearby woods where they could become accustomed to hunting new territory. The original Natchez Trace, which was once an extensive trading trail between the Indian tribes, runs right past the Yockanookany club grounds. The entire area is well forested with both hill-country and creek-bottom squirrel habitat.

Little Spot ran through the woods with her nose slightly down, searching for scent on the ground, on logs and on fallen limbs. Every tree got a sniff as she trotted past. But the squirrels weren’t moving. When Spot finally barked, L.E. didn’t become excited. “She scatter-barked,” he said. “It’s a cold track.”

Scatter barking is barking here and there instead of barking enthusiastically in one place such as on a hot trail or at a tree.

By the end of the championship hunt, Goutierez had learned one more thing about breeding squirrel dogs. “Have to get more size in my dogs,” he said. “My dogs can’t compete with the longer-legged, wider-running dogs.”

Outdoor Life

Four dogs were cast together in the first running of the championship in 1980 and many handlers thought that that was three too many. By nature, squirrel dogs do best alone. They’re highly intelligent, very independent and more than a little jealous of their relationships with their human partners. In 1981, a compromise was struck: only two dogs to a cast—male and female, if possible—and in weight categories of under and over 35 pounds.

The only reported fight occurred when the Doberman’s handler decided to be gentlemanly and let his competitor’s dog smell the dead squirrel, too. Getting to smell the squirrel is an encouraging reward of sorts and the Dobe was not ready to share that with another dog.

An impartial judge accompanies each cast, but it is up to the handler to “call” his own dog—that is, notify the judge when satisfied his dog has treed. As in coonhound competition, 100 points are earned by the first dog at the tree, 75 by the second. Unlike coonhound scoring, no points are awarded for striking (finding) a trail. This is because sometimes the trail is short, sometimes there is none and too many squirrel dogs are silent trailers. Also unlike coonhound scoring, tree points are not subtracted if no squirrel is found in the tree because squirrels all too readily travel from tree to tree.

If the squirrel is not found, the tree points are circled and do not count on the final score. Judges are allowed considerable latitude, however. If the squirrel is seen several trees away from the one the dog is treeing on and the judge is satisfied that the squirrel could have gotten there through the treetops, he can give the dog credit for that squirrel.

Six or eight squirrels an hour is not impossible in well-populated timber when the bushytails are really active. Ronnie Liles’ Australian shepherd treed 700 for him and his friends during the past two years. “The trick,” said Liles, “is to catch ’em stirrin’. You might not see a squirrel today. But you can come back through the same place tomorrow and see 10.”

Ronnie believes that the old hunters’ barometer, “If the cows are lying down, so are the deer,” also applies to squirrels. And as he says, the dogs are helpless if they can’t see, smell or possibly hear the bushytails. The squirrel that hasn’t been on the ground and is lounging on a limb or in a vine tangle probably won’t be discovered by a dog unless falling air currents allow the dog to “wind” it. This would happen mostly during the cool of late afternoon rather than during early morning when the warming sunlight causes air to rise.

Liles prefers hunting little cat squirrels (grays) in the big, swampy, bottom timbers. Shaking a vine spooks grays out of hiding faster and easier than it does fox squirrels. Other hunters choose the hills where more fox squirrels live because they don’t like the way grays often chase off through the treetops when a dog barks.

Randy Pannell minimizes that problem by staying fairly close to his dog and then getting to the tree fast. In competition, however, that’s not always possible. The second dog has five minutes in which to join the first dog at the tree. During that time, handlers and the judge stay clear.

Both Pannell and Fola Hart demonstrated the wisdom of walking apart from other hunters. At 79, Fola appeared to be walking far behind because of age. But he was the first to hear his dog bark treed while other members of the cast were busy talking.

The perceptive rapport between hunter and squirrel dog is nothing less than amazing. During a Friday afternoon cast, Orville Hart was reluctant to say that Whiskey had treed. “Them two dogs are looking at us,” he observed. “If they believed they had the squirrel, they’d be looking up the tree!”

When a squirrel was shot in Ronnie Liles’ cast, Smokey grabbed it and ran off with the second dog in hot pursuit. As Smokey raced past his master’s leg, Ronnie grinned. “That dog expects me to catch the other dog as it goes by. Then Smokey will bring me the squirrel. He won’t let the other dog have it. But if another dog gets to the squirrel first, Smokey won’t bother it.” Retrieving is an added nicety, but it isn’t rewarded with extra points during the Yockanookany competition.

Some dogs won’t bark if their masters happen to be present when the squirrel is treed, strongly suggesting that they have enough reasoning power to recognize that, in such a case, the boss doesn’t have to be called.

One Mississippi hunter recalled a boyhood dog that would always dance around the opposite side of the tree, yanking and shaking every vine or sapling it could find. Invariably, this commotion chased the squirrel around to the gunner’s side. Since such a dog is quite rare, hunters often go in pairs, taking turns circling the tree and shaking the bushes after the dog has treed.

What’s it take to train a dog that shows interest in squirrels? Mostly, lots of time in the squirrel woods. But trainer Randy Pannell speeds things up with a slingshot and steel pellets. “Just hit the tree near the squirrel, and it will bail out or take off,” Pannell said. “The dog gets a sight chase through the treetops or maybe a short ground chase. This works best with gray squirrels, especially the young ones. And there’s no problem with game laws while training dogs because you’re not killing or hunting the squirrels.”

Never study treetops when the dog is in sight and isn’t treeing. A perceptive dog believes you see the squirrel and will start treeing. And never bring the dog running by shooting at something other than a squirrel. Fooling a squirrel dog ruins it fast.

The Yockanookany fox squirrels are flat out the most colorful in America. "Lady in Blue is as fitting a monument to our era as any I can imagine"

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Tschabalala Self's sculpture is far from being out of place in Trafalgar Square, writes Priya Khanchandani.

Photo by Julian Worker/Shutterstock

Who belongs in public space? How should public art represent who we are? And who deserves to be celebrated, cast in bronze and placed on a pedestal? These are some of the questions that Tschabalala Self's commission for the Fourth Plinth, Lady in Blue, has raised in recent months, since it was unveiled in September.

I was there that cloudy morning in Trafalgar Square – a space I'd always crossed, probably like most Londoners, without thinking much about what it symbolises. When the black cloth was pulled away, we saw the figure of a Black woman caught mid-stride, one leg pushing through the fabric of her bright blue dress.

Like most of the admirals and generals monumentalised around her, she is cast in bronze. But unlike them she isn't posed in command, she's on her way somewhere. Her head, hair piled high, looks straight out into the city towards Whitehall, her left arm and fingers outstretched, moving with intention.

My Instagram post about the unveiling alarmingly attracted more than 6,000 comments, many of them either subtly or openly racist

I couldn't help but feel satisfaction that a sculpture of a Black woman was there, taking up space in the city, on what is probably the most visible public plinth in Britain, and assumed this sentiment would be widely shared. It turns out I was wrong; my Instagram post about the unveiling alarmingly attracted more than 6,000 comments, many of them either subtly or openly racist and often suggesting the sculpture doesn't fit its context.

Photo by Sean Aidan Calderbank/Shutterstock

I feel the need, therefore, to address the notion of what and who belongs in public space, and particularly in a space like Trafalgar Square.

Before Trafalgar Square existed, the site was used for the royal stables that served Whitehall Palace. Designed in 1840 by Charles Barry, the square was conceived as a stage for Britain's image of itself, articulated through the grandeur of neoclassicism, and statues were central to its story. If you linger, you'll spot George IV in Roman robes, sitting valiantly astride his horse. The generals Henry Havelock and Charles James Napier, whose reputations were made in the violent consolidation of British rule in India (the latter for the conquest of Sindh, where all four of my grandparents happen to be from) stand on two further plinths. 

Admiral Horatio Nelson himself, the Royal Navy officer whose victory at the Battle of Trafalgar gave the square its name, overlooks them from 50 metres high atop a granite Corinthian column, guarded by four bronze lions. This composition represents a particular vision of 19th-century imperial power, reflected in the monuments and civic spaces rolled out across the Empire.

"When we place people on a pedestal, we only diminish ourselves"

The square might be beautiful, and a fitting monument to its time, but it is also a living, breathing entity formed not only through its monuments and buildings, but also through the way it has been used.

Its function and meaning as a civic space keep being shaped by the people who have held festivals, memorials, gatherings here – or even simply walked through it on their daily commute. After it was built, Trafalgar Square quickly became a place for public assembly. From Bloody Sunday to the suffragettes to Black Lives Matter and the Palestine Coalition, it has a long tradition as a space of protest.

The square has been redesigned over and over, most significantly by Foster + Partners and Atkins in a 2003 scheme that closed the road along its northern edge, adding a terrace and turning what was essentially a traffic island into a pedestrianised space.

For some, in recent years the commission has merely settled into a pleasant biennial ritual

Lady in Blue is in dialogue with this context. She echoes the other figures here through her medium, bronze. And yet while the other figures are rooted to their respective plinths, she looks frozen in motion, echoing the movement of the people in the square below rather than the stillness of statues. The vivid blue of the figure's dress – evoking lapis lazuli, the stone that has been ground into pigment since antiquity – is far from an attack on the Western canon, but rather in conscious conversation with it.

Once intended for an equestrian statue of William IV, the Fourth Plinth stood empty for more than 150 years before it became a space for contemporary sculpture in 1998. Unoccupied, it was always suggestive of a history waiting to be told or – given that the sculptures around it were all commemorating a contemporary moment – a future moment waiting to be solidified in bronze.

The absence of a statue suggested the question of who belongs on a pedestal was always open. Its dedication to contemporary art has allowed for redefinition and offered a chance to bring a quite spectacular historic space, a space of democracy, protest and celebration, into sync with the present.

The Fourth Plinth has become a space where identities are made visible and histories challenged, from Marc Quinn's powerful Alison Lapper Pregnant, to Yinka Shonibare's sculpture of Nelson's own ship in a bottle. Works like these have also, at times, provoked arguments about how the square should be used, and yet each of them has become a part of its changing meaning.

For some, in recent years the commission has merely settled into a pleasant biennial ritual – and yet, this year's sculpture underscores why it exists and why it's so necessary right now.

In 2026, when a Black woman crossing this space is one of the most ordinary sights imaginable, Self's statue validates her as worthy of belonging, not just in the city but here, in a culturally and politically charged public space dominated by white men sculpted by other white men.

Any notion that she doesn't belong isn't an aesthetic judgement, so much as a claim about who the city is for

That elevating such a woman into the visual language of the monument should provoke rage is telling, especially when all the figure has done is walk. Any notion that she doesn't belong isn't an aesthetic judgement, so much as a claim about who the city is for.

Self has said her work is meant to speak to human experience beyond racial identity. She has described the sculpture as depicting "everywoman", implying that an ordinary person merits acknowledgement.

In other words, she hasn't put it into the world simply as a statement about race – though its reception shows how readily the presence of a Black woman in this setting becomes about who is allowed to stand for the universal.

It's as fitting a monument to our era as any I can imagine. At such a fractured time, I can't help but feel relieved she is there, elbowing her way through the noise, making room for all of us.

Alysandra Dutton on Writing Historical Noir and Investigating Ancient Roman Crimes

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In 753 BCE something happened to the Sabine Women. Romulus, fresh off of founding Rome, could not make marriages for himself and his men, who were considered little more than a gang of particularly nasty bandits. He opted for stealing his brides from neighboring Sabinum during a festival, cementing Rome’s legacy in their wombs.

And when I was fourteen, sitting in the audience of a local high school production of Seven Brides for Seven Brothers, a romantic comedy retelling of the Abduction of the Sabine Women, listening to the cast sing jovially about how those women kissed and cried all over that Roman countryside, a feeling welled in me that I would later associate with the need to clear my desk and get writing: indignation. Or maybe, spite.

Ever since, the Sabine Women have haunted me, much the way true crime stories haunt my generation of American women; their pain felt cautionary, their story morbidly compelling, as they showed up again and again in my drafting notebooks. Sure, the crime was solved. We know who took the Sabine Women, and we know why.

But in time I realized I wasn’t able to let them go because the injustice was ongoing; historians writing seven hundred years after the founding of Rome claimed the women were happy to be kidnapped and in love with their captors, marking perhaps one of the earliest incel arguments that women secretly desire to be overpowered, that rape is not that violating after all. And until the record said otherwise, I couldn’t not write.

The Stolen Women came together for me, probably not surprisingly, as I discovered an interest in detective literature. I already felt like an investigator, cynical and working against the grain for my victims; it made sense to take years of notes and half-started stories and filter them through classic noir conventions.

My favorite noirs are about fighting—often futilely—against the system. That seemed to me a fitting framework by which to see a young Sabine woman, pitted against Rome. As contemporary readers, we know what’s coming—we know Rome wins.

But in noir, the the tragic end—often as foregone a conclusion as Rome—is an aside. The story is about what drives a person to leave their well-earned cynicism at the door and fight anyway. I wanted to give the Sabine that story.

Soon, Safinia appeared on the page. My classic, hard-boiled detective; at first, all she wants is to be left alone to put food on the table and protect her sisters. But then, the femme fatale, a woman in danger, in tears, draws her into the twisting, atmospherically moody world of a mystery.

The disappearance of an ex-lover, answers that lead to more questions, and a sidekick classical historians will immediately recognize as keeping vital secrets—it was all there. I may as well have given her a pipe to smoke on as slats of light shine through her dusty office (she does, at one point, smoke hemp in the fashion of the ancient world—one of my favorite scenes, and a send-up I couldn’t resist). But what would happen when her investigation led to Rome?

As Safinia descended further into her obsessive pursuit of mysteriously disappeared women, I also began to feel obsessive, my research notes looking more like a murder board every day. By this point, I was living in Rome, as a member of the awards-holder cohort at the British School at Rome. Before this moment, I had written a lot about trauma; my history necessitated it. Writing about violence, about violation, was my way of making sense of my own past.

In Safinia, I found cathartic satisfaction in her quest for answers and subsequent fight for survival. But what began as an intimate story about gendered violence began to take a larger shape during my days at the BSR. Suddenly, I realized I was writing a book not only about violence—but about violence in Rome. And the qualifier mattered.

Any contemporary archeologist will tell you that the study of Rome is also the study of fascism. I’ve written before about how it is impossible to extract fascism from its birthplace, and the reason for this is practical and material: fascism mucked up a lot of crime scenes in its day. Or, I should say, excavations.

Mussolini’s thirst to associate himself with the image of the Roman Empire meant large swaths of history were greedily uncovered, poorly documented, and then left to rot. Far from the conservation practices of archeological excavations today, even the Colosseum Mussolini was determined to see from his balcony, tearing down a historic medieval neighborhood to achieve this, was neglected by the 1970s. It was a known site for drug-users and criminals (and underground cinemas and raves, but that’s a story for another day), crumbling dangerously around its edges.

I note the irony in an essay at the back of The Stolen Women: Mussolini wanted to see the colosseum Emperor Domitian built so that he could feel like a God, too. But Emperor Domitian, my research found, proudly displayed an impossible to verify family tree that tied him to the original kidnapped Sabine Women, eight hundred years before his reign. This was the brag of important men in Rome, their Sabine ancestors promoted from victims to propaganda that proves a family’s interests in the Eternal City.

From here, my tact had to change; a moody, forget-it-Jake-it’s-Chinatown ending had no place in this world. I’d been so consumed by the crime of kidnapping, and the futility of Safinia’s hopes for solving it, that I was missing the crime of history, of rhetoric; not of how you tell the story of an Empire, but of how you justify it.

Ancient historians edged around the Sabine Women, acknowledging the crime but inventing, in the same breath, an ending that renders their kidnapping palatable. Titus Livius—Livy—claims in his histories that the women kept their freedom of choice and chose to stay with their Roman captors; Dionysius of Halicarnassus praises Romulus’ bold foreign policy in his decision to kidnap them in the first place. And Mussolini, in Italy’s fascist era, rips the protective shell off of history and lays it bare and eroded, absent any context that doesn’t spell empire.

In these days of writing and drafting, Safinia’s task looked enormous, insurmountable. But if nothing else, she taught me how to move doggedly, determinedly through history; to trust the way the Earth churns up new answers and new mysteries. They will never have all of you, a historian tells her at a low moment, and he’s right.

The truth has a way of breaking through.

Poll: Americans don’t trust polls

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

This just in, according to new poll results: Americans don’t trust polls.

Only 25% of Americans said political surveys correctly estimate votes, according to a new POLITICO Poll, conducted by PF Global. The result reveals that a significant portion of the public lacks faith in the industry as it contends with accusations of consistently failing to reach Republican voters.

It comes amid an intensely scrutinized 2026 midterm cycle, where public polling and campaigns’ internal polls have shown many races within the margin of error in key battlegrounds and in once-safe red territory.

Roughly one in four respondents say that polls showing a blue wave coming in November are wrong, and they expect Republicans to win key races. And 41% of Americans — including voters of both parties — either say polls tend to underestimate Democrats or that they tend to underestimate Republicans.

There have been some high-profile blunders: Polls have missed themark throughout the Trump era. This cycle alone, surveys in Democratic primaries incorrectly predicted that progressive Francesca Hong would win the race for Wisconsin governor and that Abdul El-Sayed — who did become Democrat’s Michigan Senate nominee — would win by a much wider margin than the 1 percentage point victory he eked out.

Speaker Mike Johnson and other Republicans have cast doubt on the latest forecasts: The polls are “grossly underestimating Republican enthusiasm around the country,” Johnson said on Fox News this week, adding that “more and more Republicans are unwilling to participate in these surveys.”

President Donald Trump has called the polls “fake” and “rigged.” Vice President JD Vance said he tells people to ignore the polls because they have a “demotivating effect on our voters.”

Pollsters know their industry is facing heavy scrutiny. However: “There isn't another thing waiting in the wings to replace the full purpose of this tool,” said Charlotte Swasey, the director of analytics for the Democratic think tank Searchlight Institute, told POLITICO. “This is what we have, and it's difficult, it's increasingly difficult. But it's not dead by any sense of the word.”

Pollsters have made a number of changes over the years — like capturing respondents across all levels of education attainment or adjusting their methodologies to account for low response rates among any given group.

 Still, roughly two in five Americans who voted for Trump in 2024 say political polls tend to underestimate Republicans.

Their feelings are not completely unfounded. In 2016, pollsters grossly underestimated support for then-candidate Trump, with most polls coalescing around a relatively secure victory for former Secretary of State Hilary Clinton.

Distrust is especially widespread among Trump voters who identify as “MAGA Republicans” — his most loyal base. Three in five MAGA Trump voters say the polls indicating Democrats will win key races next week are incorrect. Among non-MAGA Trump voters, just a third feel the same.

“National polls always underestimate support for Republicans,” said RNC national press secretary Natalie Baldassarre. “Polls may suggest one thing, but the American people get the final say, and Republicans remain laser-focused on driving voters to the polls, defying history, and protecting our majorities."

“I do think that non-response among base Republican voters in some of these red states is a problem,” Christine Matthews, president of public opinion polling firm Bellwether Research, told POLITICO in a recent interview.

Republican candidates and officials are banking on the non-response caveat as Senate races in states like Kansas, Mississippi and Nebraska — and to some extent, in South Carolina and Louisiana — all tighten, with less than one week until Election Day. If polling proves true, they could experience once-unthinkable losses in deep-red territory.

But distrust is not limited to Republicans.

Many in both parties were outraged after one group — Wedgewood Polls — admitted under scrutiny from FiftyPlusOne to using fake data in its polling results in several Democratic primaries, which some believe impacted election results. Another group published fake polling data on the Los Angeles mayoral race as a test to see how the data would travel before being verified.

And just under a third of voters who backed former Vice President Kamala Harris say political polls tend to underestimate Democrats.

Part of the distrust can come down to the fact that polls are not intended to forecast outcomes. They are a snapshot of public sentiment at a given point in time — and people can change their minds. One in five Americans said in POLITICO’s survey they have filled in a poll on how they would vote differently than how they actually voted.

“Of course there are people who say something to a poll and then do something different a couple of weeks later,” said Seb Wride, head of polling at London-based PF Global, POLITICO’s polling partner. “I think it's quite possible that some of the races that were real big misses earlier this year were decided in the very last week.”

According to a recent POLITICO survey, polls — which, when conducted correctly, involve careful planning and meticulous methodology — don’t earn much more trust than prediction markets, where odds are determined simply by bets placed by the public.

Just 18% of people said they trusted poll-based forecasts over prediction markets to correctly predict the outcome of an election. That’s a marginal lead on the 12% of people who said they trust prediction markets over polls. A third said they trust neither.

Still, most voters say they do make decisions based on polling. Roughly 55% of Americans say they would “definitely” go vote if the polls showed their preferred candidate far behind. Even among Republican midterm voters who say the polls underestimate their party, roughly 75% say the same.

“There are a lot of voters that are motivated to send a message to the system,” said longtime GOP strategist Kevin Madden. “I think particularly on the [political] right, they'll be motivated to send a message, and essentially that message is ‘You're wrong, and the voters are right.’”

The big question for campaigns is “are there enough of those?” he said.

MoMA, Centre Pompidou, and M+ Will Treat Their Holdings as One Collection in Unprecedented Alliance

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Six years ago, the director of Hong Kong’s M+ museum, Suhanya Raffel, told cultural strategist András Szántó, in an interview for the book The Future of the Museum, that in the years to come museums will need to “stand together and work to help one another to deliver a more sustainable baseline.” Collaboration, she went on, “will be a key aspect of the next chapter of museum work.” Her words seem to have been prophetic: on Friday morning in Hong Kong, M+, the Museum of Modern Art in New York, and the Centre Pompidou in Paris announced a wide-ranging cultural alliance unprecedented in its scale. At the heart of the alliance is an agreement that the three institutions will treat their respective holdings as a shared collection, curated by staff spread across three continents.

“If we succeed,” Laurent Le Bon, the Pompidou’s president, told ARTnews in Paris, during a joint interview that also included Raffel and MoMA director Christophe Cherix, “we will be more than the sum of our parts, and I think that is something quite new.”

The combined holdings of MoMA and the Pompidou mean the total shared collection will number in the hundreds of thousands of objects, and the three institutions are also stewards of voluminous archives. The collection at M+, founded six years ago, numbers around 8,000 objects, but the museum has a definitive collection of contemporary Chinese art, much of it donated by Swiss businessman and former diplomat Uli Sigg. The Pompidou houses the largest modern art museum in Europe (its holdings are a national collection owned by the French state). The potential audience will be enormous: based on current figures, the museums will have a combined 10 million annual in-person visitors and 125 million online visitors.

The collection sharing will kick off with several initiatives. Beginning in late 2027, a selection of major works from M+’s collection will be installed on long-term loan in MoMA’s collection galleries, and in 2028, a long-term Centre Pompidou gallery will also open at MoMA, with similar, parallel plans in development at the other museums. (The Pompidou is currently closed for a five-year restoration of its building and is scheduled to reopen in 2030.)

As part of the alliance, the partner institutions will, over the next five years, co-organize an exhibition exploring the complex concept of “the contemporary,” which will include works from all three collections and be presented at all three institutions; curators at the Centre Pompidou and M+ will join MoMA’s C-MAP (Contemporary and Modern Art Perspectives), a cross-departmental research program founded in 2009 with the aim of exploring art histories outside North America and Western Europe.

Additionally, a joint steering committee, made up of the three museum directors along with department heads and curators from the three museums, will begin to work on such priorities as exhibitions and collection sharing, research, conservation, and professional development. The committee will meet monthly, and there will be one major in-person meeting a year, the first of which will take place next month at MoMA. (Subsequent annual meetings will rotate among the museums.)

To a certain extent, the alliance comes out of previous partnerships between M+ and the other two institutions. In February 2025, M+ and MoMA signed a memorandum of understanding that encompassed collection sharing, joint research, and curatorial exchanges. Earlier this year, M+ and the Centre Pompidou announced their own multiyear strategic partnership, which included plans for the Pompidou to inaugurate its renovated space with an exhibition of works from both museums’ collections, followed by a presentation of that show at M+.

In the interview with ARTnews in Paris, the three directors acknowledged that the new cultural alliance grew out of the previous bilateral partnerships. They stressed, however, that it also grew out of the three institutions’ commonalities, such as cross-disciplinary collecting and a focus on the art of the present, as well as the lineage the museums form: nearly a century of collecting contemporary art, starting with MoMA’s founding in 1929 and continuing through the Pompidou’s opening in 1977 and M+’s in 2021. Raffel has acknowledged that Centre Pompidou was “one of the key institutions” that M+ “looked to that helped us guide the principles around building an institution like this for Asia.”

“We each have things that are absolutely unique in the world,” said Cherix. “Our first role is of course preservation. But how can we make those collections work harder for us and touch people well beyond the walls of our institutions?”

This alliance differs from past models such as traveling exhibitions, co-organized shows, and collaborations on specific projects, Cherix explained, in that it is a long-term commitment to building programs together from the ground up, one that will involve “bringing our teams much closer together.”

The new arrangement also aims to take advantage of economies of scale and to make things like shipping more sustainable. Raffel and Cherix pointed out that, for instance, sending parts of M+’s collection of contemporary Chinese art to MoMA for extended periods would not only allow those works to rotate through the MoMA galleries but also make them more easily available to other museums in the United States.

In a world in which museums want their collections to reflect global perspectives, sharing makes more sense than continuing to accumulate separately. “We can’t just keep collecting, so we are sharing instead,” said Raffel. This raises the bar for acquisitions: to be added to the shared collection, a work will, as Cherix put it, “need to have no equal” in any of the three collections.

“Even if we want to be devoted to the art of our time, we become a historical collection,” Cherix continued. “How can we reset that? I think this alliance gives us exactly the capacity to have a different scope, and a forward-looking vision.”

More museums have partnered in recent years to share collections, pool resources, and expand their audiences. The strategy has evolved from standard short-term exhibition loans to deeply integrated models that include joint acquisitions, shared private donations, regional networks, and long-term international exchanges. Joint acquisitions have become increasingly common since 2003, when the Whitney Museum, Tate, and the Centre Pompidou teamed up to purchase Bill Viola’s video installation Five Angels for the Millennium.

An arrangement somewhat similar to the MoMA–Pompidou–M+ model came in 2018, when Chinese Indonesian collector Budi Tek partnered with the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA) in a collection-sharing arrangement with Shanghai’s Yuz Museum, which Tek had founded. (China rejected attempts to make the Yuz Museum a public institution; Tek died in 2022.) In that case, a new foundation was formed to oversee the collection, which would live in China but otherwise travel between Los Angeles and Shanghai for temporary exhibitions. Works from LACMA’s collection, for example, traveled to the Yuz Museum in 2009 for the exhibition “In Production: Art and the Studio System,” while in 2021, 20 pieces from Tek’s collection went on display at LACMA for the exhibition “Legacies of Exchange.”

More recently, museums have agreed to be the joint stewards of certain collectors’ holdings. In 2022, around 100 works from the collection of Greek industrialist Dimitri Daskalopoulos became a shared gift to the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum and the Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago. In 2024, three Los Angeles museums—the Hammer, LACMA, and the Museum of Contemporary Art—jointly received some 350 works owned by Jarl and Pamela Mohn. Those three institutions continue to add to that collection, called the Mohn Art Collective (MAC3).

When Your Parents Are Dying: Some of the Simplest, Most Difficult and Redemptive Life-Advice You’ll Ever Receive

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Death makes human beings seem like very small containers that are packed so densely we can only be aware of a fraction of what’s inside us from moment to moment.”

“Your children are not your children. They are the sons and daughters of Life’s longing for itself,” Kahlil Gibran wrote in his poignant verse on parenting. And yet we are, each of us, someone’s child — physiologically or psychologically or both — and they sing themselves through us as we sing ourselves into our longing for life, whether we like the melody or not.

Like a Zen koan, this fact becomes utterly discomposing when you begin thinking deeply about the fundamental, layered realities beneath the mundane, even banal factuality of the fact. Parents — the very notion of them. The notion that you — this immensely complex totality of sinew and selfhood, this portable universe shimmering with a million ideas and passions and little ways of being-in-the-world that make you you — began as a glimmer in someone else’s eye, a set of chemical reactions that became molecules that became cells in someone else’s body before they constellated into you. The notion that so many dimensions of your personhood, so many of the givens you take for granted in making sense of the world, were forged by someone other than yourself (and possibly other than the body that begot the cells that became you) — someone who occupies, in the cosmogony of you, this strange and staggering position of arbiter between the existence and nonexistence of the particular you that you are.

Kinship by Maria Popova. (Available as a print.)

The doubly discomposing experience of what happens when that arbiter crosses the threshold of their own nonexistence is what Mary Gaitskill addresses in her thoughtful, tender contribution to Take My Advice: Letters to the Next Generation from People Who Know a Thing or Two (public library) — the wondrous 2002 anthology by artist and writer James L. Harmon, inspired by one of his own spiritual parents: Rilke and his timeless Letters to a Young Poet.

Gaitskill writes:

My advice here is very specific and practicable. It is advice I wish someone had given me as forcefully as I’m about to give it now: When your parents are dying, you should go be with them. You should spend as much time as you can. This may seem obvious; you would be surprised how difficult it can be. It is less difficult if you have a good relationship with the parent or, even if you don’t, if you’re old enough to have lost friends and to have seriously considered your own death. Even so, it may be more difficult than you think.

With the sensitive caveat that there exist people “to whom this general directive does not apply” and her advice is not meant as a rebuke to those people, Gaitskill addresses those of us raised by fallible parents who, in one way or another, failed dreadfully at the deepest task of parenting — unconditional love:

If you’re a young person who has had a bad relationship with your parent, it’s a nightmare of anger, confusion, and guilt. Even if you hate them, you may still not want to believe it’s happening… Even if your parents have been abusive, physically or emotionally, they are part of you in a way that goes beyond personality or even character. Maybe “beyond” isn’t the right word. They are part of you in a way that runs beneath the daily self. They have passed an essence to you. This essence may not be recognizable; your parents may have made its raw matter into something so different than what you have made of it that it seems you are nothing alike. That they have given you this essence may be no virtue of theirs — they may not even have chosen to do so. (It may not be biological either; all I say here I would say about adoptive as well as birth parents.)

Art by Ekua Holmes from The Stuff of Stars by Marion Dane Bauer.

Being with a dying parent, Gaitskill notes, is a way of honoring the fact — so basic yet so incomprehensible a fact — that they will soon be gone, and with them will go your experience of being their child in the way you have known, a fundamental way in which you have known yourself. At the heart of this dual recognition is “the hard truth that we know nothing about who we are or what our lives mean.” She writes:

Nothing makes this plainer than being in the presence of a dying person for any length of time. Death makes human beings seem like very small containers that are packed so densely we can only be aware of a fraction of what’s inside us from moment to moment. Being in the presence of death can break you open, disgorging feelings that are deeper and more powerful than anything you thought you knew. If you have had a loving, clear relationship with your parent, this experience probably won’t be quite as wrenching. There may in fact be moments of pure tenderness, even exaltation. But you might still have to watch your parent appear to break, mentally and physically, disintegrating into something you can no longer recognize. In some ways this is terrible — many people find it absolutely so. There is another side to it, though: In witnessing this seeming breakage, we are glimpsing the part of our parents that doesn’t translate in human terms, that which we know nothing about, and which the human container is too small to give shape to.

Art by Dorothy Lathrop, 1922. (Available as a print.)

Because any emotional experience we have when facing another is always an emotional experience we have within, and about, ourselves — especially if that other gave rise to this self — facing this supraknowable quality is facing the limits of our own self-knowledge. Gaitskill writes:

Knowing your feelings is hard too because there’s so much emotion, it’s hard to tell which is truest. Part of you might want to leave right away; part of you might want to stay forever. That’s why I advised that you stay “for as long as you can.” What that means will vary with each person, with the needs of the parent and the other relations. A day might be enough, or it might take a whole month. If it’s a prolonged situation, it might be good to leave for a few days and come back. Those decisions are so personal they are beyond the scope of my advice — except my advice to pay close attention to yourself. If you feel, To hell with this, I’m getting out, don’t worry — there’s room for that. Maybe in fact you should leave. But before you do, be sure that voice is not shouting down a truer one. When your parents die, you will never see them again. You might think you understand that, but until it happens, you don’t.

Art by Margaret C. Cook from a rare 1913 English edition of Walt Whitman’s Leaves of Grass. (Available as a print.)

In a sentiment on the surface contradictory but in fact consonant with the deeper meaning of what artist Louise Bourgeois inscribed into her lifelong diary in her old age — “You are born alone. You die alone. The value of the space in between is trust and love.” — Gaitskill concludes:

They say that you come into the world alone and that you leave alone too. But you aren’t born alone; your mother is with you, maybe your father too. Their presence may have been loving, it may have been demented, it may have been both. But they were with you. When they are dying, remember that. And go be with them.

Complement this fragment of Take My Advice — which also includes novelist Richard Powers on the most important attitude you can take toward your life and philosopher Martha Nussbaum on how to honor your inner world — with Richard Dawkins on the luckiness of death, Marcus Aurelius on embracing mortality as the key to living fully, and Zen Hospice Project founder Frank Ostaseski on the five life-redeeming invitation to extend in facing death, then revisit this tender illustrated meditation on the cycle of life.

Sirui has announced a new series of prime, autofocus lenses. The Swift lineup includes two focal lengths at launch – 20mm F2.4 and 40mm F2.2 – both of which are compact, lightweight offerings that are available in L, E and X mounts for just $149.

Unlike Viltrox’s similarly affordable 20mm F2.8 and 40mm F2.5 Air lenses, both Swift primes include useful on-lens controls. That includes a clicked aperture ring, a manual focus ring, a customizable Fn button and an AF/MF switch.

Sirui

Both of the Swift lenses are compatible with 58mm filters. A stepper motor drives autofocus in each lens, with Sirui promising minimal focus breathing. They each feature a nine-bladed aperture diaphragm. The company also says the lenses are weather-resistant, with protection against dust and splashes.

The 20mm F2.4 lens features an 11-element in 9-group optical design, with 2 aspherical, 2 ED and 2 HRI elements. It can focus as close as 0.2m (0.65′). Sirui doesn’t provide weights or measurements for the X-mount model, but the L-mount version weighs 212g (7.5oz), while the E-mount comes in at 207g (7.3oz). They measure 60.3mm (2.4″) and 62.3mm (2.5″) long, respectively.

Sirui

The 40mm F2.2 lens features a slightly different optical design, with 11 elements in 8 groups. That includes one aspherical lens and two ED elements. Its minimum focusing distance is 0.4m (1.3′). Its size and weight are very similar to the 20mm lens, with the E-mount version weighing 210g (7.4oz) and measuring 62.3mm (2.5″). The L-mount model weighs 215g (7.6oz) and measures 60mm (2.36″).

The new Swift 20mm F2.4 and 40mm F2.2 are available to pre-order now for $149.
