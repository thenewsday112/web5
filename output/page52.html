Scientists uncover genetic clues to how Chornobyl’s dogs survived

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

When people fled the Chornobyl nuclear disaster in 1986, they had to leave their pets behind.

Some dogs escaped the crews sent to kill them, and the free-roaming dogs there today likely descend at least partly from those pets.

Today, dogs living at the power plant are genetically distinct from dogs in Chornobyl town, just 10 miles (16 km) away. A new study suggests natural selection may help explain that gap.

Many genes near the stretches of DNA that caught the researchers’ attention help repair DNA, fight infection, or play a role in cancer.

The findings offer clues about how the dogs survived, though they don’t prove that these genes helped.

Megan Dillon, a postdoctoral research scholar at North Carolina State University’s College of Veterinary Medicine, led the work with Matthew Breen, a professor of genomics there.

A genetic divide

“While we can’t say for certain whether what we’ve found can be directly confirmed as selection, we do find these genomic signatures suggestive of selection,” Dillon said.

“When we dig deeper into those genomic regions, we find that these genes are involved in biologically relevant pathways that could allow them to overcome the effects of being exposed to toxicants in this environment.”

Earlier studies found a surprising divide between the two groups. Town dogs were genetically closer to dogs in Poland and Russia than to the plant dogs, even though free-breeding dogs across Eastern Europe live much farther apart.

Earlier research had examined breed ancestry, physical separation, and inbreeding without fully explaining the genetic divide.

A separate study also found no evidence that radiation had accelerated mutations in the plant dogs.

That left two main possibilities: genetic drift, where genes become more common by chance because some dogs happen to have more puppies, and natural selection.

With selection, dogs carrying genes that help them survive and reproduce pass those genes on more often.

Comparing the dogs’ DNA

Veterinarians collected samples in 2018 and 2019 while treating dogs at spay and vaccine clinics run by the Clean Futures Fund.

The team began with complete genomes, or full sets of DNA, from 106 dogs, now available in a public database.

Researchers then removed close relatives and dogs whose DNA looked more like the other group’s. That left 33 plant dogs and 30 town dogs.

Genetic drift has a stronger effect in smaller breeding populations, so the researchers estimated how many dogs contributed genes to each group.

The plant dogs didn’t consistently have a smaller breeding population, weakening the idea that a small group alone explained the gap.

Patterns in the DNA also helped the researchers trace the dogs’ population history.

The plant population appeared to start shrinking about 35 to 40 generations ago, around the time of the 1986 disaster and the culling that followed.

The authors think most of today’s plant dogs descend from pets in Pripyat, the workers’ city about two miles (three km) from the plant. Residents left the city 36 hours after the explosion.

An island comparison

The team turned to dogs from the Galápagos Islands to explore what isolation could explain. Those dogs had lived apart from other populations and faced an attempted cull, but they had never lived beside a ruined reactor.

This comparison included 250 dogs from eight groups, including village dogs from Poland and Russia.

On a standard measure of genetic difference, the plant dogs were about twice as far from each of three Galápagos groups as the town dogs were.

The island dogs were genetically closer to the town dogs and Europe’s village dogs than to the plant dogs. Isolation on islands hadn’t produced the same degree of difference.

A computer program also sorted all 250 dogs into groups based only on their DNA. It identified three clusters, including one made up entirely of power plant dogs.

Differences across chromosomes

The researchers then compared individual chromosomes, the packages that hold DNA. They expected random drift to affect the chromosomes broadly alike, but some stood out much more than others.

In the full-genome data, comparisons involving plant dogs varied about four times as much between chromosomes as comparisons involving the other groups.

Chromosomes 8, 14, 26, and 27 consistently showed the greatest differences.

On chromosome 8, the genetic difference between plant dogs and a purebred group was nearly 50% greater than their difference across the whole genome.

The plant dogs also retained their overall genetic variety, unlike the loss often seen when drift affects a small population.

Narrowing the search

Next, the team examined about 4.8 million places where dogs’ DNA commonly varies.

They compared plant dogs with town dogs, mixed-breed dogs, and 24 purebreds from eight breeds, including Chihuahuas and golden retrievers.

The plant dogs stood out at 1,138 spots, most of them packed into clusters. That pattern can develop when selection favors a particular stretch of DNA.

The researchers ran four more tests on each region to look for other signs of selection. Every region passed at least two tests, and 111 passed at least three.

What the genes do

The team identified 247 genes in or near those regions. Cancer-related pathways showed the strongest associations, including those linked to melanoma and cancers of the urinary and reproductive systems.

Other leading functions involved copying and repairing DNA, controlling when cells die, and managing immune and inflammatory responses.

These links make the genes worth studying, but they don’t show that the dogs resist cancer or other damage.

Other species in the exclusion zone have offered similar clues: birds showed signs of adapting to oxidative stress, a form of cell damage, while silver birch and evening primrose showed improved DNA repair.

In gray wolves, genes involved in DNA damage and immunity were active at different levels.

Did the dogs adapt?

The researchers still don’t know how the genetic differences arose.

“When we consider the toxic environment around Chornobyl, I can see why most people would think, ‘Surely there’s got to have been some consequence of living in that environment,'” Breen said.

“But what we’re saying is the consequence may not have gone as far as changing the dogs’ genome in order to adapt, it could be the ones that were able to survive the initial exposures had this level of underlying genetic diversity and variants to offer a higher degree of fitness,” Breen added.

“Obviously that’s speculation, and we don’t know exactly what’s happened.”

The study compared DNA without measuring the dogs’ health or their individual exposures. The team also lacks DNA from their ancestors before 1986, so it can’t connect any particular gene to a specific hazard.

Following the clues

The authors see dogs as useful stand-ins for studying the exposures people face.

As nuclear power expands, the researchers say understanding how long-term environmental exposures affect animals could help scientists assess risks following future accidents.

Future research will need to determine whether these genetic differences actually helped the dogs survive.

Until then, Chornobyl’s dogs offer an intriguing glimpse of how animal populations may respond to extreme environmental pressures.

The study is published in the journal Genes.

Michael Douglas Gives Rare Update on Jack Nicholson: 'I Still See Him'

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Michael Douglas has kept in touch with Jack Nicholson despite the latter’s step back from the limelight.

“I would have liked to have acted with Jack. He’s not working any more,” Douglas, 82, said during a recent event in Wales to discuss his One Helluva Ride memoir, per The Independent. “He’s well. He has disappeared. I know that.”

Nicholson, 89, previously starred in 1975’s One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest, which Douglas produced. He last acted in 2010’s How Do You Know? and has since largely resigned from the public eye.

“I still see him, but he is fine. He is up there by his house and walking around in his slippers,” Douglas quipped. “It happens. He deserves it. I understand. He is a few years older than me, but I understand.”

Jack Nicholson’s Life Out of the Spotlight: Time With Loved Ones and More

Nicholson made a rare public appearance at the milestone 50th anniversary celebration of Saturday Night Live in February 2025.

“Ladies and gentleman, Adam Sandler,” he said last year, introducing the SNL vet’s musical performance about the sketch comedy series.

Sandler, 60, even gushed about Nicholson’s attendance in his lyrical soliloquy.

“Yeah, baby! Let’s hear it for Jack, baby,” Sandler added during the show. “Jack made it out tonight. Love you, brother.”

Nicholson attended the show with his daughter Lorraine, whom he shares with ex Rebecca Broussard. (The prolific actor and Broussard, 63, are also parents of son Ray. He also shares daughter Jennifer with Sandra Knight, son Caleb with Susan Anspach, daughter Honey with Winnie Hollman and daughter Tessa with Jennine Gourin.)

“For my father, Lara acted as a bridge between him and his children. Suddenly, we were doing things we had never done before,” Lorraine, 36, wrote in a Vanity Fair essay published in January 2025. “In Aspen, we went whitewater rafting down the Colorado River. We cackled wildly while playing Parcheesi. In one particularly traitorous game of Uno, my brother and I kamakazied ourselves so that while we wouldn’t win, my father wouldn’t either.”

Nicholson dated Boyle, 56, from 1999 to 2004.

“I don’t know if he cried or if she cried, but I certainly did.
