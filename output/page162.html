Coinbase wants six futures contracts to stop expiring on October 26. The CFTC letter it leans on covers five of them by design, and its own footnote points away from the sixth.

The Division of Market Oversight issued Letter 26-29 on October 3, 2026. The CFTC announced it two days later, on October 5, in Release 9308-26. Coverage then focused on the headline that exchanges can now strip expiry dates from perpetual-style index futures. The Merkle flagged the relief's hard October 20 stop on October 19, and outlets like Finance Magnates and FinanceFeeds ran the same window. That coverage, however, predates what Coinbase filed next. This breakdown sets the October 19 filing beside the letter's text, because the two don't line up cleanly.

Meanwhile, the CFTC is working on retail crypto leverage through a different door. As NullTX's earlier breakdown shows, the actual-delivery ANPRM (2026-20888) takes comments until December 14. Perpetuals on registered exchanges skip that process. Instead, they move through staff letters and self-certification.

What Coinbase Actually Filed

Coinbase asked for the relief on October 1, and the Division granted it two days later. The letter says staff won't recommend enforcement if a DCM drops expiry dates from existing perpetual-style contracts, as long as the DCM meets eight conditions. First, it must gather feedback from holders of open positions. Next, it must give at least five calendar days' notice and let them close out under the old terms. It must also disclose the risks and change no other material term. Finally, it must file under the CFTC's rules, notify the Division and certify compliance.

Coinbase's Submission #2026-67 then names six contracts: US500, Tech100, Defense10, AI10, China10 and Coin50. It says everything except the expiry stays the same, and that the exchange knows of no substantive opposition. The contract list reached the CFTC by email on October 16.

Where the Waiver Stops: Four Checkpoints

1. The Asset-Class Limit

Letter 26-29 defines its scope by asset class. A footnote shuts out any perpetual-style contract referencing something other than a broad-based security index. Coin50, by contrast, follows the Coinbase 50 Index, and Rule 1148 in the filing itself calls it a futures contract based on a cryptocurrency. In other words, the letter's scope and Coin50's own rule text point in opposite directions.

2. The Letter That Ran Out

Crypto contracts did have their own relief. Letter 26-19, issued June 12 to Bitnomial and Coinbase Derivatives, covered digital commodities only and carried the same eight conditions. Even so, it says its positions expire on June 30, 2026. It rests on the Commission's May 29 order and its perpetuals policy statement.

3. The October 20 Cutoff

Letter 26-29 sets October 20, 2026 as the day its no-action positions expire. It doesn't say whether implementation itself must happen by then, or only the notice and filing. Coinbase filed on October 19, one day early, but picked October 26 to go live. That choice matters. Ten business days from October 19 lands on November 2, and Coinbase specifically asked to skip that wait. As a result, going live on October 26 depends on the relief still applying.

4. The Stay Alternative

Without relief, the Division's own words make the path clear. Letter 26-19 says certifications that remove expiry dates will generally draw a stay under Regulation 40.6(c). A stay then gives the Commission another 90 days to review and opens a 30-day public comment period.

No Public Response Yet

Neither side has explained the gap. Searches of news coverage and official channels turned up no statement from Coinbase or the CFTC on whether Coin50 sits inside Letter 26-29, on what authority covers it after October 20, or on why the filing's October 26 date falls after the letter's expiry. We also found no CFTC stay notice and no extension or amendment of Letter 26-29. Likewise, the Coinbase posts we found cover other perpetual launches, not this conversion.

That silence doesn't mean a problem exists. Coinbase may be relying on authority the documents don't mention, and the CFTC may read the letter's dates differently than we do. Still, the paper trail shows a crypto-based contract sitting under relief written for stock indexes, with a start date that falls after that relief expires.

Why October 26 Matters

Three developments would settle it: a CFTC stay notice, a new or amended staff letter, or a clarification from Coinbase Derivatives.

Whichever arrives first tells us whether the first U.S.-listed crypto index perpetual goes live on schedule or lands in a 90-day review.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has told prediction-market operators Novig and ProphetX not to list a set of sports event contracts the two companies had already self-certified, according to Front Office Sports. The agency also asked both firms to explain how the markets would comply with federal commodities law.

A CFTC spokesperson confirmed that letters had been sent to both companies. “Any derivatives contract offered by a CFTC-regulated exchange must be compliant with the Commodity Exchange Act,” the spokesperson said.

Micro contracts and scorigami

The two letters target different products. Novig was told that its micro contracts, such as a market on which player opens the scoring in a basketball game, are open to manipulation. ProphetX was told that its scorigami contracts, which settle on whether a game finishes with a scoreline that has never occurred in that sport, do not meet the Commodity Exchange Act’s definition of a swap.

Sources told Front Office Sports that the letters describe the filings as false self-certifications, the term used when an exchange certifies a contract that may not be permitted under the statute. Neither set of contracts had been listed, so no customer has traded on them.

ProphetX framed the request more narrowly. A company spokesperson said CFTC staff “requested that we voluntarily defer listing the Scorigami contracts pending further review, not that we withdraw them.” The company said it complied, has since supplied further analysis on the contracts’ compliance and economic utility, and remains in contact with agency staff. Novig did not immediately respond to a request for comment, the outlet reported.

A signal to the wider market

Under the current framework, exchanges can self-certify and list new contracts without prior approval, and the CFTC can step in afterward. The agency would not say if it has ever before told an operator to hold back a self-certified sports contract. One source described the letters as “kind of a warning shot” for the sector.

The move does not appear to be limited to two companies. Senior CFTC officials have also spoken with DraftKings and FanDuel and cautioned that listing micro contracts could bring the same response, according to the report. Other exchanges have voiced frustration with the agency’s stance, pointing out that many state regulators allow comparable in-game wagers at licensed sportsbooks.

The letters follow a separate round of CFTC inquiries last month into incentive programs at Kalshi, Polymarket, Rothera and Novig. They also come ahead of a final rule the agency is expected to issue after its June proposal, a 267-page notice that would restrict in-game props and limit markets on high school sports. The proposal sits inside a wider dispute over how sports contracts are treated under federal rules, with several states arguing those products should fall under gambling law.

Leagues have pushed for limits

One source said the Novig and ProphetX markets were exactly the type of event contracts professional leagues have objected to most. On October 19, the NBA tightened the rules on player props and micro bets for its sportsbook partners, among them markets on who scores a game’s first basket, Yogonet reported. Those league rules cover traditional sportsbooks, not prediction markets.

It is not clear what the CFTC would do if either company went ahead and listed the contracts. Both firms have the chance to make their case to the agency, and the outcome is likely to shape how far other exchanges push into single-play sports markets before the final rule lands. Sports already account for most of the activity on the largest platforms, as shown in recent data on NFL trading volume across prediction markets.

What’s Downshifting? The Trend That’s Helping Homeowners Do More With Less

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Sometimes less IS more. At least, that’s what one home and lifestyle trend would have you believe.

A house can demand more time and money than homeowners expect. Spare bedrooms fill up with things nobody uses, the yard needs another afternoon of work, and there’s always something that needs fixing. Downshifting is one way homeowners are trying to change that.

Here’s what downshifting means, how it’s different from downsizing, and whether it can actually save money.

What Downshifting Means and How to Get Started at Home

Downshifting is pretty much what it sounds like. It’s a decision to slow things down a bit, spend less, and get rid of some of the things that take up too much time or space around the house.

Permaculture writer David Holmgren covers the idea in his book RetroSuburbia, which describes downshifting as “a conscious choice and action to shift to a lifestyle with less consumption, complexity, stress and status seeking.” The idea is to make everyday life a little easier to manage, whether that’s by cutting expenses or having fewer responsibilities.

It sounds a lot like downsizing, but the two aren’t quite the same. Downsizing usually means moving to a smaller house or getting rid of belongings. Downshifting doesn’t necessarily involve moving at all.

One family might trade a large house for a smaller place that needs less maintenance. Another might stay right where they are, stop buying things they don’t need, or spend less time working on the house.

Homeowners can start by looking at what’s taking up the most time or money. Maybe that’s a garage full of things that haven’t been used in years, or a spare bedroom that’s become a storage room. Clearing out those spaces can mean less to clean and look after.

Other changes can be pretty small, too. Borrowing a tool instead of buying one, fixing furniture rather than replacing it, or starting a small vegetable garden are all examples. The Environmental Protection Agency also recommends buying less and reusing things to help reduce waste.

Even looking for ways to lower utility bills, like sealing drafty windows or keeping up with heating and cooling maintenance, can be part of downshifting.

How Downshifting Can Affect Household Expenses and Daily Life

One reason people consider downshifting is to save money. Buying fewer things and using less energy can certainly help, but moving to a smaller home doesn’t automatically mean smaller bills.

“New homeowners are often surprised by the costs of owning property,” according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The agency recommends putting together a budget that includes property taxes, insurance, repairs, and other expenses beyond the mortgage.

There are some practical things to consider, too. A smaller house might not have enough room for a growing family, and moving farther away could mean a longer commute. Even growing vegetables or repairing things around the house takes time.

Still, for some households, having fewer things to pay for and maintain can leave more time for family, hobbies, or just enjoying the house they already have.

And that’s really the point of downshifting. It doesn’t have to mean selling a house or getting rid of everything. Sometimes it’s as simple as deciding the house doesn’t need another project or another piece of furniture.

Musician Allie Teilz says becoming a mom made her “bigger, not smaller”

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s dinnertime at Allie Teilz’s house, and the music is already on. Perhaps a film score courtesy of Italian classicist Morricone, maybe some Italian disco easing in behind it. One of the twins, age 3, is already dancing to it. The phones go in the ceramic cow on the counter for the remainder of the evening, where her almost-8-year-old likes them. There’s a projector, a record player, and a wall of VHS tapes with Yellow Submarine and The Land Before Time on heavy rotation. Teilz calls it, half-joking, a ’90s-electronics household. 

She is, for the record, impossibly cool. A vinyl DJ and producer who came up in Los Angeles nightlife sharing booths with the likes of Grace Jones and Daft Punk, she now scours thrift stores and estate sales for those VHS tapes and describes herself as “a mother first and an artist second.” She is a mother of four who weaves those two versions of herself authentically into one. 

We talked for an hour, and I could have gone three more. Somewhere in the course of our call she made me wish, for a second, that I could do the whole motherhood thing over again. My kids are grown and nearly grown, and I am not in the market for a redo. Still, the childhood she is building for hers, steeped in art and music and curiosity, had me calculating.

Her debut album, Come to Mommy, out now, is the sound of her reclaiming the artist within after the babies arrived. It’s atmospheric, irresistibly dance-able and laced with an electronic vibe that’s both throwback and futuristic. The title turns “come to daddy” on its head. The culture asks what Daddy wants. Teilz would rather ask what Mommy wants, and she answers without flinching. “Mommy wants joy,” she says. “She wants to have fun and dance. She wants pleasure and joy.”

We can let motherhood deepen the art

Teilz doesn’t treat her kids as the thing she makes art around. She treats them as the reason her art got deeper. “Becoming their mom,” she says, “only made everything bigger, not smaller.” Motherhood taught her that “the deepest creativity can come from having very little time and knowing exactly what matters.” 

There’s a surrender in becoming a parent, she says, a soul handing itself over to lead someone else through life, and for her it cracked the work open instead of closing it down. It “liberated me in creation,” she says, and gave her “a deeper well to pull from.” She has taken to calling motherhood “the highest form of art I’ve ever experienced.”

It changed what she wants to make, too. She looks at her own work through her children’s eyes now and holds it to a single north star. What do I want to see more of in the world? Certainly not  “AI art and lobotomy chic,” her encapsulation of the soulless, machine-driven work she’s watched creep in. She wants her kids to have what she had, the feeling of hearing Talking Heads for the first time and being free, the permission to flail around in  an imaginary version of David Byrne’s giant suit and stay in “that beautiful state of play.” Come to Mommy is her attempt to put that feeling back into the world, and she credits her kids as the inspiration. “They’re the greatest teachers,” she says.

We don’t have to choose a singular version of ourselves

Most women are familiar with the shift that happens when you become a mother. The self gets smaller to make room for everyone else, and for a while Teilz felt it, sliding toward the motherhood she’d watched other women vanish into. The mom groups where everyone gave and gave without ever taking their own needs into consideration. The careers set down and never picked back up. The inability to truly answer the question, “Who am I?” She calls out the insidious culprits that get woven into culture. “We’re indoctrinated by The Giving Tree and The Rainbow Fish,” she says, the children’s books that sell self-erasure as generosity. Make yourself a stump and you’ll be loved. Give away your sparkles and you’ll be accepted. “That’s such a dangerous thing,” Teilz observes.

For a long stretch, her own priorities became submerged. What pulled her back was simple. Boundaries. A flip phone for the hours she refuses to be reachable. She points to a line she saw online, “have boundaries or get an autoimmune disorder,” and says that her health was what finally made her stop. “My kids don’t need a mom who disappears into anything,” she says. “Even motherhood.”

We get to decide what their childhood feels like

Teilz builds her kids’ childhood the way she builds a record, out of specific, deliberate, slightly magical details. When her daughter earned a leader award at school and got to choose the celebration, she asked to make “ice cream mountains” with scoops stacked into a castle on parchment paper, modeled on the shop Teilz loved as a girl. Ridiculous for the sake of being ridiculous. The memories Teilz recalls most fondly from her own childhood aren’t the cruise or the big trip. They’re the one-bedroom apartment and her mom letting the kids pick a favorite ice cream before the first day of school. Now she makes the small, arguably more impactful kind of memories for her own crew.

There’s also a healthy helping of wonder. The kids get sent out to the garden to pull parsley or a tomato so their hands are involved in making dinner–she’s been teaching them to grow things from seed to fruition for years, because watching a baby artichoke push up from a seed you watered is the kind of small miracle she wants them to appreciate. Books work the same way. She still talks about a woman she once watched on the subway, reading beside her small daughter while the car churned around them, the two of them just reading, an image that stuck with her long before she had kids of her own. Now one of her proudest moments is her daughter climbing onto the couch to get lost in Harry Potter. “Stay curious about the world,” she tells other mothers. “Keep reading. Stay interested in what matters to you.” 

None of this came easily. Come to Mommy was written through a brutal stretch that included a traumatic birth, one that nearly cost Teilz and one of her sons their lives, and the album is partly the record of her finding the way back. The final track, Back From the Dead, was written for the twin she almost lost. It’s her way of telling all of her kids that they can come back from the worst thing. Making art out of it was a way to process, and she says, proof to her children that you can take something that breaks you and “make something beautiful out of the ashes.”

Near the end, I painted an image for her. A mom pulls into the driveway and sits in the car a minute before going in, the album still playing. What does she want that woman to carry inside with her? Teilz didn’t hesitate. She wants her to “steal the fire” of her own passion, to think of the song she danced hardest to at her best friend’s 13th birthday, to feel held, to have a reason to dance. Mostly she wants her to feel more like herself.

Not many people tell women that the self doesn’t have to shrink to make room for mothering. Teilz spends a whole album saying it.

There was a time when buying a cheap new car was relatively simple. The answer might have involved crank windows, manual door locks, an AM/FM radio, and seats upholstered in something that looked suspiciously like industrial carpeting.

Today, the idea of a truly low-budget, no-frills vehicle is getting hard to find. Even the least expensive new cars come with touchscreens, driver-assistance systems, connectivity features, multiple airbags, and enough electronic wizardry to make a 1990s luxury car look like a farm tractor by comparison.

That raises a reasonable question: Can you still buy a genuinely inexpensive, basic vehicle in 2026? The short answer is yes, but “basic” no longer means what it used to.

The No-Frills Car Isn’t Really No-Frills Anymore

Before we talk about what no-frills, low-budget vehicles are available, it’s important that we set a baseline for what that kind of car is in today’s market. 

Modern safety and fuel economy technology are two of the biggest reasons today’s inexpensive vehicles don’t look anything like their ancestors. A new car sold in the United States must meet extensive federal safety requirements. That means airbags, stability control, rearview backup cameras, crash protection, and a long list of other requirements.

Consumers have also developed expectations. Try selling a new car without air conditioning today and see how quickly the internet catches fire. Nobody wants to return to the days when a buyer had to choose between air conditioning and an AM/FM radio. Budget buyers today expect power windows, remote locks, smartphone connectivity, and decent climate control.

The result is an interesting middle ground. Today’s cheapest cars aren’t truly bare-bones. Automakers have figured out how to put a surprising amount of technology into relatively inexpensive vehicles without necessarily making them luxury cars.

The $20,000 Car Is a Unicorn

For years, the sub-$20,000 new car was the entry point for budget-conscious buyers. Models like the Mitsubishi Mirage, Nissan Versa, Chevrolet Spark, and various tiny hatchbacks gave shoppers an inexpensive way into a new vehicle.

Many of those cars are gone now. The market has steadily moved toward larger vehicles, more equipment, higher prices, and greater complexity. That doesn’t mean inexpensive cars have disappeared. It means the definition of inexpensive has moved upward. A buyer looking for a new vehicle in the low-$20,000 range can still find options, but those vehicles aren’t exactly stripped-down A-to-B appliances anymore.

Even inexpensive cars now tend to have infotainment screens, Bluetooth, backup cameras, automatic emergency braking, lane-departure warnings, and other features that were once the exclusive territory of expensive luxury models. 

“More Expensive” Is Not Actually a Problem

The economy in the U.S. has changed significantly. Not just from COVID and various political changes, but also from the general mindset of most Americans. When we compare pricing from 30 or even just 15 years ago, we often mentally skew the results due to our perspective of that change. It’s important to remember that, thanks to inflation, a dollar from 2011 is not a dollar in 2026. That ‘11 dollar is now $1.49. Inflation compounds and that 15 years has meant a 49 percent decrease in value for our money. 

Knowing that, we can compare the average new vehicle purchase price (currently about $51,000) today with the average 15 years ago ($30,500). Then we can compare those values to the average household income from both eras: $50,000 in 2011 and $87,500 in 2026. Doing that, we find that things haven’t changed much. 

The average new vehicle price seems very high compared to 15 years ago, but it was about 60 percent of the average household income in 2011 and is still that for 2026. 

Are Used Cars Where the Real Bargains Live?

Yes and no. If your definition of low-budget means “I need reliable transportation and I don’t care whether it has the latest technology,” the used market is still where you’ll get a good deal. But a good deal doesn’t always mean good value. Shopping for a used vehicle is a whole discussion unto itself and countless books, videos, and articles have been made detailing the do’s and don’ts.

What’s important and relevant here is to realize that while a three- to seven-year-old compact car can provide much of the transportation value that new economy cars once offered, it will come with some downsides. The trick is to shop based on condition and ownership history rather than simply finding the cheapest four-door vehicle.

What’s more, the used car market has changed significantly since the COVID blip. Up until just before the shutdowns began, the used car market remained relatively steady in pricing versus the new car market, with long-term changes largely following inflation. During COVID, used car prices jumped by about 49 percent as demand for vehicles collided with a lack of new vehicles due to closures. Afterward, as new cars began to once again appear on dealership lots, the used car market eventually saw less demand and adjusted accordingly.

That adjustment, however, wasn’t back down to pre-COVID numbers. It’s still 17 percent higher than it was. And it seems to have decided to hold steady there. The reason used cars are much more expensive is, again, supply and demand. This time between used car availability and demand from buyers wanting them. There are fewer used cars being sold because owners are keeping their used cars longer. This disparity and its higher price result has been a big driver in new car sales for the past few years. 

So, Can You Still Buy One?

Yes, but you have to adjust your expectations. If your definition of “no-frills” is “nothing I don’t need,” the new-car market still has possibilities. If your definition is “give me an engine, four wheels, a steering wheel, and absolutely nothing else,” you’re probably shopping used or waiting on the upcoming Slate pickup truck.

For now, here are the five lowest-cost new vehicles you can buy in 2026, ranked by base model price tag and not including shipping and destination charges:

  1. Hyundai Venue, $20,550 – the subcompact size and lower-cost interior materials are the primary reason for this car’s low price tag. 
  2. Chevrolet Trax, $21,700 – as a subcompact SUV, the Trax has a long-lived engine that’s cheap to build and a lack of interior amenities to keep its price low.
  3. Kia K4, $22,290 – previously the Forte, the K4 has long been Kia’s lowest-cost vehicle and has kept competitive through high volume global sales.
  4. Nissan Kicks, $22,430 – the Kicks replaces the Versa as Nissan’s budget vehicle, but at a higher cost due to its SUV styling.
  5. Nissan Sentra, $22,600 – a long-lived and well-accepted sedan, the Sentra is now the lowest-cost car in Nissan’s lineup.

From Chip Lab to Center Stage

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


Many engineers are more comfortable working behind the scenes developing technology than speaking to consumers about products. But Preethi Ashwath has learned to take center stage, talking about the “hearable” and wearable devices she has had a hand in developing as an analog integrated circuit designer in the consumer electronics division at Analog Devices of Wilmington, Mass.

The IEEE member is one of the few women who work in the specialized field, where about 11 percent are female, according to career platform Zippia.

Preethi Ashwath

Ashwath stepped out from behind the scenes last year to meet with attendees at the Consumer Electronics Show (CES) in Las Vegas. She demonstrated Analog’s new earbuds, which track people’s heart and lung health through vibrations in their ear canals.

After she demonstrated her ability to explain the technology to consumers in simple terms, Analog selected her to represent the company at other events. She says she has “come to see speaking as part of contributing to the field, rather than something separate from technical work.”

She also gives technical talks on various aspects of electrostatic discharge (ESD) and other topics, as well as participates in panel discussions, she says, because they “reach engineers well beyond my immediate team.”

Ashwath is an active volunteer with the IEEE Circuits and Systems Society.

“Whether through designing semiconductor products, publishing technical articles, reviewing conference papers, or speaking at industry events, I try to contribute to the growth of the engineering community and the advancement of the field,” she says.

Discovering circuit design at Intel

Ashwath, who grew up in Bengaluru, India, learned about electronics engineering from her high school physics tutor.

That introduction led her to take electronics as one of her core courses in her final years there. At the time, Ashwath’s older sister was pursuing an engineering degree.

“Between my interest in electronics and seeing her already on that path, it became clear to me that engineering was the way to go,” Ashwath says.

After earning a bachelor’s degree in electronics and communications engineering at the SJB Institute of Technology, in Bengaluru, she wanted to pursue a career in very-large-scale integration. But that changed once she moved to the United States and began her master’s program in electrical engineering at the University of California, Irvine.

She discovered her passion for circuits and devices during an internship at Intel in Santa Clara, Calif. She learned about circuit design, package design, and reliability verification tools such as Ansys Totem.

“Seeing every layer of the chip development process gave me a more complete picture of what goes into building a semiconductor product,” she says. “Through that experience, I realized I felt most connected to design.

“It was the hard work and creativity that drew me in. You start with a specification, build a circuit, simulate it, then wait for the chip to come back from fabrication. When your design works, it’s a real feeling of satisfaction. It teaches you patience and perseverance.”

After attaining her master’s degree, she was hired by Intel as an analog design engineer. She gained experience in mixed-signal circuit design before joining Analog. In her current role, she works on consumer hearables such as earbuds and wearables including fitness trackers and health monitors.

Her main focus is designing ESD pad rings, the borders of specialized circuits built around a microchip’s outermost edge to protect it from the discharge, which can damage circuit paths or introduce latent defects that lead to failures later in the chip’s life cycle.

“On a chip with multiple voltage domains, you have to plan how the protection structures are placed and connected across the whole pad ring—and get it right,” Ashwath says. “A mistake there could cause a chip to fail in the field in ways that are very hard to debug afterward.

“It’s detailed work that requires understanding both the device physics and how the whole chip fits together.”

Part of the job is designing and creating an integrated circuit’s building blocks, including ESD protection structures and other mixed-signal blocks. Ashwath conducts rigorous checks to verify they work as intended before being integrated into a chip.

She collaborates with ESD advisors, layout designers, and the digital team that designs the chip’s digital logic.

“I work with them to make sure my analog circuits and their digital blocks work together correctly,” she says. “No two days look exactly the same.”

In 2024 she was recognized with the company’s Impact Award for her “exceptional work on a critical project with significant cross-functional business impact.”

Presenting the final product

Ashwath was selected to represent Analog at events after a senior leader encouraged her to ask for high-visibility opportunities, she says.

“Most of my time is spent on designing circuits and simulations,” she says. “At CES, I stood in front of customers, engineers, and members of the press, explaining our technology and answering questions on the spot.”

The earbuds she demonstrated monitor minute vibrations from a user’s heart and lungs, according to the Analog website. The vibrations can detect arrhythmia, a leaky heart valve, or fluid in the lungs.

“Hearables are evolving from playing back audio to becoming health-monitoring platforms,” she says. “The ear turns out to be a good location for picking up physiological signals, so these devices can capture vital signs while someone is simply wearing them normally.

“The hard part is achieving high signal quality. The signals you’re trying to detect are very small and buried in the noise from the person’s movements and the surrounding environment.

“That’s where analog front-end design becomes critical. Extracting a meaningful signal from a noisy real-world environment is fundamentally an analog problem.”

Being face-to-face with customers at the conference was new to her.

“What struck me most was seeing how people react to the technology,” she says. “When you’re designing a block, you’re focused on specifications and margins. At CES, after explaining what the product does, I had a better understanding of why it matters to them.

“That connection between the circuit work and the final product is something you don’t always see from the design bench.”

Since the CES, Ashwath has presented other technologies she’s worked on. At the annual National Association of Music Merchants show in Anaheim, Calif., she demonstrated the A2B Automotive Audio Bus to the audio industry. The bus uses a low-cost unshielded twisted-pair cable to carry multichannel digital audio, control data, synchronized clock signals, and power in fewer than 50 microseconds, according to Analog.

“IEEE connects you with academia, which you don’t get much exposure to once you’re in industry. That connection between industry and academic work is valuable in both directions.”

At the company’s Global Technology Conference—an internal event held in Boston that draws engineers from the company’s more than 50 sites around the world—Ashwath led a demonstration of its solutions for augmented reality smart glasses.

“Each of these events has a different audience,” she says. “Learning to explain my technical work to different people has become a skill I’ve acquired.”

The most rewarding part of her job, she says, is seeing the end result of her work.

“You spend months on a design, and there’s a long wait between finishing it and finding out whether your instincts were right,” she says. “When the measurements match what you simulated, that’s the most satisfying moment in this job.

“The other part is knowing my work ends up in a real product. The circuits I design go into devices people actually use every day.”

Giving back to the community

Like many engineers, Ashwath joined IEEE to access research published in the IEEE Xplore Digital Library. It keeps her current in a field that keeps moving, she says.

During her first year of membership, in 2021, she discovered other perks including networking and educational opportunities.

“IEEE connects you to a community outside your own company,” she says. “Attending lectures and technical talks exposes you to approaches and problems you wouldn’t encounter in your own work.”

She eventually began seeking out volunteer opportunities, specifically with IEEE conferences and journals, because they are “how you stay current with what’s happening in circuits and systems beyond your own day-to-day work,” she says.

“As an analog designer,” she says, “being connected to that broader community matters. The field moves, and you need a way to keep learning from it.”

She serves as a peer reviewer of papers for several IEEE conferences including the IEEE International Symposium on Circuits and Systems and the IEEE International Midwest Symposium on Circuits and Systems. ISCAS is the flagship conference of the IEEE Circuits and Systems Society. MWSCAS is the oldest U.S. conference on the technologies.

“Someone has to assess whether submitted work is technically sound and worth presenting,” she says, “and that only happens if practicing engineers are willing to put in the time.”

The combination of learning from the community and contributing to it is what makes her membership worthwhile, she says.

“Analog design is a field where you’re always learning. Just when you feel you understand one architecture, there’s another one to learn,” she says. “Being an active learner isn’t optional in this field; it’s the requirement, and being part of the IEEE community helps with that.

“IEEE also connects you with academia, which you don’t get much exposure to once you’re in industry.

“Reviewing papers means reading what researchers are working on, which is often quite different from how the same problems get approached in a product environment. That connection between industry and academic work is valuable in both directions.”

How to be more productive: 6 tips to help you get things done

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Do you feel like you're always behind? Learn how to be more productive with 6 strategies for better focus, less procrastination, and calmer days.

You start your day with a plan: clear your to-do list, get ahead, maybe even sign off early. Instead, your attention gets pulled in every direction. Back-to-back calls, a stream of emails, countless tabs open at once. Next thing you know, the day's almost over, your list is still full, and you're left wondering how you’re supposed to get anything done. 

Busyness and productivity can look identical from the outside, but they’re not the same. You can stay busy all day tackling small, urgent-feeling tasks without ever touching the work that really counts. Being productive means doing the tasks that matter, which requires staying intentional about where your time and energy go. 

So what's getting in the way of your productivity, and what can you do about it? Here’s a closer look at the everyday friction that can slow you down, plus a few shifts to help you make the most of each day.

 

What gets in the way of productivity?

Most productivity drains are minor, routine points of friction that are easy to miss, yet they add up fast. A few usual suspects: 

  • Context switching: Research shows that every jump between tasks costs you the time it takes to mentally reset, even when it feels instant.

  • Constant interruptions: Responding to notifications and drop-in requests can feel productive, but it turns your attention to other people's priorities.

  • Unclear priorities: When everything feels urgent, you often default to whatever's quickest instead of what matters most.

  • Running on empty: Skipping breaks seems like it saves time, but it slows you down later and wears down your focus. 

  • Procrastination: When a task feels overwhelming or unclear, it’s tempting to reach for something more manageable instead of starting the job you need to do. 

How to be more productive: 6 daily habits and strategies

Good news: you can reduce these points of friction without turning your whole day upside down. The tips below aren’t a set system, just a few ideas you can implement on your own terms. Start with one or two, and notice what feels different. 

1. Choose your top priority before you start the day

Decide what would make today count before you open your inbox. Not five things, just one that matters most. Write it down somewhere visible, then dedicate the first stretch of your day to that task before anything else pulls your focus away. 

💙 Start your day mindfully with this Finding Focus practice on Calm. 

2. Finish one task before you start the next

Multitasking may feel efficient, but your brain can’t really do two things at once. It's switching fast between them, and each switch costs you a little focus and energy. Over a full day, those costs add up.

Try working in single-task blocks. Close the extra tabs, silence your phone, and stay with one task until it's done or you hit a natural stopping point. You'll often finish faster than if you’d juggled three tasks at once.

💙 Learn more with Jay Shetty’s Settle Down by Single-Tasking session on Calm. 

 

3. Cut down your digital distractions

The apps and platforms you use are built to stretch your attention in a dozen directions. Every ping invites you to switch tasks, and you’re driven to take the bait without thinking twice. 

You don't have to go off-grid to take back control. Check your email at two or three set times instead of leaving it open. Turn off notifications you don't need, and put your phone in another room during focused work. If one site keeps tempting you, a browser blocker can hold the line.

💙 Explore Digital Distractions from the 7 Days of Focus series on Calm.

4. Work in short bursts, then take a proper break

Your focus isn't a tap you can leave running. If you push too long without pausing, your attention frays, mistakes creep in, and even small tasks can start to feel hard. 

Try working in stretches of about 25 minutes, then step away to stretch, breathe, or look out a window. It’s also worth protecting your downtime in the evenings and on weekends, so you're not completely drained by mid-week.

💙 Dr. Alex Pang makes the case for smarter rest in The Power of Rest, a Calm masterclass. 

5. Batch similar tasks together

Your to-do list is probably a mix of very different kinds of work. A report needs deep focus. A stack of emails or calls may not need any. Some work requires creativity, while some mainly requires attention to detail. Tackling tasks in a random order means starting cold, over and over.

But if you batch similar tasks, you can move through them while you're already warmed up for that type of work. Keep your quick replies to a couple of set windows instead of scattered across the day, and stack your calls so they run one after another. Once you're in the groove of one kind of work, staying there beats starting over.

6. Timebox your most important tasks

A task tends to stretch to fill whatever time you give it. Timeboxing flips that. You pick a task, decide up front how long it gets—say 30 minutes—then stop when the time runs out, whether you're finished or not. That cap keeps one email from swallowing a whole morning, and stops you from polishing something long past the point of diminishing returns.

It also takes the guesswork out of what comes next, since you've chosen in advance. Block the slot on your calendar and set a timer. When the box closes, decide consciously whether the task gets another round.

How to be more productive FAQs

How do I train myself to be more productive?

You can train yourself to be more productive by gradually incorporating new helpful habits, like choosing a top priority for the day, single-tasking, and reducing digital distractions. Start with one small change and repeat it until it feels natural. One well-known study found that a new behavior took an average of 66 days to feel automatic—ranging from 18–254 days depending on the person—so give yourself time to adjust. 

Do people with ADHD struggle with productivity?

Yes, people with ADHD often struggle with productivity, and it’s not about effort or willpower. ADHD involves differences in executive function, the mental skills you use to start tasks, plan, and hold attention. This is why even starting tasks you want to do can feel difficult. The strategies in this article are still useful, though you may need to adapt them to what works for your brain. 

💙 If ADHD is getting in the way of your work, here’s more on why that happens and how to deal with it. 

What is productivity peacocking?

Productivity peacocking is when you put more energy into looking busy than into doing meaningful work. Think replying to messages instantly instead of finishing the task at hand, or reorganizing your to-do list for the third time instead of crossing items off. 

What time of day are people most productive?

There's no single best time of day to work, since it depends on your body clock and when your energy naturally rises. Instead of trying to follow someone else's schedule, notice when you feel most alert, and dedicate that window to your most demanding work.

Is it possible to be more productive without working longer hours?

Yes, it's possible to be more productive without working longer hours, since a lot of time gets lost to switching tasks and reacting to interruptions. Try protecting focused blocks, sticking to one task at a time, and taking proper breaks, since a tired brain works slowly and rest can help you get more done.

How does stress affect productivity?

Stress affects productivity by making it harder to focus, plan, and think clearly. When you're stressed, your brain pulls resources away from the areas that handle focus and memory. Bring your stress down and calm your mind with a few minutes of slow breathing.

What's the best way to stop getting distracted while working?

One of the most effective ways to stop getting distracted while working is to remove the distraction before you start. Silence notifications, close the extra tabs, and place your phone out of reach so you're not relying on willpower in the moment. When your mind wanders anyway, and it will, bring it back without giving yourself a hard time.

Can meditation really help me be more productive?

Yes, meditation can help you be more productive, though the effect is indirect. In one randomized study, a two-week mindfulness course improved people's working memory and reduced how often their minds wandered mid-task. 

You don't need long sessions, and even a few minutes a day of focusing your attention on your breath works the same mental muscle you use to stay on track while working.
