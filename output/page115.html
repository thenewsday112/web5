Louvre Signs Deal with Unions, Ending Threat of Continued Strikes

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As nationwide protests continue to loom across France, with the next action planned for Saturday, October 31, at least one social grievance has been addressed at the country’s top museum. On Thursday, the Louvre in Paris signed an agreement with unions who had threatened to strike for months, reports Le Figaro and AFP.

“The signature of the agreement amounts to a positive step for the Louvre,” a museum representative told AFP.

A few months after last year’s daytime robbery at the Louvre emptied the museum of over $100 million worth of its crown jewels, a broad coalition of French unions went on strike and, until this week’s deal, had pledged to continue protesting over poor working conditions, insufficient personnel, and the handling of museum renovations. The strikes began in December and forced the museum to close entirely for a total of four days and partially for another four days. Meanwhile, unions maintained an official “notice” to continue protests as needed, signaling they were not backing down and could block the museum at any time. All told, the dispute with unions is one of the longest face-offs of its kind for the Louvre.

As part of the new deal, the Louvre pledged to hire 50 new security personnel in 2027, or about 100 new hires in total by 2028, according to Le Quotidien de l’Art and other French reports. Additionally, the museum will improve pay for certain categories of workers, including a €50 ($56) monthly raise for security staff.

“The more of us there are carrying out our surveillance duties, the more we will deter criminals from targeting our institution,” Christian Galani, a representative of the French union CGT, told AFP.

The museum is in the throes of raising funds for an over one-billion-dollar renovation that will include security upgrades, the preservation of its aging infrastructure that has fallen into disrepair, and a separate, new entry and gallery dedicated to the Mona Lisa. However, there is disagreement about the cost and scope of the major renovation, and union representatives want a say in how it is handled. “We will quickly ask for a meeting on the management and prioritization of the construction works. We still have very little visibility on the safety and security plan announced in November 2025,” Alexis Fritche, leader of the CFDT-Culture union, told Le Quotidien de l’Art.

Following the crown jewel theft a year ago, Louvre president Laurence des Cars stepped down amid staff criticism that she used a top-down management style and ignored their concerns about the building’s disrepair and lack of personnel, while spending on projects they considered lavish. The arrival of the museum’s newly appointed president, Christophe Leribault, seems to have calmed some of those tensions.

One of the most common arguments we hear in favor of choosing larger cartridges for hunting big game is that a magnum cartridge will offer margin for error. When considering cartridges like 6.5 Creedmoor vs. .300 Win. Mag., the .300 has more energy, drives a bigger bullet faster, and, we assume, will do more damage than the 6.5. That means that if we don’t hit exactly where we are aiming, we are more likely to still kill the animal cleanly by choosing the magnum. At face value, the logic is sound. But how does it hold up when we start examining what the bullets actually do downrange? You might be surprised.

Smaller Cartridges Vs. Magnums

Magnums like the .300 Win. Mag. are popular, but do they really give you margin for error? Tyler Freel

There are two schools of thought that commonly divide hunters when it comes to choosing cartridges. One opts for milder cartridges that have lower recoil and are generally easier and more pleasant to shoot well. The other wants the extra power to cover their bases. Everyone, of course, falls somewhere on a spectrum, and the categories aren’t mutually exclusive. 

Small Cartridge Theory

I’ve written a lot about why I think smaller cartridges are often the right choice for big game, and it generally boils down to two observations. First, with thoughtful bullet selection, I haven’t noticed a dramatic difference between the wound characteristics of smaller and larger cartridges — and the smaller rounds have produced consistently satisfactory results. Second, milder cartridges are more pleasant to shoot and, despite having no real limitations on the cartridges I have access to, that’s what I reach for consistently. I have the rifles and ammo to torch a hundred rounds of .300 PRC in a range session practicing positional shooting but, realistically, I’m going to pick a 6.5 Creedmoor, 6 Creed, or 22 ARC to do that. Practice with smaller cartridges can also benefit how you’ll shoot magnums, but there’s a lot of value in hunting with the cartridge that you train with.

Proponents of smaller cartridges will point out that they deliver enough terminal performance to get the job done but are, generally, much easier to shoot well. Tyler Freel

Large Cartridge Theory

You’ll commonly hear hunters argue that you should choose the largest cartridge that you can handle without flinching — especially for larger game. Lots of hunters consider this basic conventional wisdom, and it’s rooted in the belief that a larger cartridge, bullet, or more kinetic energy will result in a larger wound and will kill the animal more quickly. This makes up for some degree of shooting error, or will allow more forgiveness on less-than-ideal shot angles. Essentially, choosing a more powerful cartridge is viewed as cheap insurance. 

The .300 Win. Mag. works very well on big game like moose, even beyond 300 or 400 yards, so it’s easy to understand why people like it. Tyler Freel

Real-World Results

There are many ways that bigger cartridges like the .300 Win. Mag. outperform smaller ones like the 6.5 Creedmoor. When choosing something for yourself, you need to know what the real benefits are, and decide whether they actually apply to you and your hunting. What does that look like in terms of margin for error? That’s exactly what we took a look at in the video above. 

I took a 6.5 Creedmoor and .300 Win. Mag. to the range and fired the same line of factory ammo — Federal Fusion Tipped — from each into identical blocks of Clear Ballistics gel. This gel isn’t a one-to-one representation of flesh, but it is a homogeneous, consistent medium in which we can get a side-by-side comparison of two bullets. The blocks were placed at 200 yards with a Garmin chronograph positioned about 15 yards in front of the target to record impact velocity. The Fusion Tipped bullets were 140 grains and 180 grains for the 6.5 CM and .300 Win. Mag., respectively. The rifles were the same except for different barrel lengths — 18 inches for the 6.5 CM and 22 inches for the .300. Impact velocities were recorded as 2,233 fps and 2,526 fps, with the advantage going to the .300.

As expected, the .300 Win. Mag. load delivered higher velocity and more energy at the target than the 6.5 Creedmoor. The block was knocked off the table and was clearly hit harder with the 180-grain slug than the 140. However, the actual wound cavities were not much different. The expanded diameters of the recovered bullets were within .015 inch when measuring both the extreme diameter of the jacket (which would typically be smashed down flat in an animal) and the diameter of mushroomed bullet bodies. The .30-caliber bullet left a slightly wider overall wound cavity across its traverse, disrupting slightly more tissue, but the wound channels aren’t perfectly cylindrical either. The overall diameter of the wound channels and penetration were nearly identical. 

While the .300 Win. Mag. (bottom) delivered significantly more energy to the target, that didn’t result in a dramatic difference in results. Tyler Freel

Takeaways

This demonstration shouldn’t be taken to mean that the 6.5 CM and .300 Win. Mag. are completely equal. They each have their advantages and disadvantages. The results, however, do track pretty well with other gel testing I’ve done, and what I’ve observed on animals. Those are that, with the same bullet construction, results don’t vary too much across most common hunting cartridges. In fact, things like penetration and wound cavity size can be more greatly influenced by bullet selection than simply picking a bigger or smaller cartridge. 

Some hunters place a lot of weight in having a cartridge that “hits harder.” While we can see some of the effects in the movement of the gel blocks during this demonstration, and that delta in impact force could possibly offer increased effect to the central nervous system, it’s not a reliable predictor of how fast an animal will die, or how much room for error you have. What we can rely on is the damage the bullet does.

One of the real advantages of a magnum cartridge, with a thoughtfully chosen bullet, is that it can deliver effective velocities over longer distances. A disadvantage of a 6.5 Creedmoor, in common shorter barrel configurations, is that it will produce lower muzzle velocities and result in a shorter effective velocity window. Ensuring that your impact speeds are high enough to make the bullet work properly is critical. 

Our field shooting exercise did demonstrate that you might be able to shoot a magnum just as well as the 6.5. If you can, then you are better poised to take advantage of a magnum’s real benefits. Over time, most people will find that they shoot milder cartridges more consistently. It is most important to choose a cartridge that you can shoot well and will practice with.

Final Thoughts

Along with better equipment and more refined shooting technique, we are constantly learning more about terminal ballistics. Many of our long-held beliefs are simply a product of lore and oversimplified logic. If you’re a successful hunter and your favorite cartridge works well for you, we’re not asking you to change. But if you’re feeling the pressure to choose a cartridge that you don’t think you’ll shoot as well, because you feel that it’s powerful enough to make up the difference, you’re barking up the wrong tree. The smaller cartridges will work just as well on the good shots, and the big ones will do just as poorly on the bad shots.

Paul Merson has claimed that Liverpool will need to beat Arsenal this Sunday to be involved in the Premier League title race.

A victory at Anfield would see the Reds leapfrog Brighton in third (pending the result of their trip to the Etihad on Saturday) and go within three points of Mikel Arteta’s men.

A Premier League title race feels too early for Liverpool

(Photo by Alex Grimm/Getty Images)

A Premier League title race still feels a little early for an Andoni Iraola side that’s still trying to patch up the damage done in the 2025/26 season. Not to mention a summer transfer window that saw the club add quality but not necessarily balance to a side that finished fifth last term.

Nonetheless, Merson appears to be of the mind that beating the reigning champions would put the Merseysiders firmly in the race.

“Liverpool are starting to cook a little bit, but they have got to win this Sunday if they want to be in the Premier League title race,” the former Gunners star told Sky Sports.

“If they don’t win, they stay six points behind at least and still have to go to the Emirates.

“You’re then asking Arsenal to lose two football matches without you losing one. And given Liverpool will lose more games this season, you’re then asking Arsenal to lose three or four games for the rest of the season. That’s a lot of games.

“But at the same time, it’s also must-not-lose for Liverpool as they still need to stay on track for the Champions League places.

“If Arsenal go and win against Liverpool, that could nearly be it for the Premier League title. You then start to think: who’s going to go and beat them?”

We’re not necessarily opposed to that viewpoint – and it’s worth emphasising that Liverpool should be heading into every individual game thinking it puts them a step closer to mounting a challenge on the title.

But ultimately, we’re not sure nine games represent enough of a sample size to indicate that we’re potential Premier League champions in 2026/27.

We’ve seen enough to suggest that Iraola is the right fit at Anfield and the club is showing green shoots, but there’s a lot more than needs to come from Liverpool both on and off the pitch.

The January transfer window could prove pivotal

(Photo by Carl Recine/Getty Images)

As we say, if Liverpool do find themselves in a position where they’re increasingly looking up at the top of the table, and at a slim gap between themselves, City and Arsenal, why not go for it?

But as things presently stand, we’d be amazed to see this version of Liverpool mount and maintain a title challenge over the course of the season. Especially once the games start coming thick and fast past the turn of the year and the club is juggling knockout games in the Champions League and cup competitions.

In that sense, if Liverpool appear to be negotiating their way through a title challenge come the opening of the January transfer window, the market could prove pivotal in enabling us to go the distance.

We’re all firmly aware of the weak points within the squad; the fullbacks have question marks (albeit, Ronald Araujo has excelled and Milos Kerkez is showing signs of improvement), Liverpool need a holding midfielder that can protect the backline and pass the ball forward, and there’s a clear need for a right-sided winger. We could add a backup striker on top of that, but at this point it just seems like the club will wait for Hugo Ekitike to return to the fold.

Either way, if Liverpool somehow find themselves in the thick of battle at the top of the table come January – we hope the club will back the manager in the market rather than laud the Basque head coach for being ahead of schedule.

For over four decades, REEF has been powering adventures by the water with sandals, slides, and flip-flops that live up to the brand’s “Ridiculously Comfortable” promise. Now, thanks to the versatile Del Mar Sneaker, REEF is ensuring that you can stroll from the boardwalk to the boardroom in the same pair of shoes. Stylish, practical, and, of course, comfortable, the Del Mar is an everyday sneaker built for morning meetings, sunset sails, and everything in between.

Unmatched Versatility

Think of the Del Mar as the opposite of the specialty shoe. The minds at REEF wanted a sneaker that broke down barriers: Why let athletic shoes have all the cushioned comfort? Why let dress shoes be the only ones that catch the eye? And why let a casual shoe be resigned to the weekend?

The Del Mar aims to skip the split and give you a shoe for every part of your busy life, whether you’re hanging out or hustling. Its details and restrained pops of color give it enough polish for a meeting but still feel right at home at those weekend barbecues. Jack Crivici-Kramer, co-host of The Best One Yet podcast, put it simply: “They go with jeans, they go with khakis, they go with shorts…they might even go with a tuxedo.”

 

All-Day Comfort

So what’s actually under your foot that takes the Del Mar from “comfortable” to “Ridiculously Comfortable?” Several thoughtful elements combine to give this sneaker the kind of upgrade that elevates a shoe from a once-in-a-while wear to a daily driver. A rubber-infused foam midsole provides cushioning, a plush FlexCore insole adds support, and a heel gravity pod helps the shoe roll forward with each step. This combination makes the Del Mar feel springy rather than stiff, the kind of all-important element that’s missing from so many professional-ready footwear options.

Corey Wilson, a photographer who’s constantly on his feet, sees the value in exactly that. “As a creative, every single day is different. I’m constantly in different environments; life’s constantly changing,” he says. “I need something that’s comfortable, something you can just slip on and just do so many different things with.”

 

The Details

The engineered knit upper is what separates the Del Mar from other lifestyle sneakers; it flexes with your foot so you can move with confidence on bike commutes or long afternoon walks. Just as importantly, it breathes well enough to ensure that your feet won’t overheat. When you’re moving from an active setting to a more formal one, keeping cool is invaluable.

The elastic lace option is where the knit does its best work. It fits closer to a sock than a sneaker and holds the foot without pinching, giving you a secure fit that feels as though it’s perfectly molded to your foot. The hands-free step-in is a convenient and comfortable way to get up and go. Meanwhile, standard laces come in the box if you’d rather tie your shoes for a more traditional look and fit.

The REEF Del Mar is a versatile men’s sneaker designed for all-day comfort, easy step-in wear and everyday performance. (Photo: REEF x Outside)

Because REEF was born by the water, the SoCal-based company also knows how much traction matters. The Del Mar has a grippy outsole with rubber pods at the toe and heel, thoughtfully placed where daily wear tends to concentrate. This unique design keeps the shoe’s weight down, too. That rubber is used only where it’s most essential for secure footing on slippery docks, wet streets, and freshly mopped office floors. You’ve got durability and security, all while the shoe maintains its light, carefree feel.

Sustainable Style

As a brand that’s named for and constantly inspired by the ocean, REEF is committed to protecting coastal ecosystems and the communities that depend on them through sustainable practices and environmental partnerships.

One way in which sustainability takes shape in the Del Mar is in the form of responsibly sourced leather. REEF uses Leather Working Group-certified leathers to ensure that its manufacturers are independently verified for sustainability. REEF also partners with organizations working to get more people out enjoying and protecting the ocean, including a partnership with The Mega Lab to map 1 million coral reefs by 2030. Additional partnerships with the Surfrider Foundation and Better Beach Alliance work to keep beaches clean and help preserve the planet.

The Verdict

The Del Mar won’t replace your hiking boots or your dress shoes. That’s because it doesn’t want to. Instead, it covers the wide middle of the day. So when you’re moving between errands, hustling from meeting to meeting, or sneaking away from it all for a quick walk in the sun, you can do it in a shoe that looks as good as it feels. And that’s saying something.

At the Court This Week

On Thursday, an environmental group, a local landowner, and a group formed to preserve a church urged the Supreme Court to deny the Trump administration’s request to be allowed to resume construction of its border wall in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas. For more on their filing, see the On Site section below.

On Tuesday, the Supreme Court denied a request from three cities and a group that provides legal services to immigrants to pause an agreement between Texas and the Department of Justice that prevents enforcement of a Biden-era rule allowing immigration judges to pause certain deportation proceedings. There were no noted dissents.

A Closer Look

Justice John McKinley

Friday’s A Closer Look is typically a judicial biography. This week we cover Justice John McKinley, who served on the court from 1838 to 1852.

With the Eighth and Ninth Circuits Act of 1837, Congress added two more seats to the court. The first went to Justice John Catron, and the second went to Justice John McKinley, whose confirmation created a nine-justice court. While the seat itself was an honor, its duties were less so, as McKinley would discover.

McKinley was born in May 1780 in Culpeper County, Virginia. His family soon moved to Kentucky, where he taught himself law, passed the bar in 1800, and practiced in both Frankfort and Louisville. After moving to Alabama around 1818, McKinley grew wealthy from land speculation, partnered with Andrew Jackson in the Cypress Land Company, and helped found the city of Florence, Alabama. Jackson himself called McKinley “the most prominent man” in the state.

McKinley entered the Alabama Legislature in 1820, and, after backing Jackson for the presidency (he had previously supported Henry Clay), won a U.S. Senate seat in 1826. In the Senate, McKinley advocated for bankruptcy policies “favorable to small farmers.” However, his vote to override Jackson’s Maysville Road veto (the Maysville Road Bill essentially proposed federal stock purchases for internal improvements, like state roads) raised doubts about his loyalty, and he was not reelected in 1830. After one term in the U.S. House and another in the state legislature, he won a second Senate term in 1836, only to leave the seat within two months due to his Supreme Court appointment.

In 1837, the Eighth and Ninth Circuits Act expanded the court to nine justices, and Jackson’s advisers pushed McKinley for one of the new seats, but Jackson preferred his friend William Smith instead. When Smith declined, the pick then fell to the newly inaugurated President Martin Van Buren, who went with McKinley. Van Buren gave McKinley a recess appointment in April 1837, and the Senate confirmed him by voice vote that September.

After his nomination, McKinley wrote to then-House speaker James Polk that he had “accepted the appointment, although it is certainly the most onerous and laborious of any in the United States.” The new 9th Circuit included Alabama, Arkansas, Mississippi, and eastern Louisiana and required eight circuit terms a year. McKinley told Polk he would travel more than 5,000 miles annually, although McKinley later reported a figure closer to 10,000. The duties also likely ate into his finances (the justices were required to personally pay for their own circuit court duties) and took a toll on his health (he later had a paralyzing stroke on his way to Jackson, Mississippi).

Even before his first term started, McKinley asked Van Buren in person about changing the new 9th Circuit, but the president left the issue out of his next annual State of the Union, upsetting McKinley. Following McKinley’s first full term, the justices disbanded in March 1838 to “confront some 6,000 cases on their cumulative circuit dockets,” according to Steven Brown, who wrote McKinley’s first full-length biography. McKinley’s share from the 9th Circuit alone made up 4,700 of those.

Brown detailed one of the more interesting stories from McKinley’s extensive circuit duties in a 2011 Journal of Supreme Court History article. In April 1839 while holding circuit court in Jackson, a fight spilled into the courtroom and an officer of the court (who had been called “a stupid jackass” by McKinley for not bringing in the court marshal) pulled McKinley’s nose. Newspapers reported that the justice swore never to return to Jackson, and McKinley referenced the “gross personal indignity” in a letter to Van Buren.

In his 1839 State of the Union address, Van Buren told Congress that the circuit’s schedule and distances made it “impossible” for its judge to keep up. McKinley missed much of his circuit duties, along with the entire 1840 term, due to illness. Combined with his failure to hold court in Arkansas for almost five years, states in his circuit felt “neglected,” and the Mississippi Legislature called for McKinley’s impeachment. Congress eventually reorganized the circuits in August 1842, relieving McKinley of Mississippi and Arkansas; beginning in 1843 he rode the reconstituted 5th Circuit, which covered Alabama and Louisiana.

As for McKinley’s actual approach to cases, he was strongly for states’ rights, supporting them “over the authority of the federal government.” During his time on the court, McKinley wrote 19 majority opinions, four dissents, and two concurrences by one count, 22 opinions by another, and 23 by a third, the lowest per-term rate of his era. One of his opinions for the court was in 1845’s Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan, where he held that new states enter the union on equal footing with the original 13, including ownership of the lands beneath navigable waters. Four years later in the 1849 Passenger Cases, McKinley argued in his concurrence that the federal government, not the states, controlled immigrants arriving from overseas until they became permanent residents of the states.

Increasingly ill after a stroke in 1842, McKinley died in Louisville in July 1852 at age 72, after 14 relatively difficult years on the court. He was replaced by Justice John Archibald Campbell, also from Alabama.
