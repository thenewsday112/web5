The channels reporting the most revenue during peak season aren’t necessarily driving the most sales. Holdout tests help you identify which channels generate incremental revenue, so you can make better budget decisions before peak spending begins.

Cyber Week 2025 resulted in a record-breaking $44.2 billion in online spending, according to Adobe. For the brands we work with, Cyber 5 (the five days spanning from Thanksgiving Day through Cyber Monday) generated 21.5% of total November and December revenue. With so much money moving in just a few days, over-crediting a channel is costly.

Nielsen’s research found that 84% of teams say they can prove ROI, but only 38% look at every channel. That discrepancy leaves room for over-crediting. Plan holdout tests before peak season to get results you can act on. 

What a holdout test measures

A holdout test is a controlled experiment. You choose one channel, such as a coupon partner or a content partner, and switch it off for part of your audience. Everybody else remains exposed to it. The difference between these two audiences during the same period is the incremental revenue generated by the channel.

Incrementality means the portion of a sale that wouldn’t occur without the marketing touchpoint in question. If you switch off the coupon partner for part of your audience and sales don’t move much, then most of the revenue attributed to it would’ve been earned anyway.

Choose a design that fits the channel

Geo holdouts, where you pause a channel in matched regions, are the most common choice for affiliate. Audience or code holdouts, where you suppress a coupon or link for a random percentage of a customer list, are useful for closed-loop programs. 

Coupon and loyalty partners are often over-credited at last click, so run coupon holdouts. Creator marketing often takes place off-pixel, such as on TikTok Shop and Amazon, so plan code or geo tests.

If you can, hold out 20% to 30% of traffic, aim for 200 or more conversions a week, and run the test for four to eight weeks. Even shorter tests can generate useful data. 

If a full holdout is too expensive, start smaller by holding out a smaller segment of traffic or testing one channel at a time. Even a partial test can show whether a channel gets too much credit.

Keep the control group clean

Switch off links, block coupon extensions through network controls, and inform key publishers about your tests. Watch for anything that could contaminate your control group, such as competitors’ flash sales or your own promotions, and log it. 

One sign that your control group is compromised: direct traffic grows in paused markets while brand search stays flat.

Compare exposed audiences to the holdout audience, then the previous period, and then the previous year’s result. Move from ROAS to incremental ROAS (iROAS): test revenue minus control revenue, divided by spend, with the groups scaled to equal size.

Say coupon partners report 21x ROAS and content partners report 8x. Ordered by ROAS, the content partners are pruned first. Ordered by the number of new customers acquired, they are pruned last. Content and PR land early in the path to purchase, so a short last-click window gives them little credit.

Run tests and lock down tracking before peak

A holdout run only during peak volatility produces less actionable data, so start early. Cyber 5 runs from November 26 to 30. A four-week test starting the week of October 26 ends around November 23, just before peak.

Audit all links, UTMs, and pixels first, since you can’t fix broken UTMs once a test is running. Lock down your tracking from November 1 through the holiday season, as changes to any tag during the test can compromise your data. Log every promotion, email send, and price change, and establish a single source of truth.

Check results and pull-forward after peak

Pull your data the week after Cyber 5, not in January, and expect some reversals to trickle in. Don’t declare peak a success until you’ve checked for pull-forward, meaning buyers who would have purchased later anyway. 

Discounted volume from existing customers is revenue timing, not growth. Then keep testing, since results can drift from one season to the next.

Turn findings into decisions

Every finding needs an owner, a budget line, or a test. Otherwise, it’s a retro deck sitting in a drive. Put your spend where you see proven lift, rather than on the greatest last-click numbers, and consider commission structures that reward new customer acquisition.

During peak, the channels that report the highest numbers win the budget. The brands that succeed know which number is true.

Most marketers say AI is improving things, but few of them can prove it.

According to a new Metric Sherpa study produced with Sprinklr, 92% of organizations with customer-facing AI in regular use said it met or exceeded expectations. Only 27% said they could show proof of that.

Similarly, 67% of the people who said AI made work faster also said it reduced costs. However, most of those had no baseline to base it on: Only 32% of that group said they tracked costs before launch.

Measurement needs to start before the AI does

This is likely because most teams know what they want AI to improve, but few define the metric, record the starting point, or set a clear threshold for success. So when the results arrive, even if there’s a success story, there’s no way to prove it.

The study found that while 99% could name an intended outcome for their AI use, only 26% defined success metrics, and just 10% also recorded a baseline.

Without the last two, it’s impossible to prove AI’s ROI.

If the goal is lower acquisition cost, the team needs a cost baseline before the AI starts making media or campaign decisions. If the goal is better customer service, containment alone won’t tell you much. The team needs data on resolution, repeat contacts, escalations, and handoffs.

These measurements must be built into the workflow before launch.

Don’t let the platform define success

If they aren’t, teams are forced to rely on whatever numbers the platform gives them.

A paid media platform can optimize conversion. A service platform can optimize containment. A content system can optimize engagement. All three can show improvement inside their own dashboards while the business outcome stays flat.

Eighty-five percent of those surveyed reported improvement in at least one metric category they hadn’t tracked before launch. The researchers couldn’t tell whether those teams added measurement later or relied on another source, but the disconnect is hard to ignore.

Marketing ops needs to connect platform activity to CRM, finance, customer, or experiment data. Otherwise, the tool doing the work also gets to decide whether the work succeeded.

Someone still has to own the result

Even with the right metrics in place, somebody has to be responsible for the outcome.

Only 17% said their organization names an owner for AI business outcomes. It was the least common of the eight practices measured in the study.

That role is different from owning the platform or the workflow. The platform owner makes sure the tool works. The workflow owner makes sure the process runs. The outcome owner answers for whether the AI produced the desired result.

Organizations using a named outcome owner were able to quantify results 46% of the time, versus 23% of those without one. They also used controlled tests and financial reporting more often. The study doesn’t establish that ownership caused better measurement, but the association is substantial.

Better measurement can make AI look worse

Teams that measure more carefully may also find more things going wrong.

Organizations using three or more of the study’s measurement practices reported more AI-to-employee handoff problems, adoption issues, and data-quality friction than those using zero or one. It isn’t clear if the increased measurement exposed more problems or if more complicated deployments had more problems to find. Either way, the operational lesson is useful.

A mature AI program can look messier because somebody is finally checking what happens after the automation fires. That means reviews need to cover more than output and speed. Look at where handoffs break, where employees ignore recommendations, where customers repeat themselves, and where a metric moves in the wrong direction.

Decide what would make you stop

Only 28%  said their organization uses agreed criteria to decide whether to scale, change, or stop an AI application. Just 37% review AI results on a regular schedule.

That leaves plenty of AI running without a clear rule for what happens if the direct metric improves but the business result doesn’t.

For one application, define the outcome, baseline, success threshold, and comparison before the next review. Then write down what result would justify scaling it, what would require a change, and what would cause the team to stop.

AI can make a martech stack much more capable. It can also make weak measurement much harder to unwind once the system is running.

Stablecoin Volumes Drive New Demand

Polygon Labs is expanding the Open Money Stack’s suite to cater to more networks recording huge user activity. The offering on Solana will let builders accept funding directly from bank accounts, handle payouts, and other multiple crypto services.

Stablecoin demand topped the reasons for the expansion to Solana and other networks. Earlier this month, it integrated on Tron, still based on stablecoin and user activity. 

Both networks posted huge numbers this year, capturing a huge chunk of institutional and retail demand. Tron recorded $6.94 trillion worth of stablecoin transfers while Solana saw $5.76 trillion between January and October alone.

“As that activity grows, connecting Solana to bank accounts, wallets and global payout networks allows businesses to deliver stablecoin payments to more customers and markets.” Vibhu Norby, the chief product officer of the Solana Foundation, added.

These assets are growing in popularity because of institutional demand for digital asset products in the same period. Largely, stablecoins are seen as gateway or bridge assets that corporate holders use to gain exposure to other assets. 

Stablecoins are the go-to coins before investors make a foray into other crypto like BTC, XRP, SOL, etc because they are tied to the dollar and lower risk. Also, most products lean towards users integrating stablecoins and then gaining decentralized finance (DeFi) exposure with these assets. 

According to most users, this adds to the excitement of traditional firms already exploring stablecoin infrastructure. Banks moved on these assets to lower the cost of cross-border payments and improve processing speed.

Marc Boiron, Polygon Lab’s CEO, explained that wide adoption and diversity paved the way for new integrations. “We hear from businesses that their customers and payment activity are already spread across networks and that they don’t want to force a chain choice or stitch together a bank provider, wallet, routing service and payout partner for each one.”

The Betsy Ross House: A Home Woven into the American Story

Built around 1740, the Betsy Ross House offers a glimpse into the determination, ingenuity and entrepreneurial spirit of a working woman during America’s founding.

The Philadelphia home is associated with Betsy Ross, the upholsterer, entrepreneur and Revolutionary-era flag maker whose story became intertwined with the creation of the first American flag. While historians continue to debate parts of the familiar legend, records show Ross was an accomplished tradeswoman who made flags for the government for decades.

The home’s remarkable story extends beyond the Revolutionary era. When it faced demolition in the late 19th century, Americans from across the country came together to preserve it, contributing through 10-cent memberships. It was an early demonstration of the collective pride and community spirit that continue to give historic places their meaning. 

Its Redfin listing-style page looks beyond the legend to explore Ross as a business owner, working mother and survivor who built an extraordinary life within two small, rented rooms. 

1892 Bishop’s Palace: A Remarkable Gilded Age Home

Built between 1887 and 1892, Bishop’s Palace was designed by Nicholas J. Clayton, Galveston’s most influential architect, for lawyer and congressman Walter Gresham, his wife, Josephine, and their seven children. The home spans 52 rooms and 19,086 square feet. It became the residence of Bishop Byrne after the Catholic Church purchased it in 1923.

Each detail of the home was custom-chosen, from hand-carved woodwork throughout to a dining room ceiling mural painted by Josephine herself. At the heart of the home, an octagonal mahogany rotunda staircase rises beneath French stained glass, surrounded by finishes from Venice, Holland and North Africa.

The property also includes a few conversation pieces, among them Josephine’s china collection, the original bed, Walter’s desk and Bishop Byrne’s player piano. It sits in Galveston’s East End Historic District, a neighborhood of colorful historic homes and lush landscaping, with the Gulf only a few blocks south. 

Today, Bishop’s Palace stands as a one-of-a-kind blend of elaborate craftsmanship, Texan influence and Gilded Age ambition.

'A wave can hide a lot of weaknesses': Dems question if they'll learn anything

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Democrats are poised for a good night next Tuesday. That doesn’t mean they’ve figured out how to win.

More than a dozen lawmakers, campaign operatives, strategists and pollsters warned in interviews that national Democrats risk drawing the wrong conclusions if they take back control of at least one chamber of Congress next week.

Those figures from across the party worry that the midterms, which have become a referendum on President Donald Trump, have done little to improve the Democratic Party’s brand. They caution that a favorable political environment has papered over persistent problems for a party still grappling with why it lost the 2024 presidential election. And they admit they are still sharply divided over what its message should be heading into 2028.

“Don't confuse them losing with us winning,” said Morgan Jackson, the senior adviser to Democrat Roy Cooper’s Senate campaign in North Carolina. Cooper has consistently led in the polls by a wide margin in a state Trump won three times.

“This is not an affirmation of the Democratic Party in any which way,” said one veteran Democratic pollster working on Senate campaigns, who was granted anonymity to speak candidly. “It is very much a rejection of Trump.”

The Democratic Party’s approval rating plummeted in the aftermath of the 2024 presidential election, and remains relatively weak. That’s even as voters tell pollsters they are more likely to trust Democrats than Republicans on several fronts, including addressing the cost of living, which voters consistently say is their top issue this cycle.

Democrats’ quest for a coherent, positive pitch to answer rising prices and perceived corruption in Washington has at times been stymied by infighting between the party’s progressive and moderate factions over the best way to respond to the Trump administration.

Democrats fought over whether to prolong government shutdowns to protest funding for Immigration and Customs Enforcement and the slashing of health care subsidies in late 2025 and early 2026. They were quick to attack their own upon release of the Democratic National Committee’s formal autopsy on where the party went wrong in 2024.

But as Americans soured on Trump’s agenda and the Iran war drove up costs, Democrats zeroed in on a broad “costs, chaos and corruption” message that blamed the White House — without having to resolve their own internal tensions and disorder.

“When your opponent is destroying themselves," said Matt Barron, a Democratic strategist who has done work across the rural Midwest and Rust Belt, "just get out of the way and let it happen.”

Nabbing wins in a midterm election with a historically unpopular president is one thing. Building a winning brand for a party that was soundly defeated at the polls just two years ago is another.

“If all you do is say the other people are terrible, that only gets you so far,” said Democratic donor Oliver Libby, who has advised Democratic House candidates Cait Conley in New York and Johnny Garcia in Texas this cycle. “You can have a minimum viable product of a midterm with criticism. You definitely don't win a presidential year with just criticism.”

In a new POLITICO Poll conducted by PF Global, more than half of Americans said if Republicans lose control of both the House and the Senate, it would be either because of Trump’s actions since he became president, or because Republicans have run weak campaigns. Just one in four said a Democrat-controlled Congress would be because the party had run a strong campaign.

“There are people who are going to vote in this election who are going to vote against Republicans,” said Antjuan Seawright, a Democratic strategist and adviser to House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries. “That does not mean they are in love with Democrats. It just means they are voting against the people in charge.”

Democratic strategist Lis Smith, an architect of Pete Buttigieg's 2020 presidential campaign, said the Democratic candidates performing well in historically deep-red territory are the ones distancing themselves from the national party.

“That should tell party leaders something,” she said.

Smith helped found the moderate groups Majority Democrats and The Bench, and is advising Democratic Senate candidates like Adam Hamilton in Kansas, James Talarico in Texas and Josh Turek in Iowa — all of whom have taken on that tack.

In Kansas, Hamilton publicly asked former Vice President Kamala Harris to stay away from his campaign, and said he would vote against Chuck Schumer remaining the party’s leader in the Senate. In Texas, Talarico vowed to challenge the party’s “establishment” and “change the Democratic Party.” And in Iowa, Turek says he rarely thinks about the party, while Rob Sand, the Democratic gubernatorial candidate in the state, has said he wants to end the two-party system altogether.

“The reality is that a lot of very average and even mediocre candidates get across the line in a wave election,” Smith said. “A wave can hide a lot of weaknesses, and so we need to be able to make sure that the party can survive when the wave recedes.”

Assuming Democrats take back control of at least one chamber of Congress next week, top party operatives see an opportunity for their side to deliver for voters who have wanted more checks on the Trump administration’s expansive use of executive power. But many caution that Democrats on Capitol Hill should avoid leaning too hard into an anti-Trump message next year, while others argue that the anti-corruption message will continue to galvanize the electorate.

"The thing that I would be concerned about, if you go from 2026 to 2028, is that people think that corruption, that elite control over our economy, is just a Trump issue," said Bill Neidhardt, who worked on Bernie Sanders’ 2020 presidential campaign and is now advising left-leaning Democratic candidate Will Lawrence and working to boost Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic candidate for Senate in Michigan. “What they need to focus on is that corporate power, those billionaire elites who are dictating the terms of our economy, that's what people are upset with."

William Garcia, the co-chair of Wisconsin’s La Crosse County Democratic Party, has been canvassing extensively for his party in the state’s rural-heavy 3rd district, where voters swung toward Trump in recent years. Democrats are now trying to claw back that support.

“I think we’re going to have a great night on Nov. 3. And then all these Democrats are going to have to deliver on all of these things we’re talking about,” Garcia said. “If we get to the end of 2027 summer and we haven’t passed bills to make a substantial difference in people’s lives, that’s my biggest fear. We gotta deliver.”

Others acknowledge that voters may hold the party to a higher bar if they succeed in taking back control of both chambers of Congress — and they’ll be ready to punish Democrats in 2028 if they don’t follow through on their campaign promises to hold the Trump administration to account and bring down the cost of living.

It’s a slippery slope for a party that has historically struggled to convince voters that it is well equipped to handle the economy. In 2024, Trump persuaded the majority of voters they would be financially better off with him in the White House, while painting Democrats as a party more concerned with social issues.

“If we start getting back into a party that cares more about non-economic issues, then we haven't learned the lesson,” warned Pete Giangreco, a veteran Democratic strategist who has worked on House, Senate and gubernatorial campaigns this cycle. “People just are going to keep hitting the change button until the economy starts working for them again.”

THISS STUDIO PUTS RADICAL REUSE BEFORE THE FINISHED DRAWING

 

An old barn, a pontoon boat, and a dismantled exhibition might seem like unlikely starting points for an architectural brief. For THISS Studio, they offer materials with which to begin designing. Founded by architects Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, the London practice works across cultural spaces, fashion, retail, and public installations, developing projects around the people and resources available to make them. Hanke’s research into low-impact design meets Scott’s interest in material experimentation and structural invention, an approach informed in part by his experience designing and flying high-performance kites. In an exclusive interview with designboom, Hanke explains how working with reclaimed components changes the architect’s role, the construction process, and the expectations attached to a finished space.

 

‘Primarily, reuse changes the order in which we design,’ Hanke tells designboom. A project can no longer be fully resolved on paper before its materials have been found. Instead, the studio establishes its intentions, gathers available stock, and revisits the design as that stock arrives, continuing to make adjustments during construction. Suppliers, craftspeople, and the dimensions and condition of individual components all influence the outcome. ‘With reuse, necessity means we can no longer be the sole authors of the outcome,’ he explains. This requires a strong concept and details capable of accommodating differences between materials. As Hanke puts it, ‘The work becomes a conversation rather than a monologue.’

Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, founders of THISS Studio | image by Jasper Fry

 

 

SALVAGED TIMBER AND AN UPTURNED BOAT SHAPE NEW SPACES

 

That willingness to let existing objects redirect a project is visible in Barn Again, developed with architect Tom Svilans for the 2025 Copenhagen Architecture Biennial. Beams recovered from disused Norwegian barns are stacked into a tapering pavilion, retaining weathered surfaces and traces of previous connections. Designed for disassembly and further use, the structure gives those irregularities a place in its architectural expression. At Kiko Kostadinov’s London flagship, a pontoon boat installation by artist Ryan Trecartin provides a very different starting point: inverted railings become a ceiling-mounted support system for the shop’s shelving. ‘Often the material will suggest a move we would never have drawn from scratch,’ Hanke says. Both projects make that possibility tangible, finding a new spatial role for something with an established identity.

 

The same attention to available resources extends to Pergola, a public outdoor theater in Cairo developed with CLUSTER and Orient Productions. Its red structure incorporates materials from local reuse initiatives, including textiles made from recovered river plastic by VeryNile and recycled-plastic bricks from Rebloxx. Elsewhere, the studio’s work on PoliNations demonstrates how a temporary installation can continue beyond its original setting. First presented in Birmingham in 2022, its monumental tree structures returned at Glastonbury’s Shangri-La in 2025. These projects approach material life at different scales, from individual components to substantial structures that can be used again. ‘Reuse should never dilute the conceptual ambition of a project,’ Hanke argues. ‘We are happily now in a position in the industry where reuse is not even enough to drive the design, it is just part of the process.’

monumental tree structures frame a temporary garden in Birmingham

 

 

EXHIBITION DESIGN STARTS WITH WHAT HAPPENS AFTERWARDS

 

For THISS Studio, exhibition design brings these questions into particularly sharp focus. A display may occupy a gallery briefly, yet its construction can determine whether its materials remain useful long after visitors leave. ‘Exhibition design is never neutral,’ Hanke says. ‘It participates in the conversation the exhibition is having with the work, and that conversation is ideological, even political.’ Discussing work with the V&A, he describes an all-timber assembly without metal fixings, conceived to preserve the components for subsequent use. The design considers how pieces will come apart and what condition they will be in when they do. ‘As we see it, an exhibition should be an interruption in a material’s life not the end of it.’

 

Working with the Design Museum has prompted another shift in method: taking stock before drawing. The studio catalogued and assessed existing exhibition infrastructure, then tracked design decisions against that inventory, scrutinizing each proposed addition. ‘We worked more like archaeologists or archivists than conventional architects,’ Hanke explains. Here, the challenge becomes how to alter the experience of a space through careful adjustments to what is already available. ‘The creative challenge is restraint: making something feel new while touching as little as possible.’ The approach gives the less visible work of sorting, documenting, and evaluating materials a direct influence on the architecture visitors eventually encounter.

repurposed boat railings support shelving inside Kiko Kostadinov’s store

 

 

MAKING REUSE WORK MEANS CHANGING THE COMMISSION

 

Turning this approach into routine practice requires institutions to reconsider how they commission and fund design. Hanke identifies a tension between the certainty clients expect at the outset and the adjustments that reclaimed materials can demand later. Design programs need to accommodate those revisions, while funding must arrive early enough to secure stock before rapid exhibition construction schedules take over. He points to organizations such as Yes Make, which can source and hold materials while a design develops. ‘Money and materials need to move earlier,’ he says. A willingness to reuse therefore has practical consequences for budgets, procurement, and decision-making, well before the first component reaches the gallery.

 

Long-term galleries introduce further pressures, particularly around maintenance and durability. Hanke argues that these requirements need not exclude previously used materials, citing the studio’s research into the recovery of hardwoods and cross-laminated timber after extended service. What matters is designing both the assembly and its eventual separation with sufficient care. ‘Dismantling should be designed, not improvised. The materials should leave as a resource not as waste.’ For THISS Studio, the architect’s responsibility consequently extends beyond the appearance and performance of an installation during its public life. It includes organizing the collaboration that produces it and anticipating what its parts might become once the exhibition is over.

Male black bear ‘Nugget’ wins Introvert Award for his adorable hiding technique

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Like the patient Paul, a male black bear (Ursus americanus) named Nugget would rather hang back alone than jump right into the salmon fest. For his solo tendencies, the team at Tongass National Forest in southeastern Alaska have awarded Nugget the 2026 Introvert Award.

“For much of the season, Nugget could be seen wedged between rocks by lower falls,” the U.S. Forest Service team writes. “If you saw a furry, black head with glassy eyes poking out of a crack—that’s Nugget! You could also find him perched on a rock waiting for his turn to fish in the lower falls hierarchy; afraid to assert his dominance in the crowd.”

Nugget was first spotted in the area only two years ago, and is beloved for his small size. He has a reddish coat that shifts to black later in the season. Nugget is often described as “scrappy.” He mostly scavenged during his first season at the creek, but slowly learned to catch salmon.

“The pristine ecosystem around Anan Creek allows even small, scrappy bears like Nugget to thrive,” the Rangers write.

Meet Bears of Anan Creek

The U.S. Forest Service team at Tongass National Forest are currently doling out the Anan Bear Awards to the 39 bears spotted at the Anan Wildlife Observatory over the summer. 

The nation’s largest national forest, Tongass covers most of Southeast Alaska, including the capital city of Juneau. The area includes Mendanhall Glacier (a popular destination for Alaskan cruises), numerous hiking trails, eagles, and plenty of bears. One of the hotspots is Anan Creek, home to one of the largest runs of pink salmon in Southeast Alaska. 

According to Friends of Anan, 39 known bears feast on the salmon at Anan Creek. The population includes 31 black bears and eight brown bears (Ursus arctos). Like their chunky counterparts further north in Katmai National Park, the bears return annually each summer to eat salmon and teach their young how to fish and survive. And also like the fat bears of Katmai, every bear here has their own habits and quirks. 

The U.S. Forest Service tracks and documents every bear and explore.org’s cameras also broadcast live streams of their antics.
