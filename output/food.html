Adventurer to Climb 100 Peaks on 2,000Km Taiwan Traverse

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Tomorrow, November 1, Japanese adventurer Yoki Tanaka begins a four-month, 2,000km trek north to south down the length of Taiwan. Along the way, he will summit all 100 of Taiwan's celebrated Baiyue peaks.

Taiwan has about 268 peaks exceeding 3,000m, but only 100 of them made this special list, which is based not only on height but beauty, difficulty, prominence, and character. Prominent Taiwanese mountaineers compiled the list in the early 1970s, partly inspired by Japan's own list of 100 revered mountains.

Yoki Tanaka arrives in Taiwan earlier this week. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

The 43-year-old Tanaka has specialized in this unusual combination of long-distance trekking and mountaineering. In 2014, he summited Japan's 100 famous mountains while walking 7,800km over 208 days. (Japan, though far bigger than Taiwan, has only 23 mountains over 3,000m.) A year later, he extended that by climbing 200 mountains and walking 8,000km in 222 days. Finally, he increased that again to 300 mountains. This time, it took him three years and about 20,000km of walking.

Tanaka's projected route across Taiwan.

 

Tanaka will begin on Sunday from the northernmost tip of Taiwan, at the Cape Fugui lighthouse, and finish sometime in March at Eluanbi, the country's southernmost point. The first 150km will be along roads before he reaches his first trailhead. He'll spend the next week trekking. His first five peaks will be Xueshan (3,886m), Nanhudashan (3,742m), Hehuanshan (3,417m), Qilaishan (3,560m), and Nenggaoshan 3,262m).

Precise planning

Although he won't use motorized transport for the entire journey, he will have ample opportunity to resupply along the way. His expedition plan includes a charmingly precise breakdown of how he plans to spend his evenings: town stays, 48 days; mountain huts, 38 days; tent camping, 39 days. He has also budgeted 16 rest days and 20 contingency days.

Tanaka trains in Taiwan earlier this year. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

His most difficult section, scheduled for early January, will take him through the remote interior of the Central Mountain Range for over two weeks. During this time, he'll have to carry his own food, search for trails that may no longer exist, and gamble that he will be able to find enough water.

During a December 2025 reconnaissance trip to Xueshan, Taiwan's second-highest mountain, Tanaka discovered how difficult it can be to obtain water. Two water sources marked on his map were completely dry.

On a previous hike. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

By late January, the Japanese trekker should reach the historic Batongguan Trail, eventually leading to 3,952m Yushan, Taiwan's highest mountain. The Batongguan Trail was originally constructed in 1875 under the Qing dynasty to establish a route across the mountainous interior. Decades later, Japanese colonial authorities constructed a separate crossing through the region. Sections of the route survive as remote hiking trails and provide access to some of Taiwan's highest mountains.

Tanaka first visited Taiwan in the spring of 2025 and has made several reconnaissance visits since, including an east-west crossing of the island earlier this year. He had intended to begin his Taiwan 100 Peaks Traverse project this past spring, but local mountaineers advised him to avoid the rainy season and typhoon risks. Their advice prompted him to shift his starting date to November.

Yoki Tanaka on a training hike in Taiwan. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

Interestingly, of Taiwan's 100 Baiyue peaks, only 99 are now recognized as surpassing 3,000m. The exception is Lushan at 2,981m. It was originally measured at 3,043m but subsequent measurements reduced its elevation. Nevertheless, it remains on the list.

Taiwan's representative in Japan told Japanese media that the fastest prior completion of the Baiyue by a foreign climber took three years and eight months, spread over multiple trips.

Once Tanaka begins, his expedition website will provide live tracking of his progress across Taiwan.

Scientists confirm fruit and vegetable skin really is good for you

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
Fruit and vegetable skin is often rich in fibre Ekaterina Vasileva-Bagler/Getty Images

Some people are convinced that fruit and vegetable skin is particularly nutritious, and now scientists have confirmed the idea. They have discovered that these parts of fruits and vegetables often contain far more fibre than the flesh we normally consume.

The finding suggests cooks are unwittingly discarding fibre-rich food by chucking out the seeds, skins and stems of fruits and vegetables when preparing a meal.

Home cooks and caterers should adopt “root to tip” eating and start cooking with squash peel, watermelon rind and pepper stalks to improve the public’s intake of dietary fibre, the team says.

“We’ve really got to shift our purchasing habits to buy whole fruits and vegetables, and then use all of the fruits and vegetables in dishes,” says Rachel Gibson at King’s College London.

Gibson and her colleagues tested the fibre content of 12 fruits and vegetables, including butternut squash, kiwi fruit, watermelon, broccoli and bell pepper. The team measured the fibre content both in the parts typically consumed and the parts that usually go to waste.

They found that, in 10 of the 12 produce types, the normally wasted components contain more fibre than the commonly consumed parts. Butternut squash seeds and skin, for example, contained 9.25 grams of fibre per 100 grams, compared with 2 g of fibre per 100 g of squash flesh. Meanwhile, the skin and core of a pineapple core contains 450 per cent more fibre than the flesh. These skin and core components are often tough to chew, but the researchers say they can be blended, grated or roasted to make them more palatable.

Just 4 per cent of British adults meet the recommended minimum daily intake of 30 g of dietary fibre, with the average adult consuming around half that per day.

Gibson suggests caterers could use the findings to design fibre-rich meals using more produce that would otherwise go to waste. This could reduce food waste and cut costs for commercial kitchens, as well as increasing dietary fibre intake, she says.

King’s College London’s canteen piloted a “root to tip” menu in May 2025. Meals included a West African peanut stew made with all of a butternut squash, kale stalks and leek tops, and a watermelon salad featuring pickled watermelon rind.

Simple menu tweak can nudge people into choosing climate-friendly food

Rejigging the meal choices on offer in a canteen can prompt people to make healthier, climate-friendlier decisions

Eating a larger proportion of harvested fruit and vegetables will also improve the resilience of the food system in the face of climate shocks, says Gibson. “Everything we pull out of the ground that we can eat, we should be eating.”

Christian Reynolds at City St George’s University of London says the research is an “exciting investigation”. But he points out that the most carbon-intensive food waste is meat and dairy products. Reducing fruit and vegetable waste is therefore “part of the solution, but it’s not the only thing we need to do”, he says.

Weini Kelati Has Taken a One-Mile at a Time Approach for Taking On the NYC Marathon

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

There was a time when eight miles felt like an eternity to Weini Kelati. Now, toward the tail end of preparing for her first full marathon, she can find herself 10 miles into a 20-mile workout and realize the distance that once signaled the end of a hard day has now become the halfway point.

This progression says a lot about what marathon preparation requires. The obvious difference between a half marathon and 26.2 miles is distance, but Kelati discovered that building a marathon body involved more than adding more miles to her workload. The body must be conditioned to tolerate additional volume, know how to fuel, how to recover, and eventually trust that the work was enough when fatigue arrives.

Kelati, the American half-marathon record holder, Olympian, and five-time national champion will make her marathon debut at the TCS New York City Marathon on Nov. 1. Speaking with Muscle & Fitness through New York Road Runners, she explained that she had always seen the half marathon as a gateway toward eventually racing 26.2, even giving herself a loose requirement of completing four or five before considering making the move.

“I think first I was nervous,” Kelati said. “I was like, ‘Oh my goodness. This is going to be hard because I used to be at low mileage.’”

What surprised her most isn’t how difficult the transition was, but how quickly the human body can begin adjusting to something once intimidating as simply another day in training.

Redefining Normal for the Body

During Kelati’s half-marathon preparation, she said her highest weekly mileage reached around 75 miles. For this build, she knew that number would have to climb into the 90s. The increase initially made her nervous. Once she started, however, something unexpected happened: She began enjoying it.

The biggest adjustment was redefining what constitutes a long effort. In the lead-up to a 10K or half-marathon, a six- or eight-mile tempo run could represent one of the bigger sustained sessions on her schedule. Marathon preparation introduced two different types of long runs, including easier road efforts and workouts, which she can cover approximately 20 miles at a specific pace.

“I’m not the type of person who rushes into something,” Kelati said. “I always take a lot baby steps to get used to something.”

That patience allowed her body to establish a new baseline without making every unfamiliar distance feel like a test. Her weekly structure wasn’t radically different from half-marathon training, either. The biggest change is that familiar paces and workouts extended further, asking her to sustain what she already knew for considerably longer.

The result was a changed internal relationship with distance.

“When you get to eight miles, you start saying, ‘Oh, I’m already halfway,’ instead of saying, ‘Oh, I used to stop here,” Kelati says. “The mind and the body change the way I have to handle it.”

New York Road Runners

Marathon Training Won’t Let Kelati Run on Empty

The added mileage exposed an area Kelati admits hasn’t always been a strength for her: nutrition.

At shorter distances, she could get away with habits that weren’t conducive with marathon preparation. For years, she could wake up and run without eating anything because consuming food before a morning session wasn’t something she enjoyed.

The marathon build forced her to adjust that part of her routine. When there is a large increase in workload, personal preferences eventually run into physical necessity.

“You have to help the body as much as it’s working,” Kelati said. “During the marathon training, you have to fuel your body. You have to get ready for the next hard run.”

She learned that fueling for her bigger sessions didn’t mean forcing down a huge meal. If appetite was an issue, she would have smaller meals spaced throughout the day. This same logic changed her behavior after a run.

Meals were the common recovery method for Kelati,  but the increased workload led her to incorporate recovery drinks and pay closer attention to getting nutrition back into her body sooner.

Kelati’s favorite recovery method didn’t require scheduling or involve any expensive equipment.

“I love taking naps after every single run,” she said. “It doesn’t matter how hard or easy the run I do, I just have to take a little nap every day and, of course, more than eight hours of sleep during the night.”

Kelati fully believes giving her body adequate downtime has been an important reason she was able to absorb demanding training and be able to show up the following morning and ask her body to perform again.

Training the Mind for a Foreign Distance

There is one part of marathon preparation Kelati couldn’t completely simulate.

She could run 20 miles in training, nap after training, and ask experienced marathoners what waits beyond the distances she already knows.

Until New York, however, she won’t know exactly how her own body and mind will respond to racing 26.2 miles. That uncertainty doesn’t seem to bother her as much as thinking about the entire distance at once.

“I don’t like counting the miles,” Kelati said. “It seems pretty long for me to think about it while you’re standing on the start line or you just started. You’re in the first mile, and you’re just like, ‘Oh, we have like 25 miles to go.’”

Instead, she intends on remaining completely in the moment.

“I just like to be in the present and focus one mile at a time,” she said.

Insights have helped. Kelati did the same thing before moving into half marathons, quizzing runners about which points of the race are the most challenging so she could have some idea of what awaited her. This time, the answer is a different number.

“They keep saying, ‘You’re going to start racing when you get to 30K,’” she says. “It can be different, but also it’ll give you what to expect or what’s coming when you get to that distance and how you’re going to handle it.”

Discomfort will eventually arrive, and Kelati’s strategy is a simple one. She reminds herself that the pain is temporary and that she’d rather find out what she’s capable of than finish wondering whether she would have given more.

It’s a mindset that comes from weeks of watching her body handle workloads she once was uncertain about. Kelati says one of the things she’s most proud of during this build has been how naturally she adapted to the training her coach put in front of her. Each completed run and training session has chipped away the stone of uncertainty that existed when 26.2 was still just a thought.

“When you try to do something new, you always have these thoughts that can prevent you from doing something,” Kelati said. “But then once you just start doing it, I feel like we can do anything when we believe.”

When Kelati reaches the start line in Staten Island, there will be lots of thoughts in her head. All the early morning runs, how well she’s done in races in New York, and all the months spent gradually extending what her body can tolerate.

She will let those thoughts flow. Her goal for that morning speaks to her readiness.

“I want to feel good just standing there and say, ‘Today is a great day,’” Kelati said. ‘“I feel good. Let’s do this.’”

Saddle to Paddle: Bikerafting From Canada to Mexico

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Pete Streufert and his friend Carter (no last name) are cycling 4,025km and packrafting 645km across the U.S. from Canada to Mexico. Starting in Glacier National Park, they will cross four rivers and six U.S. states to finish in El Paso, Texas. 

Their route connects the rivers with long-distance cycling trails. This allows the pair to cross landscapes that would usually require separate trips by bike and watercraft. Streufert, a geospatial wildfire scientist and cartographer, came up with the route by combining existing GPS tracks and geographical data from Bikepacking.com, American Whitewater, and OpenStreetMap. He overlaid cycling corridors and rivers to identify where the two could connect.

Before starting the journey on September 17, he said they could find no record of anyone using this particular combination of rivers and cycling routes. So it is possible that they are the first to do so.

The start

They started what they neatly call the Saddle to Paddle Traverse on September 17, from Glacier National Park, Montana, on the Canadian border. They kicked off the first packrafting section with a 115km descent of the North Fork of the Flathead River, which forms part of the western boundary of Glacier National Park.

From there, they followed the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route through Montana toward Jackson Lake, Wyoming, for 1,107km. Here, they hit their second paddling phase on the Snake River.

Every time they reach a river, the pair inflates their rafts, load their bicycles aboard, and paddle downstream. Then at each takeout, they deflate the rafts, attach them to their bikes, and continue riding.

The route. Photo: Pete Streufert

 

After 130km along the Snake River, their route joined the SLoToJa cycling corridor. They pedaled through Idaho, Wyoming, and Utah. Here, a short connector track took them onto the newly developed Golden Gravel Trail. A further 860km of cycling brought them to Utah's Green River.

Class III and IV rapids

They then paddled 328km through Desolation and Gray Canyons, including a handful of Class III rapids, and their only Class IV rapid of the trip. 

Pulling their rafts out in Canyonlands National Park, they took the White Rim Trail to Moab. They are now taking a few days in Moab to “get all the mud off our bikes and escape the rain for a day or two before we keep heading south,” says Streufert.

From here, they will once again pick up the Golden Gravel Trail through the La Sal Mountains and across Colorado, then continue on the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route to New Mexico.

Paddling the Green River. Photo: Pete Streufert

 

A final paddling section on New Mexico's Rio Chama will take them to the last leg of the trip, a 950km ride to El Paso.

The pair are riding Rodeo Labs Flaanimal 6.0 Steel, fitted with 29-inch Vittoria Mezcal tires, and using Kokopelli packrafts. The pair have described this as a "self-supported" route, although of course they're replenishing their food along the way. They seem to combine camping with staying in towns.

Has Substitution Culture Gone Too Far?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This excerpt was originally published in Pre Shift, our newsletter for the hospitality industry. Subscribe for more first-person accounts, advice, and interviews.

When Chanita Chayaluk decided to open her restaurant and wine bar Taan, she hoped “to showcase Thai food in Chicago in a different light,” she says. “The first [wave of] immigration of Thai people in Chicago was in the 1970s, and a lot of [Thai] food here still feels like it’s from that time.” In September, she opened with a menu including miang, a Northern Thai dish made with fermented tea leaves, and a whole, deep-fried fish in a fish-sauce caramel. 

Another quirk of operating in Chicago? “We’re still pretty ‘Midwest nice,’” says Chayaluk. “We try to overaccommodate and appease the masses.” In the early days of service, guests made frequent requests for substitutions or accommodations due to preferences and dietary restrictions. “It would slow down service and speed, and that was not just affecting us, but the guest experience in many ways,” she says. With a small kitchen and staff, it was hard to keep up. “It just felt like we were Chipotle.” When a guest asked for green curry mussels “but without the mussels,” it was a turning point. 

Chayaluk implemented a limited substitution policy at Taan, stating on Instagram that ingredients like fish sauce and shrimp paste are “the fabric that defines our cuisine,” and therefore, could not be removed from any dishes that include it. 

A decade or so ago, substitution culture was generally frowned upon and derided. Recently, though, some restaurants report it bubbling back up—and in full force. There’s a swirl of factors that got us here: Lunch slop bowl spots primed customers to expect a detailed level of customization with their orders. Unreasonable Hospitality pushed an intensified “customer is always right” mentality. And influencers entering the food-review space have made criticism of anything—including a restaurant choosing not to honor certain dietary preferences—ripe for an immediate, viral takedown. Couple all of that with the fact that it is hard to keep a restaurant afloat these days, and you’ve got several operators giving in, whether to specific requests, or to altering their menus altogether (see also: the great de-spicification of menus.)

At the same time, restaurants like Taan and New York’s Ha’s Snack Bar—which proudly announces in its Instagram bio that everything contains fish sauce—are standing their ground.

For Lom Wong in Phoenix, Arizona, substitution and modification requests have ramped up after receiving national attention. Chef Yotaka “Sunny” Martin was recognized by the James Beard Awards last year, and the restaurant was named a Michelin Bib Gourmand in August. “[After the award,] we had more guests than we were used to picking things apart. They were asking us to modify dishes or not understanding our service style and why we weren’t coursing things in a European fashion,” says chef and co-owner Alex Martin. “[There is a] European distinction of what an award-winning restaurant looks like, so people equated that recognition with a white-tablecloth restaurant. We were just like, ‘Well, that’s not who we’ve ever been, and it’s never what our restaurant will ever be.’”

Like Taan, Lom Wong took to social media to set expectations, including that the restaurant would not “make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar,” and that certain dishes might differ from Western expectations of them; beef might have gristle, fishes will be whole, and both choices are fully intentional. “Sunny is our guiding light of cultural context,” says Martin. “[She advises, considering if] changing something about a dish compromises the integrity of the dish.” For example, the mango salad can be ordered with the shrimp on the side, but the restaurant won’t remove beef or shrimp paste from dishes that necessitate them. 

Chayaluk takes a similar approach at Taan; there is always a vegan, gluten-free option (currently a fried rice) that she describes as a “catchall” for those who can’t have other items on the menu. “We make sure that that dish is also really, really good and substantial; it’s not just an appetizer,” she says. “It’s not an afterthought.” 

Staff at both restaurants are encouraged to uphold the boundaries, which both Chayaluk and Martin say are as much in place for protecting their staff’s peace as they are for ensuring quality control for guests. While there has been some backlash, the expectation-setting at the start of the meal (and before, thanks to the social media posts) has for the most part been taken well. “The way we explain [our policy] to guests is kindly saying we respect your preferences, but they’re your preferences,” says Martin, “and steering them toward dishes on the menu that might already align with those.”

A few weeks before my son Oliver turned eight, planning his birthday started taking up residence in my brain.

We weren’t aiming for anything particularly extravagant. We were having extended family over for dinner and cake, and enjoying the same traditions we’d built around birthdays over the years. Breakfast in bed with balloons. Presents. Time with grandparents. His favorite meal for dinner. And at the end of the night, we’d go around the table and each say three things we loved about him.

These little rituals have become the essence of our family’s birthday magic. 

They also don’t just magically happen.

In the weeks leading up to the big day, I was thinking about what Oliver wanted, figuring out the food, doing the shopping, wrapping the gifts, finding cute decorations, coordinating with family about wish lists, ordering the cake, making sure his brothers had something to give him, and remembering the special cereal he wanted. I was doing all of that alongside work and school and dinner and everything else our family already had going on.

Then the birthday came, and it all went off as planned, for the most part. Oliver was thrilled. His brothers were excited. Our family was there. At one point, the boys were all playing and laughing together with their grandparents, and I remember wanting to go sit with them.

Instead, my brain took over. If I didn’t start clearing dinner now, we’d get the cake too late, and we still needed to have time for presents. 

So I got up. Set up the cake and dessert. Found the candles I’d bought. Rounded everyone up. Took pictures. And then came more dishes and wrapping paper to clean up and wrangling three tired kids into bed.

Later that night, I sat down and looked through the photos on my phone of everyone smiling together. They were exactly the kind of pictures that make you think, This. This is the good stuff.

But, I couldn’t remember a single moment where I had really enjoyed it or even felt present with my son the whole night. And, unfortunately, birthdays weren’t the only time I felt like this.

The mental load of making memories

I always thought I’d be the type of mom who loved creating fun memories for my kids. Until I became a parent, I never realized how much invisible labor it took to create those magical moments of childhood.

Tooth fairy rituals, holiday traditions, family vacations and weekend outings… They all require someone planning things out in advance. And, usually, that someone is Mom. 

To be clear, I love creating fun moments for our family. I know it’s not everyone’s cup of tea, but it wasn’t something I personally wanted to give up. But something needed to change.

Because, while I was busy planning and managing the entire experience, I wasn’t participating. Instead of enjoying my time on the beach with the kids, I was already thinking about who needed sunscreen re-application, or the groceries we’d need to grab for the next day, or the email I needed to send back to their coach. 

Some of that is unavoidable. But all of it didn’t have to be mine.

Learning to be present in the moment

I wish I could say I simply decided to be more present, but it took years to build better habits around how our family plans and shares the work, and I still catch myself slipping into cruise-director mode sometimes.

But as the founder of Jam, an all-in-one calendar and organization app to reduce the mental load of modern family life, I’ve also spent a lot of time thinking about how to better manage the invisible labor that piles up. And over the years, I’ve found some tried-and-true ways to alleviate it, especially during those special magical moments.

  • Be a real team. My husband and I now share more of the planning from the beginning. We’ve learned to divide things based on our interests and skills. I love decorating, adding special touches, and cooking. He’s great at researching gifts and doing all the shopping. When needed, he’ll take on more of the day-to-day tasks than usual to give me more bandwidth for whatever special moment we have coming up. 
  • Make it visible. Having a shared family hub like Jam—where the schedules, lists, and to-do’s are all in one spot—makes everything visible to the rest of the family and makes it easier for us to work as a team. Now, a month before the birthday, a to-do list pops up in our shared dashboard that we can all add to together. It also means I have a place to put those thoughts when they enter my brain at inopportune times, allowing me to be more present and avoid carrying it all on my own.
  • Review the plan. Before a busy day, trip, birthday or holiday, my husband and I will also talk through the game plan together. Who’s doing what? What time does something need to happen? It sounds incredibly basic, but for years I was the only one who knew the plan, which meant I was the one orchestrating and remembering everything in the moment, as well. (Now that my kids are older, they’re part of these chats, too!)
  • Make the most of other people’s help. I’ve gotten much more comfortable outsourcing, delegating, and dropping things altogether. Sometimes we order the food. Sometimes someone else brings dessert. Sometimes the extra thing I had in my head simply doesn’t happen. I’m much better now at understanding whether something will actually add to the experience or just add more work.

Enjoying the magic moments that happen every day

Over time, I also realized that some of the most special moments with my kids require absolutely no planning at all. A slow Sunday morning when nobody has anywhere to be. A few extra cuddles and arm tickles at bedtime. The last ice cream cone of summer, before “real life” starts again.

But those moments are also so easy to miss when half your brain is already in tomorrow. 

Now I’m better at recognizing that constant pull of the mental load, and pressing pause on it, too. 

There will always be something that needs to happen next. I’m a working mom with three kids, after all, so it’s unavoidable.  But I am more aware now that I don’t want to spend their childhood constantly preparing for the next part of it. Sometimes Sunday morning is a good time to get a jump on a few things that need to get done… and sometimes, it isn’t. 

When I look back at the pictures, I want to see more than the beautiful moments I created for my kids.

I want to remember how it felt to be there with them, too.

The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
The thin-crust pizzas popular throughout the Midwest have taken off as the “tavern-style” pizza trend | Brad Bobrow/La Merna

In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years. 

Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

“Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent  across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s. 

The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee. The Tavernetta hasn’t displaced the other pies at the restaurant; it’s ordered in addition to them, with most groups getting at least one.

In June, Los Angeles cocktail spot Bar Next Door celebrated its third anniversary by adding food to its menu. Previously, the bar had allowed customers to bring in pies from Prince Street Pizza next door since Prince Street co-owner Lawrence Longo is a partner in it. “But, you know, after having a slice, sometimes you get a little full and you’re like, I want to go to bed,” says owner and bar director Brynn Smith. “That’s the whole goal for us: for people to stay a little longer, drink more. We thought the tavern-style pizza would be perfect.” The fact that the pizzas — they’re technically listed on the menu as “bar pies,” to better go with the establishment’s name — are made exclusively for the bar by Prince Street just adds another reason for people to go out, Smith says.

The idea of the tavern as a social and convivial space is inseparable from the tavern-style pizza. Shorten’s goal with La Merna — which he runs with his wife Meg Schmitt, who grew up in St. Louis — was the feel of a Chicago dive bar or a Wisconsin supper club: a place with wood-paneled walls and checkered floors, where people could slide into a red leather booth for pitchers of beer and pizza and hang out for a while. As my colleague Francky Knapp wrote last year, the rising appeal of the “tavern” vibe across restaurants nationwide is rooted in the sense of providing somewhere homey and lived-in. At taverns, diners are beckoned to sprawl out and stay awhile; to free themselves of pretense and to tap into something welcoming in its familiarity. This, for so many people, is exemplified by the archetype of hometown pizza shop, which is forever tethered in our cultural imaginations to the trappings of a previous, simpler era.

It’s for this reason that, in Oregon, the Grosvenors have started serving their take on Bosco sticks, the stuffed breadsticks of Midwestern cafeterias, and Italian beef with giardiniera. While they could find certain Midwestern hits around Portland, it sometimes frustrated them that they couldn’t find everything in one place. According to Brennan Grosvenor, “We’re just trying to embody the feeling: If you went into a tavern in Chicago, what could you order there?”

Baby Sign Language 101: Baby’s First Signs

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Well before they utter their first words, infants as young as six months can learn to sign. Baby sign language is thought to reduce baby’s frustration levels, boost self-esteem, and bolster language development. And you don’t need to be an expert. Begin with three to five signs, saying the word as you sign it. Encourage other caregivers and siblings to join in. Then repeat, repeat, repeat. Your little one’s motor skills may not allow them to perfectly mimic the sign, so watch for hand gestures that indicate she’s trying, usually within two months of being introduced to it. Here are some great baby sign language starter signs.

Family Signs

Show your little one the sign for a person they know, like mommy, daddy, brother, grandma, or baby. It doesn’t matter if you plan to use mommy, mama, mother, or mom; the sign is the same — using an open palm with fingers spread, tap your thumb on your chin. For “daddy” and its variations, use the same hand position; only tap the forehead. Remember to point to the person as you sign.

Food Signs

 

Food is a great motivator. Teach your little eater the sign for their favorite food or the ones for eat, milk, or bottle. When Baby’s plate is empty, “more” and “all done” allow them to set the pace for the meal. To sign “more,” press the thumb and fingertips of each hand together, and then tap the fingertips of the two hands together several times. Look for baby to put their fists or palms together as they start to get the hang of this sign.

Animal Signs

Animal signs are fun because they often mimic some aspect of the animal. Baby will love learning the signs for her beloved pet or for the animals around the neighborhood. Dog, cat, fish, and bird are all good candidates. You can even have a favorite stuffed animal act out the motions for its sign. The baby sign for “dog” is patting your hand to your hip, as if calling a dog. This is a slight modification from the American Sign Language version, which includes a snap that is too difficult for emerging motor skills.

Please and Thank You

It’s never too early to introduce your little bundle to being polite. “Please” is a great word to start with. Try this technique when it’s clear your baby desires something specific, like a toy or a drink, and he’s not upset or in any distress. Say that you will get it and ask if they can say “please,” demonstrating the sign as you do so. Then give them the item, regardless of whether they do the sign or not. Eventually the concept will begin to sink in, and they’ll be doing it on his own. With a flat hand and thumb extended, sign “please” by moving the palm of your hand in a circular motion in front of your chest.

Daily Routine Signs

Use the repetition of baby’s routine as an opportunity to teach her the signs of daily life. Learn “sleep” for naptime and bedtime. Let baby know it’s playtime by signing “play,” and then show her the sign for whatever you are playing with, be it a ball, blocks, or a book. The sign for “bath” mimics the act of washing. To do this sign, simply move both your fists up and down in front of your chest as if scrubbing it.

 

Should We Look to Hunter-Gatherer Societies as Models of Equality?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

On the shores of Lake Eyasi in Northern Tanzania, a small number of people still hunt and gather their food. Today a Hadza hunter has taken down an animal—a kudu. He can’t carry it by himself, so he cuts his poisoned arrow from its flank, covers the carcass in dry grass to hide it from hyenas, and heads home to enlist help.

News spreads like wildfire across the camp. Everybody rushes to the kill. They divide up the carcass, carry it back, then divvy it up some more. Every family gets some, and after the meat is cooked, there’s more sharing still. On a good day like today, the hunter and his family retain half, but sometimes they keep much less. People may even walk from other camps, and almost everyone, from miles around, is entitled to a share.

The post-hunt scene above is idyllic. It paints a quintessential picture of egalitarianism and equality. But our experience as anthropologists working with the Hadza people of Tanzania and with the Tsimane people of Bolivia’s Amazon rainforest is that inequalities are part of even the most equal of societies. Furthermore, the equalities that do exist often emerge from selfish motives.

Insights like ours are revising researchers’ understanding of both the past and how people operate today, as inequality grows globally. Hunter-gatherers are often at the center of heated academic battles about what it means to be human, including debates over gender, war, and peace and whether humans are, by nature, egalitarian or hierarchical.

Sometimes these debates oversimplify. So we pulled together hundreds of different sources covering more than a century of ethnography to find out just how egalitarian hunter-gatherer and other subsistence societies really are.

        People living in egalitarian societies like the Tsimane have similar, modest levels of material wealth (Christopher von Rueden).              

Is any society truly egalitarian?

Anthropologists have called hunter-gatherers such as the Hadza “egalitarian societies,” with equalities of power, wealth, and social status. And it’s true that the traditional subsistence communities we work with successfully limit many inequalities.

Most families have similar, modest possessions and store little material wealth. When leaders do emerge, they are highly constrained in how they can direct other people. People often make decisions via consensus, and, if they cannot reach agreement, families will move away.

While the same anthropologists also emphasize that some hunter-gatherers are very unequal and that equality takes deliberate effort, the prevailing impression is that equality characterizes hunter-gatherers, past and present.

A quick trip to your local bookshop will reveal grand claims, including that past hunter-gatherer societies had no permanent leadership and no hierarchy, inequality only spread with the development of agriculture, and humans are noble by nature.

Some scientists are pushing back against these utopian portrayals. The popular bestseller, The Dawn of Everything, calls egalitarianism a “Garden of Eden narrative” that lacks substance. Other scholars have called egalitarian societies a scientific origin myth.

So where does the truth lie? Probably somewhere in between.

Inequality among hunter-gatherers

In our recent wide-ranging review of the evidence from the early 20th century onward, we found that no society lacked inequality.

Like the Hadza, the Ju/’Hoansi of the Kalahari desert in southern Africa had little property and widespread food-sharing, yet certain families controlled valuable hunting territory surrounding waterholes. The Bayaka of the central African rainforest had little material inequality, yet they bought, sold, and hoarded ritual knowledge. The Philippine Agta controlled and arranged their children’s marriages, hugely discriminating against orphans.

Gender inequalities were commonplace. The Tanzanian Hadza keep certain cuts of meat exclusively for older men, and give more respect to better hunters. Across societies, women have universally done more direct parenting, and men have participated more frequently in community-level decision making.

No society lacks informal leadership. Age was another source of inequality. In many Australian societies, older men controlled ritual secrets and had power over the marriage choices of younger relatives.

Equality arises from self-interest

Our study also found that although hunter-gatherers are frequently generous, the processes limiting inequality often do not require any generosity. Instead, equality often results from people’s self-interested motives to acquire resources, status, and autonomy.

A great example of this is food-sharing. Although each Hadza family gets a cut of kudu meat, it often happens not because the hunter’s family prioritizes total equality, but because other families request it vociferously. It can involve more taking than giving, often through strongly stated demands or complaints.

Another example is ridicule and mockery of stingy or braggadocious individuals, which keeps greed and bravado in check. Paradoxically, status-seeking can also promote equality, when cooperating to gain status diffuses status throughout the community.

        The Tsimane reach decisions through community deliberation. Some have more influential voices than others, but everyone has the opportunity to contribute (Christopher von Rueden).              

Egalitarianism is alive and well

Many of these inequality-reducing processes are familiar to people everywhere.

Ridicule and mockery are ubiquitous in industrialized societies, from political cartoons to social media. Lampooning authority is even common in autocratic societies where governments spend large sums of money suppressing speech. Demands and complaints form the basis of strikes and collective bargaining. And key to social mobility is cooperation among people of different social strata.

Like many great debates, the truth is complex: Our ancient hunter-gatherer ancestors didn’t reach the giddy heights of inequality seen in other societies over the last 6,000 years, but putting hunter-gatherers on a pedestal as paragons of equality is a mistake. Sweeping statements about equality in the past blind us to the tools that probably allowed ancient hunter-gatherers to combat inequality, just as they and people in all societies do today.

Egalitarianism, then, is best seen not as perfect equality but as a set of tools that people use to secure their own autonomy and access to resources. Equality along one dimension or another might be the outcome, but it doesn’t rely on people being amazingly noble or completely lacking in greed or status-seeking. If you consider egalitarianism as equality-creating processes, you can see that it is a widespread reality among hunter-gatherer societies, and beyond.

Just Some Things I Tried and Have Opinions About

I don’t know that anyone cares about this, but I spend too much time thinking about these things.

  • This recipe works best with Greek yogurt rather than regular yogurt! It gets more evenly brown and has a nice rise like you see in the pictures, whereas regular yogurt is wetter and makes the batter more dense. (It does still work, if you’re in a pinch.)
  • You want rolled old fashioned oats for this one – don’t use the Costco sprouted oats, and do not use quick oats. They both absorb liquid in a different way. I just buy the Quaker old fashioned rolled oats!
  • I did try this with grating the apples, but I prefer just throwing them into the blender at the end of blending. 1) It’s easier, and 2) I felt like the muffins got a better rise with the apples being more incorporated.
  • Our previous muffin recipes similar to this have used maple syrup, but it gets really expensive to use that much real maple syrup, so I switched to brown sugar for this one!
  • The streusel is optional – they’re still pretty and delicious without!
  • The batter will bake differently the longer it sits! I find he sweet spot is about 10 minutes. You may find that your second batch gets a bit more puffy than your first.

Description

These apple streusel meal prep muffins are super wholesome, loaded with streusel, and ideal for prepping ahead! My kids love them!

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Muffins:

  • 1 cup whole milk Greek yogurt
  • 3 eggs
  • 3 1/2 cups old-fashioned oats (I don’t recommend the Costco sprouted oats or quick oats)
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar, lightly packed
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 2 cups roughly chopped Honeycrisp apples (I did not peel mine)

For the Streusel:

  • 4 tablespoons butter, melted
  • 1/3 cup brown sugar
  • 1/2 cup + 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Prep: Preheat the oven to 350 degrees. Spray a muffin tin with avocado oil.
  2. Blend: Add ingredients to the blender in order, but leave out the apples for now. Blend in short bursts until it starts to move (you may need to help it along initially). Once it gets going, blend for 15-20 seconds, until mostly smooth. There should still be some flecks of texture, similar to a bran muffin batter.
  3. Apples: Add the apple chunks; blend again for 15-20 seconds until they are worked into the batter in small flecks.
  4. Streusel: Mix the streusel ingredients in a small bowl until it resembles small pebbles – I start with a fork, and then mix with my hands. (The batter can rest for 10 minutes during this time.)
  5. Assemble: Pour batter in the muffin tin – filling each cup 2/3 of the way full, but not all the way to the top. Add a spoonful of streusel on each one. (I typically get 16-17 muffins from this recipe, so I bake in two batches.)
  6. Bake: Bake for 16-18 minutes, until the tops spring back when gently pressed. Cool for 10-15 minutes so they are easier to remove from the pan. I love eating these warm with a shmear of butter!
  7. Storing: I put them in a ziplock bag and keep them in the freezer or fridge. To reheat, I pop them in the microwave for 30 seconds (from fridge) or 60 seconds (from frozen).

Pastina Soup (Italian Penicillin)

4.9 from 10 reviews

  • Total Time: 45 minutes
  • Yield: 6–8 servings 1x
Print Pin

Description

This soup has become my go-to recipe when my family is sick. The broth is amazing – simple, light, flavorful, and nourishing! It’s absolute comfort food. 

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Broth:

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 large carrots, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 2 stalks of celery, cut into large chunks
  • half of 1 yellow onion, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 4 cloves garlic, peeled and smashed
  • 10 cups chicken or vegetable broth (I use 10 cups water plus 2 tablespoons Better Than Bouillon chicken base)
  • 1 teaspoon salt

For Serving: 

  • 1 1/2 cups pastina (I use Stelline from the Good and Gather brand at Target)
  • freshly grated Parmesan cheese for topping
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Sauté Veggies: Heat oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high heat. Add the chunky carrots, celery, onion, and garlic to the pot. Sauté for 5 minutes. 
  2. Simmer: Add broth and salt. Simmer for 30 minutes, until the vegetables are very tender.
  3. Remove Veggies: Use a slotted spoon to transfer the vegetable chunks to a bowl. Set aside to cool for a few minutes (you don’t want to add scorching hot veggies to your plastic blender). 
  4. Blend: When the veggies have cooled a bit, blend them with 1-2 cups of broth until very smooth. Add the blended mixture back to the pot; stir to incorporate.
  5. Pastina: Cook pasta according to package directions. Drain and toss with olive oil and a pinch of salt. 
  6. Serve: Put a scoop of pastina in a shallow bowl. Ladle the steaming-hot broth over the top, then top each bowl with freshly grated Parmesan.

Yat gaw mein, also known as Baltimore yak or dirty yak, is Baltimore carryout comfort food at its finest. A Chinese American remix of Beijing’s zha jiang mian, the dish dates back to the late 19th century, when Chinese immigrants started settling in the city. Setting up shop in traditionally Black and working-class neighborhoods, Chinese cooks adapted their recipes to locally available ingredients, swapping fermented pastes for a simpler, soy-based gravy. Over time, something new emerged: savory wheat noodles, stir-fried with shrimp, beef, and vegetables and punctuated with a hard-boiled egg. Today you can find the beloved dish at counter spots around town like Jimmy’s Carryout, Danny’s, and Pimlico Chinese Carry Out.

In this at-home version from Steve Chu, the chef behind Baltimore’s Ekiben, smoky wok-charred noodles are tossed with umami-packed soy sauce and oyster sauce, rich fat, and sweet caramelized onions. What makes the dish “dirty” is the flavor that develops when soy sauce, sugar, and aromatics scorch against the hot metal of the wok, creating deep savory notes balanced by subtle sweetness. Be sure to keep the wok on high heat and stir frequently, since this allows the wok hei—which translates to “the breath of a wok” and refers to that elusive smokiness—to work its magic. The finishing squeeze of ketchup might sound unusual, but it delivers the acidic punch that makes the dish complete.

Makes: 2–4 Time: 25 minutes

Ingredients

  • Kosher salt
  • 1 lb. fresh wheat noodles, such as lo mein or udon
  • ¼ cup duck fat, chicken fat, vegetable oil, or other cooking fat
  • 1½ cups bean sprouts
  • 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
  • 1 medium scallion, thinly sliced
  • ¼ cup dark soy sauce, plus more if needed
  • 2 Tbsp. light soy sauce
  • 2 Tbsp. oyster sauce
  • 1 Tbsp. MSG or chicken bouillon powder
  • 1 Tbsp. sugar
  • 1–2 tsp. Tianjin chile flakes or crushed red chile flakes
  • Steamed or poached shrimp or medium-rare seared beef slices, for topping
  • 1 hard-boiled egg, peeled, for topping
  • Ketchup and hot sauce, for serving

Instructions

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil. Add the noodles and cook for 1 minute less than the package instructions so they remain slightly undercooked. Drain, then rinse briefly under cold water. Set aside to cool. 
  2. Heat a wok over high until lightly smoking. Add the duck fat, followed by the bean sprouts, onion, and scallion. Stir-fry until the mixture begins to brown around the edges, about 1 minute. Add the noodles, both soy sauces, the oyster sauce, MSG, sugar, and chile flakes. Stir-fry, tossing continuously so the sauce coats the noodles evenly and begins to brown against the wok, 1–4 minutes. (If a darker color is desired, add a little more dark soy sauce.)
  3. Add 1½ cups of water to the wok, scraping up the browned bits, and simmer until the noodles absorb some of the liquid, about 1 minute. (If you prefer a saucier dish, add a little more water.) Add the shrimp and hard-boiled egg, tossing gently so the egg warms through and takes on some of the dark soy color without breaking apart. 
  4. Serve immediately, with ketchup and hot sauce on the side.

If you’ve ever been starstruck by a perfect Cinderella pumpkin or a stately stalk of Brussels sprouts, you likely understand the appeal of a book focused on a single fruit or vegetable. While books like this aren’t always long, they typically go deep, with the fervor of an author who is fully obsessed.

One charming example, The Core of an Onion: Peeling the Rarest Common Food by Mark Kurlansky, melds authority with a dose of irreverence. First published in 2023, it’s a slim 200 pages, but the writer (who’s also written nonfiction books dedicated to oysters, salt, and cod) packs a lot in. “Onions have a way of taking on unexpected significance,” he posits. The first section covers the allium’s history and cultural impact. (Did you know that ancient Egyptians tied them to mummies’ feet as a preservative?) Kurlansky then offers loosely structured recipes for breads, sauces, and more, all centering the humble bulb.

Elevating a perhaps overlooked ingredient is a core tenet of single-­subject cookbookery. In The Compleat Squash: A Passionate Grower’s Guide to Pumpkins, Squash, and Gourds, a canonical 2004 ode to members of the Cucurbitaceae ­family, master ­gardener Amy Goldman Fowler’s passion is abundantly evident. “Sometimes I don’t know where pumpkins begin and I end,” she writes, before segueing into a series of squash and gourd portraits, each showing an individual specimen more striking than the last. Fowler shares recipes too, in addition to breed spotlights, resources for seed ­buying, and advice on growing your own.

Some such tomes aim to introduce the reader to the joys of less ubiquitous ingredients. Lane Selman and Margarett Waterbury’s Radicchio: A Celebration of the Beloved Italian Vegetable tells the “human and natural history” of the bitter chicory, along with instructions for how to grow it and cook with it. Selman is the founder of the Culinary Breeding Network, an initiative that rallies scientists, farmers, and chefs to geek out over new produce varieties. The book gets weird in the best way possible, with profiles of Italian growers and recipes including gnocchi with radicchio pesto, radicchio with prosciutto ­gratin, and chocolate–radicchio cake (why not?).

Radicchio reads almost like a community cookbook, and that’s intentional. Selman’s inspiration to pursue the topic was the sheer lack of writing on this oddball vegetable: “All of these people that we’ve met on our way down the rabbit hole needed to be included,” she says. “They’re the ones who have given us all this information. Sometimes it takes a village.”

Other single-subject cookbooks showcase pantry ­staples that are rarely in the spotlight. Anna Hezel’s Little Book of Olives: A Guide to Cooking, Eating, and Drinking for the Brine-Inclined presents recipes including cantaloupe and crushed-olive panzanella, Castelvetrano and pistachio pesto, and a whole chapter of olive desserts, plus shopping tips and a breakdown of varieties. The book’s retro aesthetic lends ­gravitas to an ingredient often relegated to garnish status.

Finally, some books exist more to dazzle than to inform, as is the case with pop artist Ed Ruscha and River Café chef Ruthie Rogers’s 2025 release Squeeze Me: Lemon Recipes & Art. In the pair’s high-gloss love letter to the lemon, pop art–coded photos with cheeky captions and a suite of minimalist recipes—such as lemon–­mascarpone tart and limoncello—make this ode to the simple citrus equally at home on a coffee table or the kitchen shelf.

While styles and forms vary, these books serve a ­common goal: to illuminate the sometimes surprising pleasures of a particular fruit or vegetable. That they manage to do so in such delightful fashion is proof that, once in a while, ­working with less really is more.

Description

Pan-fried chicken in a luscious Cajun cream sauce, served with mashed potatoes and green beans – loving this easy, cozy weeknight dinner!

Ingredients

Units USM

Chicken:

  • 1 tablespoon avocado oil
  • 1 lb. chicken breast
  • 1 1/2 teaspoon Cajun seasoning (I used McCormick)

Sauce:

  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 1 shallot, minced or thinly sliced
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste

Instructions

  1. Prep the Chicken: Cut the chicken into thinner pieces horizontally, or pound them out a bit so they’re thinner.
  2. Cook the Chicken: Season the chicken filets with Cajun seasoning. Heat the oil in a skillet over medium-high heat (I use a nonstick for this). Sauté the chicken on each side for 3-4 minutes, until the internal temperature reaches 165 and the outside is golden; remove from the pan and set aside on a plate.
  3. Sauté Shallots: Wipe off any overly browned bits from the pan. Turn the heat to medium-low so the butter and shallots don’t burn. Add butter and shallots; saute until softened and fragrant.
  4. Make The Roux: Add tomato paste and Cajun seasoning; saute for 3-4 minutes until the tomato paste has developed a rich color. Add flour and mix; cook for 2-3 minutes to remove any floury taste.
  5. Finish Sauce: Add broth gradually; whisk to incorporate after each addition. Once it’s smooth, add heavy cream and whisk again to incorporate. Let it simmer for 5-10 minutes to thicken slightly. Add chicken back to the pan so it is partially submerged with sauce.
  6. Serve: Serve pieces of chicken with a spoonful of the sauce over the top. I love this SO MUCH with mashed potatoes and broccoli – see notes!

Notes

To air fry the chicken: Air fry at 375 for 10-12 minutes, or until at least 165 degrees internal temperature.

Roasted Potatoes and Broccoli (Oven): Toss 3-4 cups halved baby potatoes with avocado oil and salt on a sheet pan. Roast the potatoes at 425 for 15 minutes. Scoot the potatoes over, add 3-4 cups broccoli and ¼ cup water to the same sheet pan, and roast for another 15 minutes.

Roasted Potatoes and Broccoli (Air Fryer): Toss 2 cups halved baby potatoes with avocado oil and salt. Air fry at 375 for 12 minutes. Add 2 cups frozen or fresh broccoli; air fry at 400 for another 10-12 minutes.
