Sir Patrick Stewart Dies at 86 After Battle With Alzheimer's, Lewy Body Dementia

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Sir Patrick Stewart, best-known for portraying Captain Jean-Luc Picard in “Star Trek” and Professor Charles Xavier in the “X-Men” film franchise, has died. He was 86.

The legendary actor and thespian passed away on October 28 after a “brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia,” according to his longtime spokesperson, Kelly Bush Novak, in a statement to The Hollywood Reporter.

He died peacefully at his home in Los Angeles, where he was receiving care from his wife, Sunny Ozell.

“He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all,” Bush Novak wrote in the statement. “We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace.”

Stewart’s acting career spanned more than six decades across theater, television, and film.

A longtime member of the Royal Shakespeare Company, he rose to international fame in 1987 with “Star Trek: The Next Generation” and later became known for his portrayal of Professor Charles Xavier in the 2000 film “X-Men” and its numerous sequels.

He was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II in 2010 for his contributions to drama.

What to know about Alzheimer’s, Lewy body dementia

Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia are two progressive neurological conditions that can affect memory, thinking, behavior, and a person’s ability to perform everyday activities.

According to the National Institute on Aging (NIA), both conditions can cause dementia, a term used to describe a decline in cognitive abilities severe enough to interfere with daily life.

Alzheimer’s disease is the most common cause of dementia. The condition is associated with abnormal buildups of proteins called amyloid and tau in the brain, which disrupt communication between nerve cells and contribute to their damage.

The NIA states that memory problems are often among the earliest symptoms of Alzheimer’s. As the disease progresses, people may experience increasing difficulty with language, reasoning, decision making, and recognizing familiar people.

Lewy body dementia, meanwhile, is associated with abnormal deposits of a protein called alpha-synuclein in the brain. These deposits, known as Lewy bodies, affect brain chemicals involved in thinking, movement, mood, and sleep, according to the NIA.

People with Lewy body dementia may experience changes in attention and alertness, visual hallucinations, muscle stiffness, difficulty walking, and sleep disturbances.

According to the Alzheimer’s Association, the two conditions may co-occur, and their overlapping symptoms may make diagnosis more challenging.

Currently, neither condition has a cure, but treatments are available to help manage symptoms. Certain medications can also slow cognitive decline in some people with early Alzheimer’s disease.

The great British actor, Sir Patrick Stewart, passed away yesterday. While Stewart began his career as a stage actor and performed in many Shakespeare plays, he is best known for his portrayal of Captain Jean-Luc Picard, in Star Trek: The Next Generation. Thanks to his impressive performance, Stewart and the character he played became science fiction icons.

Stewart brought extraordinary depth and perception to the role. And the character is an interesting contrast to the more instinctive and aggressive, less cerebral, Captain Kirk in the original Star Trek Series. Perhaps more than any other character, Stewart's Picard epitomized Star Trek's progressive liberal values: Enlightenment rationalism, technological optimism (highlighted by his defense of the rights of the android Data), racial and ethnic equality, and commitment to peace and diplomacy.

In many ways, Picard and TNG promoted these ideas to an even greater extent than the original series. Whereas Captain Kirk was often belligerent and acted on instinct, Picard sought diplomatic solutions to conflict, and tried to avoid doing anything drastic without careful deliberation. In the original series, the Klingons and Romulans are mostly depicted as evil and irreconcilable enemies of the Federation, in TNG the Klingons have become allies and there is hope that there will be peace with the Romulans, as well. In multiple episodes, Picard takes the lead in working to maintain and improve Federation relations with these and other previously hostile alien societies.

There is much to admire in this ideology and Picard's advocacy of it. But, as I have pointed out in a 2016 article (see also this more recent piece), Star Trek's ideology has a dark side, as well: its uncritical embrace of socialism. This is also reflected in the Federation's seeming ideological homogeneity. Picard also exemplifies this negative aspect of Star Trek ideology, with his thinly veiled disdain for private enterprise, and people who seek to make a profit.

As depicted by Stewart, the Picard character also highlights another key tension in Star Trek ideology, this one relating to cultural diversity. Picard is supposed to be French; but other than his noticeable British accent, he seems little different from other human Federation characters. The "Doylist" real-world explanation for this is that Stewart himself was British, and trying to get him to speak with a French accent might have had bad results. But the "Watsonian" in-universe effect of this is to accentuate how the human characters in Star Trek have largely lost their cultural distinctiveness. French, British, and other human cultures have largely blended together, by the 24th century (when the show is set). Star Trek may champion diversity. But, culturally and politically, the human characters are mostly people who look different, but largely think alike on almost all major issues. The few dissenters are generally depicted as either villains or fools.

As  I have previously noted, this ideological homogeneity and uncritical acceptance of Federation ideology gets challenged more in later Star Trek series, beginning with Deep Space 9. DS-9 and later series depict more ideological conflict among humans, and raise serious questions about Federation ideology. But Stewart's Picard remains an iconic depiction of Federation ideals.

As always in assessing actors, we should not assume that the character necessarily reflects the views of the actor. We can and should recognize Picard's shortcomings and those of Federation ideology, without blaming Stewart for them. Indeed, his performance deserves credit for providing a valuable window into these issues.

The more recent Picard series is not as good as TNG. But I would argue the blame for that rests with the producers and their problematic plotlines, rather than Stewart.

In addition to his justly famous Star Trek role, Stewart also starred in the X-Men movie series (which I won't comment on, as I know little about it). His first significant science fiction role was playing Gurney Halleck in the 1984 movie version of Dune.

Patrick Stewart as Gurney Halleck in Dune (1984). (Universal Pictures)

 

The 1984 Dune movie is an incoherent mess, and not as good as the more recent Denis Villeneuve version (see my reviews of the latter here and here). But Stewart did a great job with his role, and really brought Halleck to life.

In sum, Sir Patrick Stewart was a great actor, and well deserves his status as an SF icon. He will be greatly missed.

48 Years Ago, a $1.98 Mask Helped Turn a $300,000 Horror Film Into a Hollywood Hit

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Forty-eight years after Halloween (1978) terrified moviegoers, one detail about John Carpenter’s breakthrough horror film remains astonishing: Michael Myers’ infamous mask cost just $1.98.

Released October 25, 1978, Halloween was made for approximately $300,000 and eventually earned more than $70 million worldwide, according to the Library of Congress. The modest independent production helped establish a formula Hollywood would spend decades trying to replicate.

How a $1.98 Mask Became a Horror Legend

Michael Myers’ expressionless face wasn’t the result of an expensive Hollywood effects department.

Production designer Tommy Lee Wallace purchased a Captain Kirk mask from a Hollywood costume shop and transformed it into something considerably more disturbing.

Photo by Getty Images

Carpenter recalled the process on the official Halloween website.

“It didn’t look anything like William Shatner after Tommy got through with it,” the filmmaker explained.

The crew widened the eye openings, changed the coloring, and created the pale, emotionless face that audiences still recognize.

The production’s financial limitations extended beyond its villain. Jamie Lee Curtis reportedly earned $8,000 for portraying Laurie Strode, while Nick Castle received $25 per day to play the masked killer.

John Carpenter Turned Financial Limitations Into Suspense

Photo by Getty Images

Rather than rely on elaborate effects, Carpenter built tension through carefully controlled camera movements, shadows, and an unsettling musical score.

In a Song Exploder interview, Carpenter explained why he composed the soundtrack himself.

“When we made ‘Halloween’ back in ’78, we didn’t have any money for a composer, so I always knew that I was going to be the composer.”

The director had only three days in a recording studio to complete the music.

“Everything else I was going to fake it, just improvise it,” Carpenter recalled.

That distinctive piano melody became inseparable from Michael Myers.

The Horror Movie That Changed Hollywood’s Expectations

According to the Library of Congress, Halloween inspired numerous low-budget slasher films seeking to reproduce its financial success.

Carpenter initially had considerably smaller ambitions.

“I was just trying to make a little horror movie,” he explained in an interview about the production.

Instead, the film launched a long-running franchise and helped establish Curtis as a leading horror actress.

Nearly five decades later, Halloween remains a reminder that memorable filmmaking doesn’t necessarily require a blockbuster budget. Sometimes the most frightening thing in Hollywood starts with a two-dollar mask.

“Black Zombie”

I will bet good money the first bong-ripping undergrad to declare that all horror is metaphor cited George Romero’s “Night of the Living Dead” as a prime example. That’s not wrong; Romero’s 1968 benchmark established the modern cinematic zombie as a stand-in for mindless capitalist consumption. But “Black Zombie” rewinds to the monster’s Haitian roots to examine an even darker origin tied to that country’s shared history with ours.

You may be wondering why I’d recommend a documentary in a horror roundup, let alone one that explains how the modern movie flesh gobbler began as a manifestation of white fear. The answer is that Maya Annik Bedward’s film doubles as an impressive cinematic syllabus.

By braiding America’s media history with its national history, Bedward and her assemblage of academics, creatives, authors and Vodou practitioners invite us to think differently about the prejudices driving movies like, say, Victor Halperin’s 1932 effort “White Zombie,” or 1943’s “I Walked with a Zombie.”

“Black Zombie” opens with a truth that has long escaped those fascinated by the shambling undead. To some, they’re simply mindless hunters. But Haitians understand the horror of zombies to be one of dispossession, a curse enacted by enslavers that binds victims to mindless servitude, denying them liberation in the afterlife. But long after Romero transformed them from blank-eyed puppets to ravenous murder machines, Bedward’s film demonstrates how Hollywood’s version of the zombie impacts America’s perception of Vodou, Haitians and immigration.

“Black Zombie” not only asks us to think differently about an overexposed subgenre, but also thoughtfully examines Vodou’s central role in the revolution that drove out French colonizers, and France’s imposition of debt to punish Haitians for winning their freedom.

All of this informs the zombie’s continued proliferation across popular culture, in films like “World War Z” and shows such as “The Last of Us” and “The Walking Dead.” They’re terrific entertainment, certainly. But each is, in its own way, built upon decades’ worth of misrepresenting Haiti’s syncretic faith to the detriment and demonization of a people. At the same time, “Black Zombie” recognizes this subgenre’s cultural resilience, arguing that recognizing the zombie’s true roots broadens its narrative possibilities.

Proving that point, one of Bedward’s subjects, a Vodou priest, says he grew up loving Wes Craven’s “The Serpent and the Rainbow,” despite its message that visitors to his country risk having their nuts nailed to a chair.  “It’s a fantastical world to scare children,” he says, before adding that his main problem is its inaccurate messaging about his faith. “We say, ‘Wow – Haitians are devils.’ That’s what we’re taught.”

I’d nearly forgotten about that movie. Now I might rewatch it for the first time in decades and expect to be horrified for entirely different reasons.

Stephen King’s classic may be more than half a century old and has undergone multiple adaptations, but it endures because its central moral that high school is hell never ages. Still, Mike Flanagan’s interpretation has divided the audience. Some, including King, appreciate Flanagan’s kinder portrait of Carrie White (Summer H. Howell) as an innocent unjustly sequestered from the world by fundamentalist parents who repudiate her extraordinary power. Others disagree with Flanagan’s alteration to the story’s climactic scene, which shifts culpability for the prom massacre from Carrie to a character created expressly for the series.

Pretty much everyone questions the season finale, which hints at taking an “X-Men”-style turn for its recently greenlit second season.

As someone who accepts nothing is sacrosanct now, not to mention a fan of the creator’s other adaptations, including “The Fall of the House of Usher,” I appreciated Flanagan using Carrie’s story to critique ignorance, knowledge suppression, and the dehumanizing effect of social media.

Besides, nearly all the key elements of “Carrie” remain. The bullies are still awful, and the famous “plug it up” shower scene in Brian DePalma’s 1976 standard-setter is worsened by her tormentors posting Carrie’s frightened sobs on social media.

A protester holds a French flag at Republic Square during a demonstration against rising living costs, joining students’ grievances, in Paris, Saturday, Oct. 17, 2026. (AP Photo/Thibault Camus)

The ongoing protests are fueling debate over demonstrators’ fundamental rights and the state’s responsibility to maintain order. In Paris, the route of the demonstrations has become a political and philosophical flashpoint.

Protesting is a fundamental right under the French Constitution. Unions must declare their intended route in advance so authorities can prepare, but do not need permission to demonstrate because protests fall under freedom of expression. Authorities can impose restrictions only when there is a serious safety threat.

On Oct. 24, “Act II” of the demonstrations was pushed to the outskirts of Paris, where hundreds of thousands marched in the rain. This week, the interunion alliance declared that “Act III” would follow a traditional demonstration route from Place de la Bastille to Place de la Nation, used for similar marches for decades.

Paris police prefect Patrice Faure then announced that demonstrations along the route would be banned “for a while.” The prefecture said “Act III” would be allowed only along the city limits, citing Halloween as a public order risk because of potential masks, fake weapons and social media calls to emulate the American horror movie “The Purge.”

The interunion alliance, calling the decision an infringement on democratic rights, appealed to the Paris Administrative Court on Friday evening and won.

“The judge in chambers begins by recalling the scope of the freedom to demonstrate, which extends to the right to choose the time, place, and modalities of the gathering,” Chirinne Ardakani, a lawyer representing the interunion alliance, live-posted from the hearings. “The judge does not see how the imposed alternative route would avoid ‘clashes’ … alleged by a ‘vague and unsubstantiated’ note.’”

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez announced the prefecture would immediately appeal to the Conseil d’État, France’s equivalent of the Supreme Court. Hearings began at 10 p.m. Friday and continued into early Saturday.

The court ultimately ruled in Nuñez’s favor, citing a heightened risk of public disorder, the Oct. 17 riots, threats to businesses along the Bastille-Nation route and demands on police resources. It concluded that the alternative route preserved the right to protest.

Roughly 70,000 officers were deployed nationwide Saturday.

In a lifetime of living in this body, I have known no more powerful a homecoming than music — nothing roots us more firmly into the house of being, nothing levitates us more buoyantly to that transcendent place beyond marrow and mind. Stripped of its nihilistic drama, there is an elemental cry of truth, for me at least, in Nietzsche’s pronouncement: “Without music life would be a mistake.” Even Edna St. Vincent Millay, for all her lyrical love of life, echoed the sentiment: “Without music I should wish to die. Every writer unblinded by ego will concede this.

No writer has conceded it more beautifully or with more rapturous reverence for the life of the body in the life of music than Richard Powers in his exquisite 2003 novel The Time of Our Singing (public library).

Delia fell in love with singing. Singing was something that might make sense of a person. Singing might make more sense of life than living had to start with.

There is a sound like the burning sun. A sound like the surf of blood pumping through my ears. The women start by themselves, their note as spreading and dimensionless as my father says the present is. Keee, the letter-box slots of their mouths release — just the syllable of glee little Ruth made before we persuaded her to learn to talk. Rises a major third, that first interval any child any color anywhere learns to sing. Four lips curve upon the vowel, a pocket of air older than the author who set it there.

I know in my body what notes come next, even though I have nothing, yet, to call them. The high voice rises a perfect fifth, lifting off from the lower note’s bed. I hear these two lines bending space as they speed away from each other, hurling outward, each standing still while the other moves. They near their starting pitch from opposite sides, the shared spot where they must impossibly meet back up. More lines splinter, copy, and set off on their own. Aaay-laay Aaay-laay-eee! This stripped-down simple singsong blooms like a firework peony. Everywhere in the awakened air, in a shower of staggered entrances, I hear the first phrase, keyed up, melted down, and rebuilt. Everest 1971: Ambition, Death, and Dispute

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In the spring of 1971, only one party attempted to climb Everest. Led by Norman Dyhrenfurth and Jimmy Roberts, this ambitious expedition planned to split into two groups. One party would go for the steep Southwest Face, while the other would attempt the West Ridge.

It was meant to be as much a test of international cooperation as an extraordinary climbing effort. But what happened turned out to be more complicated and controversial than anyone had expected.

Before the team even arrived in Nepal, the venture was heavily commercialized and advertised to the public. The BBC secured exclusive television rights and embedded a nine-person film crew directly into the expedition. At the same time, the British newspaper rights were sold to The Sunday Times, which sent along prominent journalists like Murray Sayle. The media machinery relentlessly hyped the expedition beforehand, boldly framing the climb as a mere formality and setting public expectations for a historic success.

Difficult goals

The expedition itself came from a merger of two plans. A British-Norwegian group asked Roberts to lead a Southwest Face attempt. Dyhrenfurth was already organizing the same route, so they joined forces. As the combined team was large, they decided to try two routes at once.

The Southwest Face was the main goal, specifically aiming for a direct, vertical line (a direttissima), while the other group targeted the complete, aesthetic West Ridge route. Both goals were very difficult, and climbers chose their preferred route.

Jimmy Roberts in Nepal. Photo: The Nepal Times

 

The Southwest Face team included British climbers Dougal Haston and Don Whillans, Japanese climbers Naomi Uemura and Reizo Ito, and others. The West Ridge team included Austrian Wolfgang Axt, Swiss climbers Michel and Yvette Vaucher, Norwegian climbers Odd Eliassen and Jon Teigland, American David Isles, and Indian Major Harsh Vardhan Bahuguna. Frenchman Pierre Mazeaud and Italian Carlo Mauri later joined the West Ridge side.

In total, the whole expedition had 31 foreign climbers from a dozen countries, as well as several Sherpas, including Ang Phurba and Sonam Girme. According to early planning notes, neither leader had personal summit ambitions. Both described themselves as the older men of the team.

The Southwest Face of Everest. Photo: Wikimedia

 

Setting up the routes

Base Camp was established on March 22, 1971, at 5,425m. Both teams worked through the Khumbu Icefall. Dyhrenfurth later said it was in the most difficult and dangerous state he had ever seen it. They set up Advance Base Camp on the Western Cwm, then separated.

The Southwest Face team pushed higher. The West Ridge team, with Axt as coordinator, began setting up their own camps, although supplies arrived slowly. Some of the West Ridge climbers didn’t want to carry heavy loads. A long storm in late April made conditions worse for both teams and delayed everything by two to three weeks. Dyhrenfurth’s own account in the American Alpine Journal confirms the early harmony among the team and the exceptional difficulties of the Icefall that spring.

The 1971 Everest expedition. Photo: 'Surrender to Everest'/imdb.com

 

One member dies

On April 18, Bahuguna and Axt of the West Ridge team were descending from a high camp in a storm at about 7,000m. They were not tied to each other. Axt had left his harness and climbing rope higher up to save weight, knowing fixed ropes were already in place. He crossed a long fixed-rope traverse by hand, waited for Bahuguna, saw him wave, then continued down because he was exhausted. He later heard screams and raised the alarm.

A rescue party reached Bahuguna still clipped to the fixed rope, about 25m from easier ground. He had lost a mitten and a crampon, and his face was covered by ice. Attempts to lower him failed in the storm and darkness, and he finally died of cold and exhaustion. Bahuguna’s fingers and face were crusted with ice, his eyes were wide, and he could not recognize Whillans or speak.

Major Harsh Vardhan Bahuguna on the West Ridge. Photo: Wolfgang Axt/The Himalayan Journal

 

According to Roberts’ report in the Himalayan Database, the rescuers had to leave Bahuguna because it would be completely dark in 10 minutes, and the others would freeze too. Roberts later described the death as the result of a series of mistakes by Bahuguna and Axt themselves, and noted that allowing them to go so high alone had been a factor.

Bahuguna’s body was later recovered and cremated near Base Camp.

Inquiry and blame

Dyhrenfurth held an inquiry after the fatal incident. Axt said that the fixed ropes made roping up unnecessary and that he didn’t realize how serious things were until the screams. He later explained that if he had stayed longer, he risked losing his own fingers and toes.

“What should I do? One hour later and I am frozen,” he said. He added that he couldn’t pull Bahuguna himself and had to go down for fresh men.

Mazeaud called it murder through negligence. Dyhrenfurth agreed that Axt should have gone back to look, but said that it was easy to judge after the event. Roberts noted that Bahuguna’s death was very sad but didn’t determine the expedition's final outcome.

Norman Dyhrenfurth, Carlo Mauri, Yvette Vaucher, Reizo Ito, Pierre Mazeaud, and Michale Vaucher in 'Surrender to Everest' (1971). Photo: imdb.com

 

After the death and the storm, most of the remaining West Ridge climbers (especially Mazeaud, Mauri and the Vauchers) wanted to abandon both hard routes and switch to the normal South Col route. They said that it was more practical and gave a better chance of reaching the summit.

Many others, including most of the Southwest Face group and the leading Sherpas, believed that those climbers mainly wanted personal success: first Frenchman, first Italian, or first woman. A vote that included the Sherpas went strongly in favor of putting all remaining effort into the Southwest Face. The West Ridge group said the decision was a plot to keep them off the summit, so the press would stay focused on the Southwest Face.

Camp 2. Pumori on the left, with snow-and-ice slopes leading to the West Ridge. Photo: Wolfgang Axt/The Himalayan Journal

 

More accusations

Mazeaud’s radio message said that they expected him -- a French member of parliament -- to work like a Sherpa for Anglo-Saxons and Japanese. He said France had been insulted. Yvette Vaucher called the decision monstrous and said she had lost Everest and her friends at the same time.

According to one account, Roberts told Mazeaud to leave during a loud argument. The four went home in early May, ending further work on the West Ridge. Isles and Teigland later said that the decision to cancel the West Ridge climb, made in a highly emotional moment after Bahuguna’s death, was a great mistake. Teigland said he had dreamed of the West Ridge for two years.

Sickness, and another attempt

A virus, diagnosed by some as mononucleosis, spread through the camps and forced many climbers down, including Dyhrenfurth. Bad weather continued to block supplies. With the West Ridge group gone and fewer people left, Haston and Whillans kept pushing on the Southwest Face. Japanese climbers and Sherpas carried heavy loads high on the mountain. Camps were placed up to approx. 8,290m.

Haston and Whillans fixed ropes partway into a chimney in the Rock Band but ran out of rope, oxygen, and food. According to Roberts, they placed Camp 6 at 8,290m and were making progress every day. He said they could have traversed onto the South Buttress and reached the summit that way in one day, but that was not the route they had come to climb. Extreme cold hampered them. They needed another camp higher up. They turned back 450m short of the summit on May 21.

Roberts later said weather was the basic cause of failure: Once it goes bad, it affects everything else. He described the expedition as 50 percent successful. They failed to summit, but those who stayed worked well together.

Naomi Uemura. Photo: Wikipedia

 

Different views after the climb

Roberts said that he was bitterly disappointed after three years of work, but apart from Bahuguna’s death, he had nothing to regret. He criticized Schloemmer for contributing almost nothing and said that Axt was asked to come up and help on the Southwest Face but thought it over and went down instead.

Haston called claims that the Austrians were ready to help “complete balls.” He said that when they asked for support at Camp 5, Schloemmer replied that he needed a Sherpa to carry his personal gear. Haston added that he and Whillans were the only ones who could push hard enough to get the route going, and that the Japanese were the only others with real high-Himalayan experience.

Uemura said that he and Ito helped by carrying loads because that was their obligation, and that if the weather had been good, the expedition could have succeeded. After the 1971 expedition, Uemura decided to always climb solo thereafter.

No real authority

Axt and Schloemmer complained there was no real authority above Advance Base Camp. Axt said the two British stayed high for three weeks and monopolized the lead, and that without a coordinator to rotate people, there was no room for others. He said if there had been good organization on the Face, he could have succeeded. Isles and Teigland said the international aspect had nothing to do with the failure.

The problems came from personalities and the emotional decision after the death. Mazeaud and the Vauchers maintained that the leaders had broken the original plan of two routes and prevented them from reaching the summit for publicity reasons. Dyhrenfurth said the experiment in international climbing was only partially successful, but cooperation had been good until the argument over the route. He noted that the United Nations had tried international cooperation for a long time and had not done too well, either.

Post-mortem

In their interesting and detailed post-climb article titled Post-Mortem of an International Expedition for Mountain magazine (later reprinted by The Himalayan Club), authors Ken Wilson and Mike Pearson traced exactly how the initial merger and massive team size doomed the expedition from the start. They concluded that the 1971 expedition's collapse was primarily driven by severe natural forces, specifically adverse weather and illness, rather than interpersonal friction. They also attributed systemic blame to organizers for combining distinct climbing factions into an unmanageable, oversized team.

Memorial at Everest in 1971 for Harsh Bahuguna. Photo: Wolfgang Axt/The Himalayan Journal

 

The questions remain: Was Bahuguna’s death mainly the result of mistakes by the two men involved, or something the leaders should have prevented? Did the decision to drop the West Ridge and focus on the Southwest Face save the expedition or destroy it? And was the international idea itself the problem, or simply the mix of strong personalities, bad weather, and the pressure of being on Everest?

Piolets d'Or Insider: How the Jury Works, Style Debates, and Future Plans

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The recent 2026 edition of the Piolets d'Or awards, held last week in a remote corner of the French Alps, felt like an organization trying to adapt to fast-changing times and attract larger audiences while keeping its prestige and spirit intact.

ExplorersWeb was there to meet old acquaintances and to put a face to some of the names we wrote about in the last 12 months. We also wanted to participate in the collective exercise of reflection about the future.

Views of Les Ecrins Massif from Saint-Christophe-en-Oisans. Photo: Angela Benavides

Alpine-style experts debate

Piolet d'Or recipients enjoyed a relaxed atmosphere at the tiny village of Saint-Christophe-en-Oisans and a ceremony at Burg d'Oisans, a 30-minute drive down the valley. Yet, the most interesting event took place the following day, as an elite group of climbers sat around a table in a small hall with large glass windows facing autumn-colored forests and the ragged peaks of the Ecrins Massif, covered in the first snow dust of the season.

Representing the awardees were Nicolas Jean (awarded for climbing Jannu East with Benjamin Vedrines); the complete teams from Ultar Shar, Aikache Chokk, and Jerupaja Grande; a hyper-energetic, widely smiling Peter Habeler, 84, who received a lifetime achievement Piolet d'Or; Simon Welfringer, who gave a talk; organizers Christian Trommsdorf and David Chambre; geographer and chronicler Rodolphe Popier, and two members of the jury: Jordi Corminas of Spain and Paul Ramsden of the UK. At a press conference, these experts debated some of the hot issues in today's mountaineering.

Simon Welfringer of France speaks at the post-Piolets d'Or experts' panel. Photo: Angela Benavides

How the jury works

Trommsdorf noted that the jury had an easy time selecting the awarded climbs this year. He explained how these expert jurists choose the best alpine-style activities of the year.

The members work remotely, choosing from a Big List of outstanding activities. This list is previously compiled by Lindsay Griffin, long-time editor of the American Alpine Journal, and the UK's Alpine Club. Jurors study the climbs and narrow it down to a short list of candidates, which is not made public. The awards go to those activities chosen by the majority of jury members. In this case, decisions were finalized on the first vote, with no need for a tie-break.

Left to right, Rodolphe Popier, Jordi Corominas, Paul Ramsden (speaking), and David Chambre. Photo: Piotr Drozdz

 

That led to the first topic of debate: It was noted that some climbing teams happily announce themselves as "Piolet d'Or nominates" on social media when their climbs appear on the Big List -- although organizers make it clear that not all activities on that list are suitable for a Piolet d'Or. Yet, no one supported the idea of making the short lists public. The general agreement was to keep the small "best of the year" finalists available only to the jury.

Drones

The second topic for debate was the presence of a relatively new piece of technology now ubiquitous on expeditions: drones. These have become essential on rescues, but climbing teams also use them to scout the route ahead. In the near future, drones could also carry supplies, emergency kits, or extra equipment. Commercial outfitters are already using them in this way.

Benjamin Vedrines and Nicolas Jean of France used drones to check the state of their planned route on Jannu. However, they explained that the drone was useless in relaying information about a section that they couldn't see from Base Camp. It had a different angle of view, and the drone lost connection when it tried to approach that area.

Drones piloted by their base camp film crew also hovered overhead as they traversed the long summit ridge. Finally, before the summit push, they used drones to clearly identify the highest point, to make sure they reached the true summit.

The Ultar Sar team also had a drone, they noted. All the awarded climbers agreed that drones are okay as long as the climbers are completely transparent about what they used them for. Jordi Corominas said the drones are a technological innovation that has come to stay, no different than InReach devices, satphones, or even ice axes and crampons.

Jordi Corominas, left, discusses past climbs with Benjamin Vedrines. Photo: Piotr Drozdz

 

Interestingly, one jurist stood against the tide: Paul Ramsden is an advocate for the pure alpine-style criteria of the UK climbing school, and with five Piolets d'Or, he is the overall record holder of these prestigious awards. In his opinion, drones spoil the adventure and uncertainty inherent in pure alpine style. Ramsden himself uses neither drones, trackers, nor even satphones.

Expanded Piolets

Finally, Trommsdor suggested possibly expanding the awards to create a special Piolet d'Or for the best rock climb and perhaps even the best ski descent. The idea expand on the new performance category introduced this year in the big list as a test. The category featured, among others, the two Everest ski descents in 2025. Both were heavily supported, and Jim Morrison's descent of the Hornbein Couloir was oxygen-assisted.

In the end, one of the performance ski descents received the female award: Typhaine Duperier's alpine-style climb+ski descent of Nanga Parbat's Schell route, with Boris Langenstein.

It is unclear whether the new additions will become official in the near future, but some attendees expressed concern about the context in which these take place. Would they award ski expeditions for their boldness or technical excellence without bearing in mind how the skiers reached the start of their descent (supported climbs, fixed routes, even helicopters)?

As for rock climbs: where to set the difference between a rock climb and an alpine climb on high, but dry, faces? The debate was left open. We will find out what, if anything, changes in the next edition of the Piolets d'Or.

Buried houses at the now abandoned village of La Berarde. Photo: Piotr Drozdz

No more ice axe

The physical award is no longer an actual piolet (ice axe). In the last few years, it has been a hand-crafted trophy that changes with the venue. This time, it was a plaque with a reproduction of Ecrins National Park's most distinctive peak, La Meige. It rested on a piece of rock from the remains of La Berarde, a nearby village destroyed in a flood two years ago.

At the Piolets d'Or ceremony in Burg d'Oisans. Photo: Angela Benavides

 

Next year, the Piolets d'Or will be staged in the same location, perhaps in late summer. The event will commemorate the 150th anniversary of the first ascent of La Meige. The long-term plan is to turn the Piolet d'Or ceremony into a bigger, international mountain festival.

Steal My Food Itinerary: 3 Days on Prince Edward Island

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →
Raspberry Point oysters at Landmark Oyster House on Prince Edward Island. | Kate Heddings

Recently I spent a few days exploring Prince Edward Island, a place that embraces its local foodways with gusto. There’s a complex history here, with roots in Indigenous Mi’kmaq, Acadian, Irish, Scottish, and other cultures. And if one thing unites these histories, it’s the island’s abundance of seafood and produce, which have helped earn it the nickname Canada’s Food Island. How can you not want to visit Canada’s Food Island?

On a stretch of land only about 140 miles long, you can eat oysters pulled out of nearby waters, potatoes grown on local farms, dig into a juicy burger made with PEI beef, and lick ice cream made with milk from the cows you pass along those famous red-dirt roads. That’s a lot of good eating packed into a place you can drive across in an afternoon.

I had to structure my visit, therefore, around where to dine, but also include a bucket-list visit to Green Gables Heritage Place. (I am a complete freak for L.M. Montgomery’s novel Anne of Green Gables, set on PEI around the turn of the last century.) I also had to schedule time at the PEI International Shellfish Festival, which promised endless trays of freshly shucked oysters and clams, hot buttered lobster rolls, steaming bowls of mussels, and plenty of entertainment, from shucking competitions and cooking demos to live music and dancing.

My husband and I rented a car and stationed ourselves in Charlottetown, the island’s main hub. Prince Edward Island is situated in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, just a hop away from Nova Scotia. You’re never far from the ocean here, and on a sunny day, the air seems to sparkle. The island is known for its red-sand cliffs and beaches, rolling landscapes, fishing harbors, and lighthouses. If you’ve read Anne of Green Gables, you’ll know just how pretty PEI is.

We landed late Thursday afternoon, ready to hit the ground running. With just shy of three days, I had carefully mapped out the places I wanted to visit, using Charlottetown as my base. 

Day 1

Drinks and snacks: Slaymaker & Nichols

Charlottetown isn’t big, and most things here are walkable within 20 minutes or so. First stop in PEI: Slaymaker & Nichols, a gastropub and inn with the feel of an old-fashioned supper club. It takes its name from the 19th-century circus that once performed on the site. They take cocktail making seriously here. The summery feel of the day prompted me to try the watermelon mule, with hibiscus-infused vodka and chunks of pickled watermelon. I liked it, especially alongside the creamy lobster dip with tomatoes and harissa, and the buttery shrimp pil pil, with lots of tasty little spears of asparagus.

More drinks: Reddins

I was saving myself for my first lobster roll, which we grabbed up the street in an unremarkable spot. It was pretty mediocre, so I won’t go on about it. But after eating, we stopped into Reddins, a speakeasy that channels PEI’s Prohibition history in a dark, intimate third-floor bar with vintage furniture, good jazz, and whimsical cocktails. Our excellent bartender, AB, told us the space had once been a pharmacy where folks came during Prohibition to get their “medicine” — drugstores could legally dispense alcohol by prescription. We tried a few of the signature cocktails, and the one that stood out was the Pink Canary, with vodka, rose, lychee, citrus, and rosé bubbles, served in a small bathtub. Yup, you heard that right. It was sweet and awfully fun to sip. 

Tip: You need a secret password to get in when you buzz from downstairs. You can get it online, or just do as we did and ask the hostess next door for help.

Day 2

Breakfast: Receiver Coffee

In the pouring rain, we made our way to nearby Victoria Row for breakfast at local roaster Receiver Coffee. Fuel for the day included strong coffee, a particularly good cup of Earl Grey cream tea, and the Ham Slam, a big fluffy cheddar biscuit layered with a runny fried egg, Black Forest ham, Swiss, and Dijon mayo.

Lunch and more: Landmark Oyster House, Island Chocolates

We swung by the shellfish festival (more on that later) but the rain wasn’t welcoming, so we got back in the car and drove out to tiny and charming Victoria-by-the-Sea. When I say tiny, I mean it: The town is half a square mile, with a population under 200. Despite the town’s size, Landmark Oyster House, set there in an old general store with pretty green shingles and a steep gabled roof, thrives.


Our friendly waiter (everyone is abundantly friendly on PEI) suggested Raspberry Point oysters to start. They hit the spot: medium-sized, briny, and sweet, served with lemon wedges and freshly grated horseradish, which is now how I think every oyster should be eaten. We also tried the scallop dog, with buttery sweet scallops, bacon jam, red pepper aioli, and pickled shallots nestled in a toasted bun. It came alongside a very good, eggy potato salad. 

After seeing the cheery lighthouse and visiting a couple of little gift shops, we popped into Island Chocolates, a family-run shop and café where bars and truffles are hand-crafted, often using local ingredients like haskap, a berry that tastes somewhere between a blueberry and raspberry. That fruity haskap truffle had a nice thick shell of milk chocolate.  

Sun out? Check. We spent the afternoon at the festival and got a substantial fix of oysters and clams. The lobster rolls here were stuffed with unadorned lobster meat, then melted butter from a gigantic vat was ladled over the top.

Dinner: North Shore Canteen

For dinner, both our bartender and waiter at Slaymaker recommended North Shore Canteen in French River, a local haunt for sure. (A slew of chefs from the shellfish festival were two tables down from us.) The menu, which changes with the current haul, hit on the day’s best ingredients: halibut, striped bass, the aforementioned shellfish, beef, and potatoes. We opted for a very generous portion of local beef tartare, amazingly tasty with capers, roasted garlic, and freshly grated Parmesan, served with Covered Bridge potato chips. We wanted to try more PEI beef and opted for a juicy hanger steak with chimichurri and black garlic, which showcased it well. Dessert was a must: parsnip cake drizzled with caramel sauce for the win.

Day 3

It was time for me to check Green Gables off my bucket list. This is an article about where to eat, so suffice it to say that if you’re a fan, this is 100 percent worth a visit to see the house, wander the Haunted Wood and Lover’s Lane, and taste some nonalcoholic raspberry cordial (IYKYK).

All that walking made me hungry, which was good because this was a much anticipated two-lunch day.

Lunch x 2: Blue Mussel Café and Richard’s Fresh Seafood

First stop: Blue Mussel Café, a bright and very welcoming, beloved PEI restaurant set just back from the water in Rustico Harbor. I think it’s obvious what to order, and for good reason: These are excellent mussels — plump, fresh, and brought to the table piled high in a large stainless steel bowl. You can have them prepared with rosemary and lemon or beer and wine. I’d rate them equally, but I did like the hit of rosemary. Melted butter comes in a small ramekin on the side; I used it to dip each mussel, and my husband poured it all over the top. I didn’t have it, but the other signature, seafood chowder, looked popular. It’s made with haddock, salmon, scallops, mussels, and PEI potatoes, and served with a giant biscuit. Noted for my next visit.

We hightailed it to our next meal because I had yet to have the perfect lobster roll. Richard’s Fresh Seafood in Covehead Wharf delivered. This is one of the island’s longest-running fish shacks, where you can savor fish and chips and beer on the deck overlooking the fishing boats, or camp out on the nearby beach under the lighthouse with a lobster roll. no mistake: This is the quintessential lobster roll. It was an exercise in abundance, packed with sweet lobster meat that managed to be chunky and fine, bound with lemon and chive mayo and tiny bits of onion, all stuffed in a buttery toasted bun. It came with crunchy coleslaw, crispy fries, and a paper cup of ketchup. There are other menu items, but this lobster roll alone is worth the trip to PEI.

Dinner: Rustico Cannery

For our final dinner, we drove back toward North Rustico Harbor to Rustico Cannery, which opened this past spring. The airy, modern space has giant windows and a big deck that takes in super sunset views.

I needed greens, and Rustico delivered. We had crisp romaine hearts with a creamy, spicy charred jalapeño dressing, lardons, Parmesan, and sourdough crunchies. My husband hit the jackpot with his dinner: panko crusted, pan-fried haddock with pickled beet remoulade, jalapeño relish, confit potatoes, and coleslaw.

Ice Cream: Cows Creamery

I’d be remiss if I didn’t include our multiple visits to Cows Creamery, the island’s local ice cream shop, which — conveniently — has locations across PEI. It sources the milk for the ice cream from small, local dairy farms and the results are winning!  A few standouts were Coffee Cowrunch (English toffee and Oreo), Cownadian Maple (a triple hit of maple ice cream, maple swirl, and maple cookie chunks; when in Rome), and Messie Bessie, chocolate ice cream with Oreo, toffee, and Cow Chips — the thick, ruffled local potato chips covered with rich milk or dark chocolate that are for sale by the bag all over the island.

A final note: Many places are seasonal, so check if they’re open if you’re visiting in cooler months.

Twin Travelers

In 1977, NASA launched twin spacecraft Voyager 1 and 2, taking advantage of a once-every-175-year planetary alignment of the four largest planets in our solar system: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Using six gravity assists between them, this Grand Tour gave us up-close views of the gas and ice giants, including several moons. By the 1980s, the two probes went their separate ways at Saturn. As Voyager 2 continued toward Uranus and Neptune, Voyager 1 grabbed one more portrait of the solar system (excluding Mercury and Mars), including a final image of the pale blue dot we call home.

The Golden Record Cover NASA/JPL-Caltech

Playlist

“Hello from the children of planet Earth.”

Every good road trip needs some music to match. In this case, each craft carries the Golden Record, a 12-inch phonograph record with spoken greetings in 55 languages, 27 musical tracks, 116 images, and other sounds from Earth. Each record is protected by an outer cover featuring etchings showing our Sun’s location relative to 14 pulsars, along with instructions on how to play it. The design used various metals to protect the information encoded from space radiation: the record used copper and gold plating, and the cover used aluminum. The cover was electroplated with an ultra-pure sample of the isotope uranium-238. Uranium-238 has a half-life of 4.468 billion years.

Are We There Yet?

Did you know that a light-minute is a measure of distance, not time? In August 2012 and November 2018, Voyager 1 and 2 crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the Sun’s solar wind meets the interstellar medium. Using NASA’s Deep Space Network, Earth communicates with both spacecraft and tracks each on its journey. At the time of this article, the Canberra antenna tracked Voyager 1 at 25.80 billion km, or just over 16 billion miles.

This graphic shows the position of NASA’s Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 probes, outside of the heliosphere, a protective bubble created by the Sun that extends well past the orbit of Pluto. NASA/JPL-Caltech

On November 18, 2026, 49 years after its launch, Voyager 1 will cross the one-light-day mark, 16.1 billion miles from the Sun. At first glance, it might not seem like Alex Honnold’s free solo of El Capitan has much in common with Jim Morrison’s first ski descent of Everest’s north face, which is documented in Jimmy Chin’s new film Everest: The Other Side, which releases today, October 30.

But in a conversation during a live recording of Climbing Gold in May, it became clear that several parallels exist between these historic firsts:

  • Both feats entailed years of dreaming and preparation, though took only about four hours to pull off. While Morrison’s expedition lasted nearly two months, the actual ski descent took four hours, five minutes last fall. In 2017, Honnold reached the top of El Cap in three hours, 56 minutes.
  • Both risked fatal falls to pursue historic records—and firmly believe they took measured risks.
  • Jimmy Chin is the filmmaker who documented both achievements, with Free Solo (2018) and Everest North (working title; release date TBD). Chin has called both film projects among his most stressful ever. In June, Chin told Climbing that he never thought he’d find a project as high-takes as Free Solo—until he started documenting Morrison’s attempt to ski Everest’s north side. “On Free Solo, from the day of inception, there wasn’t a day that I didn’t wake up thinking about the worst,” Chin said. “You have to prepare yourself for all potential outcomes. It was a pretty heavy project on a lot of different levels.”
Jimmy Chin climbs up the north side of Mount Everest. (Photo: National Geographic / Erich Roepke)

Despite their shared sky-high risk tolerance, both Honnold and Morrison also agree with the general public on one thing: They consider each other “crazy.” Morrison called Honnold’s ropeless ascent of El Cap “totally insane.” Meanwhile, Honnold struggled to identify the line one might ski down Everest’s north side in a photo. Honnold is not wrong.

A short ascent history of Everest’s “other side”

A photo from the Everest ski exped on the screen above Alex Honnold, Jim Morrison, and Fitz Cahall during the live recording of Climbing Gold. (Photo: Brad Kaminski)

The Hornbein Couloir on the northern Tibet side of Everest has taken the lives of several climbers, including the snowboarder Marco Siffredi who attempted to make the first descent of the couloir in 2002; his body has never been found.

Two Americans, Thomas Hornbein and Willi Unsoeld, made the first ascent of Everest’s north side in 1963. The north side’s first ascent came just 22 days after Jim Whittaker became the first American to climb Everest via the south side. The 9,000-foot couloir they took to the top was colloquially known as “Hornbein’s Avalanche Chute.” Jimmy Chin also attempted an ascent of Hornbein’s in 2003, but was caught in a near-deadly avalanche.

At the age of 50, after two failed attempts and the staggering loss of Hilaree Nelson, his partner in life and mountains, on Manaslu, Morrison skied down Hornbein’s Couloir on October 15. He pulled it off just four months after undergoing a partial knee replacement. Before Morrison’s ascent last fall—which included a 12-person team of Sherpas, Ecuadorian fixers, and filmmakers—no human had successfully ascended the line since 1991.

Honnold’s conversation with Morrison about his historic ski descent was part of the first-ever live recording of Fitz Cahall’s and Honnold’s Climbing Gold podcast. The interview took the stage at Outside Days on May 31 in Denver, CO. The podcast episode dropped today, alongside the release of the film. You can watch an excerpt above, and read on for Honnold’s and Morrison’s thoughts on grief, the question they dread most, and risking their lives for sport.

On losing the partner in his Everest ski dream

Jim Morrison and Hilaree Nelson on Ironclad Mountain in Colorado in March 2018. (Photo: Jim Morrison) 

Morrison conceived of the goal to ski down Everest’s Hornbein Couloir with Hilaree Nelson. The two ski mountaineers met on Makalu (8,463m) in 2015 and fell in love.

“We were on a tear,” Morrison told Honnold and Cahall, “ticking off these dream runs all over the planet.”

The two had been trying to get to Everest to attempt their ski descent. But COVID delayed their plans, making it impossible to get to the Chinese side of the mountain. To stay in 8,000-meter-peak shape, in 2022, they headed to Manaslu (8,163m) where Morrison recalls they “had a pretty incredible day all by ourselves up on the mountain.” He started skiing down first; Nelson followed, but when Morrison stopped to wait for her, she never appeared. She had set off a small avalanche. “What ensued was a multi-day, horrific rescue of her and trying to find her,” he said. “And it changed my life.”

At first, Morrison wanted to hang it up—never ski or head into the mountains again. But eventually, he began to realize “this is part of me, this is in me.” And he returned to the goal they’d started together. “I wanted to do it for her, and her legacy, and us.”

On the summit, Morrison spread some of Nelson’s ashes. “It was a really emotional moment for me, looking off at mountains I had skied with her, Lhotse and Makalu,” he reflected. “I really felt like she was there.”

On coping with loss

In 2011, before losing Nelson on Manaslu, Morrison’s wife, daughter, and son passed away in a plane crash. While he has experienced unfathomable loss, he continues to try to push past the grief to keep living. On stage, he shared a metaphor he uses to grapple with loss:

I’ve described grief as a big heavy wet blanket that gets put on you. It’s just really hard. You go through a process of losing a loved one or spouse or child and it’s a really heavy weight. I like to think over time you take that blanket and you dry it out and you begin to fold it up. And eventually, you put it in a backpack and you carry it with you.

On being “enough”

After free soloing El Cap, Honnold said his life became “a crazy whirlwind.” A week and a half later, he was on an expedition in Alaska. Then the film came out, thus marking his “six month deployment to Hollywood” of screenings and award ceremonies.

The effect was that his memories of free soloing El Cap blurred with the scenes from the film as he watched himself on the big screen, again and again. What he’d set out to do for himself became a messy, public experience. As Chin releases his film, Morrison may undergo a similar experience of distorted memories and mayhem.

Honnold also recalled the dreaded question punted at him on his Hollywood deployment: What’s next? 

Morrison received the same question after his Everest descent and gave an answer Honnold wished he’d come up with years ago: Isn’t this enough?

“I just achieved this 10-year life dream,” Honnold joked of receiving the “what’s next” question. “I don’t have another life dream lined up on the back burner.”

Morrison called achieving his Everest ski descent goal an “incredible journey” and noted that he appreciated the team that supported him throughout the process.

On risk

Cahall teed up his last question by warning that “some of this is triggering to some people.” Meaning, this talk of visualizing fatal falls, of risking it all for the goal. But, Cahall wondered: Are we mistaking complacency for safety?

Morrison said that he doesn’t really see it as complacency versus risk. It’s not an either/or situation. “This is what I’m drawn to and this is what my life’s goals have been centered around,” he said. “I’m not going out there to make a mistake and not come home.” He added that while his ski descent might seem like a “horrendously risky thing,” it was the culmination of extensive planning and preparation, just like Honnold’s El Cap free solo.

“It’s really hard to look at you climbing El Cap without a rope and rationalize it and understand it and be able to relate to it because it is so totally insane,” Morrison said. But at the same time, he said he held “deep respect” for all of the preparation and focus Honnold put into ticking the goal.

“Without taking some amount of personal risk, you just can’t lead a rich and fulfilled life,” Honnold added. “People watch the film Free Solo or a film like that and they’re like, ‘Well, that’s crazy!’ Yeah it’s unusual, not the sort of risk taking you normally see, but it’s still making smart choices in their life in order to lead the most fulfilled life that they can.”

Practical Magic’s other protagonist is the free-spirited Gillian. Beautiful and impulsive, she’s Sally’s more-fun little sister whose main fault is having terrible taste in men. Kylie

April

No less a luminary than Roger Ebert lauded it as a “a beautifully executed example of filmmaking … put together like a fine watch.” Others described it as a “clockwork amusement,” a “splendid film of thrilling, tense entertainment,” and a “polished and exciting thriller.”

Allow me to dissent, at the risk of controversy. 

If you’re looking for a superior period assassin movie, 1972’s The Mechanic is the discerning consumer’s choice. With an intolerably unjust 50 percent on Rotten Tomatoes, The Mechanic is overdue for a reassessment of its place in the hitman canon. 

Like most successful hitman stories, both Jackal and The Mechanic are competency porn. At the heart of their appeal is the viewer’s appreciation of watching someone excel at their craft. But that appeal is heightened by the nature of the craft—professional murder. 

The viewer wants to root for professionalism, but since the work itself is morally dubious, the assassins must be consummate artists, so incredibly skilled that you cannot help but admire them. This dynamic runs through the entire hitman genre, at the movies, from Le Samouraï to John Wick, and in books such as Barry Eisler’s A Clean Kill in Tokyo and W.J. Weatherby’s Goliath, a 1981 novel about an attempt to assassinate the first American pope. A 2024 prestige TV remake of The Day of the Jackal threads this needle so expertly that you find yourself cheering the assassin in one episode and utterly repelled by him in the next. 

From start to finish, the original Jackal is a deeply flawed movie, with some of its problems emanating from the source material and others all its own, following a high-priced assassin through elaborate preparations to assassinate French President Charles DeGaulle. The protagonist is a strange combination of magically successful and tragically inept—he cleverly throws off investigators by repainting his car after its description is exposed, then wrecks said car in a bit of unnecessarily reckless driving. He effortlessly seduces an unreceptive woman in one scene and carelessly neglects to kill witnesses to her murder in another—a mistake that leads to his downfall. 

The storytelling of The Mechanic is far more effective. The Jackal doesn’t appear until 10 minutes into his namesake movie, and he doesn’t perform a notable act of violence until the 45-minute mark. The Mechanic opens with its titular killer already at work—in a gripping but wordless 15-minute sequence that skillfully showcases his intelligence, methodical approach, and strategic patience, setting the stage for the violence to come. 

While the writing and directing are both superior to its glossier, international counterpart, star Charles Bronson is The Mechanic’s biggest advantage over Jackal. Seen here at the peak of his career, Bronson is intensely physical, visibly tough and strong, but also impressive in his ability to convey intelligence. Again and again, the camera focuses on his shrewd, calculating eyes as he approaches each job like an intricate puzzle. And that analytical instinct is not just limited to the job; we see it play out in social settings as well, with Bronson quietly observing and silently judging some truly bizarre interactions. 

The Jackal’s Edward Fox turns in a performance that is deeply shallow in comparison, projecting what feels like an unearned confidence, physical foppishness combined with an aristocratic British persona, infused a blasé assumption that he is simply up to the task.

In fairness to Fox, the script does him absolutely no favors. The Jackal is enigmatic by design, which some viewers consider an asset. His real name and history are a mystery. His personality barely shows in his extremely limited interactions with other people. We see him succeed at seduction, but we can’t imagine how. There’s no spark with his sexual partner. Maybe he smells good? We’ll never know. The Jackal is only the task, and aside from the task, he is nothing.

The Mechanic gives Bronson a protégé, through whom we learn about his history with the organization that employs him. The movie leaves plenty to the imagination, especially his motivation for the decisions that lead to his undoing, but the writers give you breadcrumbs, and Bronson’s performance is emotionally resonant and suggestive of an explanation. The enigma of The Jackal is meant to be accepted as impenetrable. The mystery of Bronson’s Arthur Bishop invites reflection. The viewer is given enough to encourage speculation, but not enough to draw definite conclusions. You’ll have something to talk about after watching this movie.   

Fictional assassins are often enigmatic by design—we know shockingly little about John Wick, to pick an obvious recent example. But the things we know matter. The undeniable talents of everyone involved aside, it’s hard to imagine John Wick reaching the heights of its commercial and storytelling success without the dog. The competency part of competency porn can only take you so far. You need a little heat to seal the deal. 

That’s also what makes the 2024 remake of The Day of the Jackal shine. When we’re introduced to the lead character’s non-professional life, I had a pretty skeptical reaction. Do I care about the assassin’s domestic problems? Turns out the answer is yes. 

The 1973 Jackal is almost an hour too long compared to the tight 95 minutes of The Mechanic, but the ten episodic hours of the 2024 Jackal are smoothly paced, allowing the assassin’s story and eventually his backstory to unfold gradually, preserving the sense of enigma while still ultimately giving viewers a three-dimensional person to love and hate. In comparison, the 1973 Jackal sometimes feels like an old-school marketing film about assassination, one that a guidance counselor might show high school students, either as a cautionary tale or an unusual “career day” feature. 

The workings of a machine are interesting, but the workings of a person are compelling. The Mechanic is an underrated success at marrying the technical fascinations of the hitman genre with the sweaty, bloody, flawed human at its core. While it’s still also a ’70s movie, with all the flaws and eccentricities that entails, it’s a mile better than its more celebrated counterpart, with enduring lessons for today’s storytellers.

“It is not easy to give closeness and freedom, safety plus danger.”

One of the hardest realizations in life, and one of the most liberating, is that our mothers are neither saints nor saviors — they are just people who, however messy or painful our childhood may have been, and however complicated the adult relationship, have loved us the best way they knew how, with the cards they were dealt and the tools they had.

It is a whole life’s work to accept this elemental fact, and a life’s triumph to accept it not with bitterness but with love.

How to make that liberating shift of perspective is what the playwright, suffragist, and psychologist Florida Scott-Maxwell (September 14, 1883–March 6, 1979) considers in a passage from her 1968 autobiography The Measure of My Days (public library).

Kinship by Maria Popova. (Available as a print.)

She writes:

A mother’s love for her children, even her inability to let them be, is because she is under a painful law that the life that passed through her must be brought to fruition. Even when she swallows it whole she is only acting like any frightened mother cat eating its young to keep it safe.

In a sentiment that calls to mind Kahlil Gibran’s insight into the delicate balance of intimacy and independence essential for romantic love — which is always an echo of our formative attachments — she adds:

It is not easy to give closeness and freedom, safety plus danger.

Art by Alessandro Sanna from Crescendo

With a wary eye to the brunt of parental expectation under which all children live, well into adulthood, she writes:

No matter how old a mother is she watches her middle-aged children for signs of improvement. It could not be otherwise for she is impelled to know that the seeds of value sown in her have been winnowed. She never outgrows the burden of love, and to the end she carries the weight of hope for those she bore. Oddly, very oddly, she is forever surprised and even faintly wronged that her sons and daughters are just people, for many mothers hope and half expect that their newborn child will make the world better, will somehow be a redeemer. Perhaps they are right, and they can believe that the rare quality they glimpsed in the child is active in the burdened adult.

Perhaps that glimpse is what Maurice Sendak meant when he observed that life is largely a matter of “having your child self intact and alive and something to be proud of.”

Complement with Kahlil Gibran’s advice on children, the pioneering psychologist Donald Winnicott on the mother’s contribution to society, and Alison Bechdel’s superb Winnicott-inspired Are You My Mother?, then savor My Mother’s Eyes — a soulful animated short film about loss and the unbreakable bonds of love — and Mary Gaitskill’s poignant advice on how to move through life when your parents are dying.
