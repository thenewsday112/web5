Press Forward gave $20 million to 205 local newsrooms over two years. Here’s how some of them spent it.

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Most local newsrooms in the U.S. have one thing in common: They need more money, and many dream of dollars they can use however they want.

In 2024, Press Forward, the nonprofit trying to mobilize a philanthropic army for local news, announced it would give at least 100 small local newsrooms — those with annual budgets under $1 million — just that type of unrestricted funding. Almost 1,000 newsrooms applied. Ultimately, Press Forward distributed $20 million to 205 outlets. Most of them received $100,000 each, split over two years.

I wondered what the recipients had done with the money, and which problems it had (or hadn’t) solved. Between May and October, I spoke or emailed with more than 30 grantees.

The grantees represented every U.S. state, came from cities, suburbs, and rural areas, and published in multiple languages. They included for-profits and nonprofits, print and digital-only publications, college newsrooms, and public radio stations. Statewide newsrooms with fully staffed teams like The Maine Monitor, scrappy news startups like 285 South, and community newspapers powered by freelancers and part-time staff like the Copper River Record in Alaska all received funding.

For many newsrooms, Press Forward was the first grant they’d ever applied for. “Many of the country’s newsrooms are small, and few get foundation grants,” said Press Forward executive director Dale Anglin. The open call was designed to change that and to distribute the money quickly. Grantees were required to complete two LION sustainability audits a year apart; Press Forward is analyzing that data now and will release its findings next month.1

“People tell me that what Press Forward did was unusual and rare within philanthropy,” said María José Ramírez Braiz, founder and CEO of Midwest-based grantee Te Lo Cuento News.

“They said to us at the beginning of the grant period, before the shit hit the fan with the federal government, ‘If you need this money to keep the lights on, keep the lights on,'” said Copper River Record editor Allison Sayer, one of the paper’s two part-time staffers. “I feel that this was really a gift.”

First-time grant applicants and recipients, in particular, said Press Forward’s recognition gave them credibility and visibility with other funders. And publishers described the funding as a morale boost that validated low-paid work.

“It has been a life-changer, a lifesaver for us,” said Gretchen Sporleder Orr, who publishes the World Journal in southern Colorado with her husband. Her voice broke when she talked to me about the funding. “I don’t know if we would be in business right now if it wasn’t for the Press Forward money.”

The Real WV “would not still exist without Press Forward,” said Stephen Baldwin, the site’s volunteer publisher. (He’s also the former minority leader for the West Virginia Senate and a pastor of two churches.) The outlet was one of eight grantees that received just two $25,000 grants over two years. “There’s not exactly a lot of capital floating around West Virginia for nonprofit news,” Baldwin said, “and without this outside impact, we wouldn’t be able to do this work.” But he hopes that the site can be less reliant on grants in the future.

The funding did not save every recipient. One grantee, Atlanta Civic Circle, shut down earlier this year. Atlanta Civic Circle did not receive its second $50,000 grant because it didn’t complete the first LION audit, Anglin said. (Atlanta Civic Circle did not respond to my request for an interview, but executive director Saba Long wrote on LinkedIn in June that “the funding environment for local nonprofit journalism is extraordinarily difficult right now.”) Other recipients have merged or transformed since applying for the grant.2

Medill’s annual State of Local News report, released this month, describes a local news ecosystem in a “state of precarity.” That state of precariousness helps explain how $100,000 over two years can be a “godsend,” a “miracle,” and not enough. As one grantee put it, “Right now, these are shanties built on a beach, waiting for a hurricane to come through.”

“Press Forward reached newsrooms that don’t have existing relationships with major national funders,” said Nina Ignaczak, the founder, publisher, and editor of Planet Detroit. “The challenge now is whether the commitment holds.”

“A stabilizing force”

The funding helped newsrooms keep the lights on, whether they used it to pay bills, pay down debt, make payroll, or as a buffer when something went wrong.

“The flexibility that Press Forward has given us has allowed us to fill — I wouldn’t say emergencies, but unexpected gaps,” said Richard Young, founder and executive director of CivicLex in Kentucky. It brought on a consulting editor to keep city hall coverage going after losing a reporter, and purchased new software. Without the funding, CivicLex likely would have had to dip into its reserves, he said.

Bob Bonnar, publisher of the News Letter Journal in Wyoming, used the first payment from Press Forward to upgrade computers and cameras and give some staffers raises. “That chewed up the money very quickly,” he said, “and I probably should have held some of it back because the News Letter Journal ended up having a pretty rough year in 2025.” He had to use about half of the second $50,000 payment to repay an operating loan. The rest covered the remaining revenue shortfall and kept the organization in the black through the winter.

Vandana Kumar, publisher of the California-based ethnic media outlet India Currents, described the funding as a “stabilizing force” that allowed the newsroom to keep operating despite a contraction in philanthropic giving and cuts to local government advertising.

The Copper River Record built infrastructure to host digital ads and created a new website. It held listening sessions with Native villages in its coverage area and donated subscriptions to local Tribal offices. It improved the design of its print newspaper. Sayer paid freelancers to complete Press Pass training and pursued other professional development. And she was able to take on more ambitious stories about PFAS and “forever chemicals.”

The Press Forward funding was “the biggest single windfall in nearly 20 years for our small community newspaper,” Sandpoint Reader publisher Ben Olson told me. The Idaho-based paper used the money to travel to the state capital for Press Club awards and design a new media kit, which Olson said has improved ad sales. The Reader also paid a large chunk of its printing bill, paid its rent a year in advance, and gave staffers bonuses. (The bonuses helped the paper become 100% employee-owned; Olson’s two employees bought in when Olson’s business partner and co-owner retired.)

The open-ended nature of the grants gave Colorado’s World Journal “the flexibility to be able to make a change, do a thing, and then pivot as needed,” Sporleder Orr said. “When we were in the toilet, all I could think about was, like, ‘Is there going to be anything in the post office today so that I can make payroll?’ ‘Oh, my God, we’ve got to pay rent.’” Sometimes she and her husband skipped their own paychecks to pay staff. The grant gave them “emotional bandwidth” to plan ahead.

“Sustainability isn’t just about having the funding”

Many grantees used the funding to hire full- or part-time staffers. Sometimes, that worked out well.

The Mānoa Mirror, an online newsroom based at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa, received two $25,000 grants, which it used to hire a managing editor, a student editorial coordinator, three student reporters, and a social media producer. (The managing editor was paid a stipend, and the other positions were part-time.) The work had previously depended on “faculty goodwill and volunteerism, which was not sustainable for long,” said Hye-ryeon Lee, who was professor and chair of the school of communication and information until retiring this summer. With the new hires, the site went from publishing a few stories a month to publishing daily.

The Real WV’s first LION audit pointed to a need its small team had already recognized: hiring someone to sell ads. It hired a public school teacher with sales experience (including selling ads for the yearbook) part-time.

The funding allowed Te Lo Cuento News to pay contributors consistently and to add contract, part-time positions including a sales manager, an office administrator, and a couple of reporters. The only position it could sustain beyond the grant period is the part-time sales manager, Ramírez Braiz said.

The Texas Spur is one of five weekly newspapers owned by Paragraph Ranch Media. Originally, the team planned to use the Press Forward grant to secure a loan to improve company housing and hire a journalist based in Spur (population 863 as of 2020). But nobody took the newspaper up on the free housing offer, said editor and general manager Barbara Brannon. So the company hired four early-career journalists (two full-time, two part-time) living in its 10-county coverage area. Three of the four left for jobs in other industries halfway through their second year. The one who stayed is doing well as a regional sports reporter, Brannon said, and a hire who replaced a person who left is now managing editor for The Post Dispatch.3

The turnover Brannon experienced isn’t unique to Spur. Other newsrooms also found hiring with grant money challenging.

Sometimes, that was because of location. “Had we been able to find the right people from the outset, I believe the grant would have had a greater impact on the business,” said Anton Kaufer, regional publisher for Hagadone Media Montana. In rural Northwest Montana, though, recruiting and retaining staff is a challenge, and Hagadone’s Mineral Independent struggled to replace its salesperson and office manager after they retired. The experience “reinforced for us that sustainability isn’t just about having the funding,” Kaufer said. “It’s also about having the people in place to make the most of it.”

The World Journal tried placing an ad for a salesperson with the Colorado Press Association and got “absolutely no interest,” Sporleder Orr said. “People living in northern Colorado don’t particularly want to come live in a teeny-tiny town in a rural, broke part of the state.” (She credited the grant with giving her the mental space to fire a salesperson who wasn’t working out, then regroup.)

Other times, the money just wasn’t enough. “The reality is that $100,000 over two years isn’t a lot,” one grantee who asked to stay anonymous told me. “You can’t hire somebody with that.”

EcoRI’s experience illustrates those limitations. Before receiving the Press Forward grant, EcoRI had used other grant funding and reserves to hire a director of development in a one-year contract position. In its grant application to Press Forward, the team proposed converting that role into a full-time staff position with benefits.

The salary limited the hiring pool, though. The development director position paid $65,000 a year, which “is really not the market rate for a director of development” in the area, publisher and cofounder Jo Detz said. (She said it should be more like $90,000.) The staffer they ended up hiring came from another state and hadn’t worked in news before. In a staff position, he cost more than he was bringing in. Detz laid him off and eliminated the position last December. She called the experience “heartbreaking.”

“At the point where I knew this wasn’t working,” she said, “there wasn’t enough runway to pivot, rethink, rehire.”

Beyond the salary limitations, Detz thinks the development director didn’t have enough runway to succeed. The kind of development the staffer was hired to oversee — major gifts and individual giving — is a long game; “that position would need at least two or three years to begin to see some benefits,” she said. Now, she’s back where she started, juggling fundraising with her other work.

“I know it’s crazy to say that $100,000 over two years wasn’t enough,” she said, “but it was not enough to make a meaningful difference to our revenue strategy.”

Planet Detroit’s Ignaczak was in a unique position: Around the same time the newsroom received the Press Forward grant, Planet Detroit also received a $500,000 three-year grant from the MacArthur Foundation. That grant allowed her to hire a managing editor, she said.

Ignaczak believes general operating support is what small nonprofit newsrooms need. But to be effective, “I really think fully funding a revenue role for a minimum of three years is what needs to happen to build sustainability, if that is the goal,” she said. “And $100,000 over two years is not enough to achieve that.”

Print and operating in the physical world

NotiVisión Georgia, a Spanish-language local news outlet with one full-time staffer, is committed to print in a world moving away from it. The print newspaper is the only way the outlet has been able to use to “generate real economic value,” said CEO Mónica Pirela, because it’s where local companies want to place ads. The organization spent some of its Press Forward funds on printing and distribution. Pirela wears “every hat” right now, and the organization’s top need is a dedicated sales manager, she said.

California’s BenitoLink, meanwhile, used some of the Press Forward funding to begin printing in 2025. That will allow it to apply for newspaper of record status and get paid for running legal notices.

In Minnesota, Swift County Monitor-News publisher and owner Reed Anfinson called the grant a “godsend” at a time when tariffs and rising gas prices have driven up printing and delivery costs and weakened advertisers’ budgets. The for-profit Monitor-News put some of its Press Forward funding toward redesigning its print newspaper. Anfinson also purchased materials as part of an “Out of Sight/Out of Mind” advertising campaign stressing the value of print. (He created the campaign himself, using Midjourney for the images.) For news organizations in small towns, Anfinson believes, digital revenue will never be enough to sustain operations.

“The space to ask what our readers really need”

Some organizations used the funding to expand their remit beyond news.

For instance, the Copper River Record partnered with the Copper River Native Association to include weekly language graphics in the paper for Ahtna, a critically endangered language, coordinating with a local kindergarten class. That project required a lot of time and investment up front, Sayer said, but it can continue beyond the life of the grant.

Planet Detroit used some Press Forward funding for reporting, but “the most significant thing the grant made possible wasn’t any single story,” Ignaczak said. The grant gave the team time to work on its Civic Action Toolbox, which turns reporting into civic engagement prompts. In a story about a local ballot proposal, for instance, the embedded toolbox flags upcoming public meetings, links to contact information for local representatives, and suggests an organization to follow. The tool is also in beta testing with other newsrooms.

“The whole project came from having the space to ask what our readers actually need from us beyond information, and then building toward that,” Ignaczak said. “Unrestricted funding makes that kind of work possible.”

Anfinson of the Monitor-News took the unusual step of writing a grant application on behalf of the local school district, which was losing students to adjacent districts. He used some of the Press Forward funding to match a $2,500 grant to the district. The district then ran $5,000 in ads in a “goodwill effort” to stem declining enrollment. The partnership helped to raise the Monitor-News’s profile, he said, by highlighting the benefits of newspaper advertising.

“It felt like a lot of pressure”

Press Forward required grantees to complete two LION sustainability audits. But it also offered optional coaching via the media consulting company Blue Engine to help implement the first audit’s recommendations. Of the 205 grantees, 154 participated in coaching, Anglin said.

The coaching kicked off in January, with most individual coaches and grantees meeting monthly. Multiple participants said the support would have been more beneficial if it were introduced earlier. This was a tradeoff of Press Forward moving quickly to get money out the door, Anglin said.4

This was the first large, unrestricted grant Austin Common had ever received, and it struggled with how to spend the money. “It felt like a lot of pressure,” said Amy Stansbury, the site’s editor-in-chief and only full-time staffer.

Stansbury wanted to bring on the organization’s first full-time employees, but didn’t want to make those hires (and spend all the grant money) until she had a clear plan. The coaching helped her figure out which hires made the most strategic sense and how to budget for them.

A few publishers were lukewarm about the substance of the coaching, but the majority I heard from were enthusiastic. At first, Baldwin was skeptical and worried the coaching might amount to “checking a box.” In practice, though, he said it’s been “tremendously helpful” for tasks like writing the job description for the Real WV’s sales position.

Sporleder Orr called the coaching “incredible.” The monthly sessions helped her with everything from tackling grant proposals to improving her Google Analytics skills. The combination of money and coaching, she added, “makes sure that the recipients don’t just continue doing business as usual.”

“This secret shadow world”

In addition to the funding, Press Forward paid for grantees to attend conferences. Anglin said 111 grantees attended the Lenfest News Philanthropy Summit in Philadelphia in 2025, 81 attended the same conference (which I also attended) in 2026, and 87 attended the Business of Local conference in Salt Lake City in 2025. A handful of grantees attended affinity conferences.

Stansbury found attending conferences for the first time helpful and eye-opening. It taught her “there’s this secret shadow world of [invitation-only] foundation and grant funding.” Press Forward’s travel support to attend a national conference was “transformative” for India Currents, Kumar said, giving it visibility and access to relationship-building.

To small newsrooms, the grant felt like a Press Forward stamp of approval that put them on the map for other funders. The Mirror’s Lee called the Press Forward funding “instrumental” in securing a $20,000 grant from a local foundation. But that was an exception among publishers I heard from; most didn’t feel the visibility and credibility conferred by the grant directly translated to other funders giving them more money.

So several publishers, including Ramírez Braiz of Te Lo Cuento News, would like to see Press Forward go a step further and make individual introductions to funders. “Those more personal introductions can make a major difference for small organizations,” she said.

Ramírez Braiz also called for “more shared learning around outcomes and impact.” Few grantees had a good sense of what others in the cohort were up to, and some were interested in more knowledge sharing with peer organizations, though the day-to-day grind of keeping one’s own newsroom running was a common impediment.

“Folks in the local news ecosystem don’t know how good they have it”

Since 2023, Anglin said, Press Forward’s network of more than 130 funders has invested $258 million directly in newsrooms. That suggests that over half of the more than $400 million the coalition says it’s invested has gone to newsrooms.

When Press Forward first announced the direct funding for newsrooms, it “felt really awesome,” the grantee who asked to stay anonymous told me. To the grantee, it was meaningful to see Press Forward give funding to more organizations than planned in response to the scale of need. But when Press Forward went on to a second funding round in which single organizations, including both newsrooms and newsroom-adjacent organizations like the International Women’s Media Foundation and LION, received as much as $1.5 million for infrastructure projects, the same grantee said it felt like “a slap in the face.”

“Many of the problems newsrooms face aren’t unique to them individually,” Anglin said. “If we are attempting to solve problems at scale, we have to intervene not just at the newsroom level, but at the systems level, too.” She said that because many of the infrastructure grantees serve multiple newsrooms, “investing in them creates a multiplier effect across the industry.”

The argument that funding support organizations and infrastructure lifts more boats is “a cop-out,” this publisher said. “I think the philanthropists know it, I think the associations know it, and I think the potential grantees that are not funders or associations or intermediaries know it.”

Young had a different perspective. Because of CivicLex’s broad scope, it seeks out funding from both democracy funders and journalism funders. Young thinks local news funders are way ahead on getting dollars into local communities.

“I think Press Forward has done one of the more effective jobs of anything I’ve seen in the broader landscape of my world, and the civic renewal space, of delivering resources to the local level,” he said. (He added, “No one is ever happy in the local news ecosystem when I say that folks in the local news ecosystem don’t know how good they have it. That does not win me a lot of friends.”)

Philanthropy as “infrastructure”

Most publishers I heard from see grants like this as critical to continued operation, yet they’re wary of overreliance on philanthropy.

Young said he’d be surprised if local news is still a focus for national philanthropy in eight years. “Filling your cup up with money that will be impossible to recover in four years is a danger zone,” he added.

Right now, more than 80% of Planet Detroit’s revenue mix comes from foundations. Ignaczak said the organization hopes to bring that down to 50% by 2028. “But for nonprofit newsrooms covering environmental and public health issues in a mid-size metro, general operating grants aren’t a crutch,” she said. “They’re infrastructure.”

As for Press Forward, “Time will tell, as these grants go away, what impact [they’ve] actually had on the newsrooms that received [them], how many have managed to build sustainability,” she added. “I think a newsroom that needed $50,000 to survive to next year had problems that $50,000 isn’t going to solve.”

LOOKOUT executive director Jake Hylton believes there’s enough funding to go around. “While there may not be enough to support every single newsroom at a very large amount,” he said, “I do believe that there is a larger investment in news that could be done.”

A second grantee who asked to stay anonymous was blunt about their fears for the future. “We’re scared shitless about 2027,” this person said, “because not only is the Press Forward money not returning, we also see none of the national grants that sustained us in 2024 and previous years.”

“We’d love to be thriving, but we’re happy to continue to survive”

The Copper River Record was in the black before receiving this grant, and the lean paper’s fixed overhead remains low. For the Record, Press Forward has been like “a fairy godmother,” Sayer said.

“I’m not going to lie: I will miss having the money to say yes to any reasonable story,” she continued. “But I think that, even going back to our original budget, the strength of what we learned will continue to help us deliver a higher quality paper that is more relevant to the communities we serve.”

The Sandpoint Reader, Olson said, is “surviving” right now. “We’d love to be thriving,” he added, “but we’re happy to continue to survive when many print publications aren’t.” He credited the Press Forward grant with giving the burned-out team “the chance to fall in love with our industry again.”

Sporleder Orr feels nervous about what’s ahead, but with the World Journal in a much more comfortable cash flow position, she and her husband are “in a way better mental state about the business.”

“We feel good about the future right now, that we are going to be able to keep going and make it,” she said. “We’re back to, ‘This is what we do ’til we die.'”

To attract more shoppers during the upcoming holiday season, TikTok is turning to the second-most valuable entertainment company in the world. I’m talking about Disney, which will promote items on TikTok Shop as part of a week-long activation on the ecommerce platform.

To up its presence on TikTok Shop, Disney will take advantage of Super Brand Days, a campaign format that combines advertising, creator content, and TikTok Shop listings to drive sales. Other brands expected to participate in Super Brand Days include Old Navy and Crocs.

The partnership also has tangible benefits for Disney, which is eager to increase its presence on TikTok. Does ADHD Worsen With Age? It Depends.

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Not too long ago, ADHD was largely thought of as a childhood condition that diminished in adulthood. Today, we understand that ADHD is a lifelong condition. What’s less clear: How does ADHD change across the lifespan, if at all?

This is a question that professor and clinical psychologist Margaret Sibley, Ph.D., addressed during her ADDitude webinar, “How ADHD Symptoms & Impairments Change with Age.”

The answer is still coming into focus but one central truth is emerging: There isn’t a single ADHD experience. Whether ADHD worsens or improves over time for someone depends on multiple factors, from when symptoms and impairments first become meaningful to how sensitive the individual’s ADHD is to their environment.

Sibley’s take-home from the research? “It depends on who you are.”

The Symptom vs. Impairment Question

The question of how ADHD changes over time — whether it gets better or worse for people — is tricky, in part, because of the symptom vs. impairment question. That is, someone may show symptoms of ADHD, but the degree to which those symptoms impact their life may shift with age, overall health, and myriad other circumstances.

[Your Free Guide to ADHD in Older Adults]

“Not everyone who has symptoms is impaired,” Sibley said.

From major life transitions (like perimenopause and its accompanying impacts on cognition), environmental demands (e.g., a job that strains executive functioning) to coping skills, what’s going on in someone’s life “may influence whether a person actually experiences problems because of their symptoms,” Sibley said.

ADHD Waxes and Wanes

The question of whether ADHD worsens or improves over time suggests that the condition is linear. For many people, this is not the case.

“It doesn’t get better or get worse. But depending on what’s happening in your life, you may have good years and not-good years,” Sibley said, whose research found that higher environmental demands are associated with “better-functioning ADHD.”

[Read: ADHD’s Vanishing and Reappearing Act]

What Predicts If ADHD Gets Better or Worse?

If you’ve experienced symptoms and impairments since childhood, your symptoms will likely get better, while impairments may or may not worsen.

If your symptoms first began to impact you in adulthood — perhaps triggered by aging, hormonal fluctuations, or other changes — research suggests that your symptoms and impairments may worsen over time.

If you’re part of the subgroup of people whose ADHD fluctuates from year to year, you may have a form of ADHD that is environmentally sensitive.

Other factors that generally predict a worse course of ADHD, according to research, include but are not limited to the following:

  • More severe ADHD at baseline
  • Family adversity
  • Mood problems and emotional dysregulation issues

As far as what can make ADHD better? Sibley suggests the following steps:

  • Be strategic about your environment.
  • Develop coping skills to positively experience and manage your symptoms.
  • Prepare for major life transitions.
  • Learn skills to mitigate the impact of symptoms on impairment (e.g., externalizing information, adhering to routines, better managing time)

AWS growth hits 39%, its fastest in nearly five years, as Amazon misses on earnings

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
AWS now brings in revenue at a $184 billion annual pace, and it’s closing in on Amazon’s third-party seller business as the company’s second-largest source of sales. (GeekWire File Photo / Todd Bishop)

Amazon Web Services revenue grew 39% to $46 billion in the third quarter, its fastest pace in nearly five years, and Amazon CEO Andy Jassy said he expects the cloud unit to top 40% growth in both 2027 and 2028.

That wasn’t enough for investors. Amazon posted earnings of $1.89 per share, well short of the $1.97 analysts expected, as higher interest costs on its growing debt and other losses ate into its profit.

Operating income beat expectations at $28.1 billion, above the high end of Amazon’s own guidance, and the 13.9% operating margin for the quarter was the highest in the company’s history.

Revenue of $201.9 billion, up 12%, came in just under analyst estimates but at the top of Amazon’s forecast range. Shares fell more than 7% in after-hours trading, after closing down 4% during the regular session.

Update: On the earnings call, Jassy said AWS has a backlog of $578 billion in contracted future revenue, more than double a year earlier. He also said Amazon expects to spend “meaningfully more” on capital projects in 2027 than the roughly $220 billion it plans for this year.

Amazon may need to count on its legendary willingness to be “misunderstood for long periods of time,” as it asks investors to look past the costs to where its biggest businesses are headed.

In the company’s earnings release, Jassy said Amazon’s retail business is gaining market share with improving margins. Retail sales, including online stores, physical stores and third-party seller services, totaled nearly $500 billion over the past 12 months, and AWS is now at a $184 billion annual revenue run rate.

“There has never been a trillion dollar revenue business, and we may have two of them in the next 5-10 years,” he said.

On the earnings call, Jassy said those two businesses are AWS and Amazon’s stores business.

Advertising grew 11% to $19.6 billion, or 12% excluding currency changes, down from 22% on that basis a year earlier. Amazon blamed the timing of Prime Day and a few other factors, and said it expects ad growth to return to the high teens in the fourth quarter.

College Students Found Dead After Hiking Colorado Mountain

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

BY MARY ANDINO

The two young men who set off into Colorado’s Sawatch Mountains this month were both passionate about taking the outdoors seriously. One was studying to be a wildland firefighter, while the other hoped to become a mountain guide.

But both of them were found dead Wednesday, under circumstances that remain unclear. Local authorities spent days looking for missing hikers Kaden Matsuda, 19, and Jackson Donlon, 20.

The bodies were finally discovered near West Apostle Mountain on October 28, just outside of Gunnison, Colorado.

What happened?

Matsuda and Donlon, students at Colorado Mountain College in Leadville, had planned on hiking and camping near Ice Mountain, a 13,960-foot peak located about 50 miles from Gunnison.

They left for their trip on Oct. 23. College staff notified local authorities they were missing on Oct. 26, according to the Chaffee County Sheriff’s Office. The men’s families said they were both experienced hikers.

Matsuda was studying to be a wildland firefighter, and Donlon was training to become a mountain guide.

Kaden Matsuda, left, and Jackson Donlon. Supplied: Chaffee County Sheriff’s Office

 

Search-and-rescue teams were deployed and found their vehicle at the trailhead. Weather conditions looked risky, with heavy snow falling in Chaffee County.

“Ice Mountain is a very technical climb, and it is recommended that hikers be very experienced when trying to tackle it. That is when it is dry. Although there was only about eight inches of snow around Kaden’s vehicle and in the lower basin, Ice Mountain received one to two feet of snow,” the sheriff’s office said in a Facebook post.

Multiple local SAR teams worked on the case. Photo: Chaffee County Sheriff’s Office

 

Rescue teams used drones, helicopters, and dogs to search for the two hikers. On October 27, they located their tent, which had collapsed under the weight of the snow, near West Apostle Mountain (13,597 feet). Inside were sleeping bags, stoves, and other equipment, but the two men were nowhere to be found.

The next day, authorities announced they had found their bodies in the mountain’s basin. Officials declined to share potential causes of death, beyond stating that they did not suspect foul play.

The context

The Sawatch Range of the Rocky Mountains is home to many 13,000- and 14,000-foot peaks. Some of them, like West Apostle Mountain, require technical climbing ability and become substantially more difficult and dangerous during wet weather. The route up the mountain requires traversing fields of loose boulder scree and talus that can be hazardous after snow.

From 2010 to 2017, eight people have died in the Sawatch Mountains. Falls are the most common cause of fatalities for those tackling Colorado’s 13ers and 14ers, as the state’s highest mountains are known to locals.

4 Epic New Watches Every Watch Guy Is Salivating Over Right Now

In its 26th edition, Dutch Design Week brought together more than 2,500 designers, from established names to emerging talent, alongside creative collaborations with major brands. This year’s theme, Rebel Authentic Worth (or R.A.W.), highlighted the social, cultural, and economic value of “design that creates space for experimentation, material research, and radical imagination.”

The theme placed particular emphasis on the unpolished and experimental at a time when technology and AI increasingly prioritize optimization and visual perfection. It also reflected a growing sense of uncertainty around technological advancement, exploring how design might challenge these developments, offer alternative perspectives, or help us navigate an increasingly complex world.

Here, we highlight 11 projects that stood out at Dutch Design Week 2026.

1. Design Academy Eindhoven

Design Academy Eindhoven’s annual Graduation Show offered a wide-ranging look at the next generation of design talent, with projects spanning objects, installations, and experimental research. Themes ran the gamut, including how change can bring about both gains and unintended consequences. Tradition can sustain a sense of belonging while carrying colonial or nationalist histories, for example, while emerging professions create new possibilities without necessarily guaranteeing a livelihood.

‘Safe House’ graduation project by Dirk Duif. Photography by Ronald Smits.

“This year’s graduation projects can be read as maps of how graduates have navigated contradictions and established their bearings: what to attend to, what to carry forward, what to stand for and which relationships might sustain their work,” says Nadine Botha, head of the Social Design Master’s program at DAE.

‘Excess Cornice’ graduate project by Yann Thiel. Photography by Femke Reijerman.

The exhibition’s examination of institutional systems was particularly timely. Graduating DAE students recently protested conditions at the school, citing inadequate studio space and limited facilities for making their work.

2. Fairspace × Studio Shruthi Venkat × Sagitta Creatives: Blind Spots

Large-scale installations in public spaces allow visitors to encounter and interact with design as they move through Eindhoven during Dutch Design Week. One of them, called Blind Spots, asks, “Who is safety built for?”

An AI illustration of ‘Creatives Blind Spots’ by Fairspace × Shruthi Venkat × Sagitta. Rendering courtesy of Fairspace.

Created by Fairspace, Shruthi Venkat, and Sagitta Creatives, the installation used familiar symbols of security—including surveillance cameras, screens, and construction netting—to explore the relationship between protection, surveillance, and control. Participants could wander through overlapping layers of mesh that obscured sightlines, while recorded voices offered different perspectives on what it means to feel safe.

Rendering and photography courtesy of Fairspace

Visitors were also invited to write down their experiences and tie them to the nets for others to read. As the contributions accumulated, the installation gradually became a collective record of how differently people experience safety in public spaces.

3. Ori Orisun Merhav: Woven Landscapes

In Woven Landscapes, artist and material researcher Ori Orisun Merhav presented what she called a dialogue between humans, land, and other-than-human life, with an installation of sculptural forms combining woven palm fibers and insect-produced resin. The organic structures explored how materials shaped by living organisms might influence the way objects are designed and made.

Photography by Studio Mimesis

Merhav is the founder of Made by Insects, an ongoing research practice investigating lac, a natural resin secreted by certain insects, and its potential as a material for design.

Photography courtesy of Ori Orisun Merhav

“The main practice of the studio is research,” Merhav says. “The sculptures are the leftovers, segments of the time and journey of our research.” Through weaving, binding, and coating, the resulting vessels connect traditional craft techniques with biological processes, suggesting a more collaborative relationship between human-made objects and the natural world.

4. Yinka Ilori x Brabantia: There Is Magic in Joy

Yinka Ilori is a British-Nigerian multidisciplinary artist and designer whose practice spans architecture, sculpture, furniture, and interior and graphic design. Known for his exuberant use of color and pattern, he brought that same approach to everyday household objects in a collaboration with Dutch housewares brand Brabantia.

Photography courtesy of Brabantia

For the limited-edition There Is Magic in Joy collection, Ilori sought to make daily chores more pleasurable by transforming practical items, including ironing board covers and waste bins, into playful design objects. The palette is sherbet-like orange, lemon yellow, and raspberry, with aqua accents and bold graphic patterns that give otherwise mundane products an unexpected personality.

Yinka Ilori photographed with the ‘There Is Magic in Joy’ collection. Photography courtesy of Brabantia.

“At the heart of my work is a belief that art and design should be accessible to all, with projects that bring communities together and create a positive social impact,” Ilori says.

5. TAM Industries: House of Lights

Photography courtesy of TAM Industries

Prefabricated housing is often associated with standardized designs, but Eindhoven-based TAM Industries is exploring how 3D printing could offer greater architectural freedom. Its House of Lights installation demonstrated how automated construction can accommodate more expressive forms without abandoning the efficiencies of industrial production.

Printed from recycled plastic using machines developed by TAM, the structure incorporated color directly into the material, eliminating the need for paint. Integrated lighting illuminated the printed facade after dark, transforming the house’s appearance.

The project also explored the potential for reusing its materials at the end of the structure’s life. Rather than treating automation as a constraint on creativity, TAM sees it as a way to make more varied architectural designs possible.

Photography courtesy of Rive Roshan

6. Rive Roshan: Beacons of Hope

The idea that we live in uncertain times—and how design can provide spaces for reflection and reassurance—was pervasive at the festival. Beacons of Hope by the Amsterdam-based design duo Rive Roshan (Ruben de la Rive Box and Golnar Roshan) responded to this climate with an immersive installation combining textured glass, reflective surfaces, and kinetic light.

The duo treats light as a primary material, using glass and metal surfaces to shape its movement and create shifting patterns of reflection. The installation was designed to offer visitors a moment of pause, connection, and optimism.

“We believe design has a responsibility to foster connection and offer sanctuary,” the designers say. “By choreographing kinetic light, we tap into something universal and deeply emotional, offering a counter-narrative to the heaviness of the daily news cycle and the weight on our emotional well-being.”

Photography by Maarten Van der Wolf

7. Gijs Schalkx: Onderweg naar een toekomst [On the Way to a Future]

“Ever since I can remember, I have been curious about how things work,” artist Gijs Schalkx says. That curiosity has led him to build an unconventional collection of vehicles, including a motorcycle powered by swamp gas, a car that runs on waste plastic, and an electric flying bicycle.

Presented as Onderweg naar een toekomst (On the Way to a Future), the functioning vehicles question our assumptions about efficiency, progress, and sustainability. “Technology is often presented as infallible, fast and efficient. I feel that most people take it in as passive consumers, without any awareness of the processes and infrastructures behind it,” he says.

Photography courtesy of Gijs Schalkx

Rather than rejecting technology, Schalkx uses these unusual inventions to expose the systems and resources behind it. “I try not to reject it, but to rearrange it,” he says. “The vehicles really work, but they fall just outside the logic of usefulness. They show our dependence on technology and infrastructure in our daily lives while also showing possibilities we have not yet imagined.”

8. The DIRT: The Ashtray, the Cupboard, and the Leather Boots

Why do certain objects become irreplaceable, even when they have little monetary value? The DIRT’s exhibition The Ashtray, the Cupboard, and the Leather Boots emerged from conversations with three women about memories connected to favorite possessions—ordinary items transformed by personal histories into something treasured. The Dutch Institute of Royal Taste, aka The DIRT, was founded by creative duo Florine van Rees and Jeroen Dijkstra, who produce curated exhibitions and scenography.

“We form deep attachments to things and come to treasure them, often for reasons that have little to do with their material, value, or purpose,” the two designers say. At a time when so many products are designed to be replaced, the exhibition considered what makes certain objects worth keeping.

Recordings of the original conversations accompany the installation, inviting visitors to listen to the voices behind the selected objects. Three designers then interpreted those stories through new works exploring the relationship between everyday objects, memory, and identity.

‘Ritual Remains’ by Stina Henriksson. Photography courtesy of the artist.

Stina Henriksson: Ritual Remains

In Ritual Remains, Stina Henriksson explored the rituals associated with fire, drawing connections between lighting a cigarette and lighting a candle. Both acts can evoke gathering, celebration, or contemplation, while leaving physical traces of what has burned. Her work considers how an ordinary object can become a vessel for memory and ritual.

‘Slaapzak kast (Sleeping bag cupboard)’ by Lotte Raida. Photography courtesy of the artist.

Lotte Raida: Slaapzak kast (Sleeping bag cupboard)

Lotte Raida’s Slaapzak Kast (Sleeping Bag Cupboard) draws inspiration from memories of camping holidays, reimagining a familiar piece of storage furniture through the form of a sleeping bag. The piece explores how furniture can carry personal associations beyond its practical function, connecting everyday storage with memories of comfort and time spent outdoors.

‘Make Me Fit’ by Astrid Vagner. Photography courtesy of the artist.

Astrid Vagner: Make Me Fit

I started to patch myself up in feminine attributes,” Vagner explains.

9. The Swedish School of Textiles: Exit26

‘Weaving Life Stories of Resilient Women’ by Phlox van Oppen. Photography courtesy of the Swedish School of Textiles.

‘The Jeweld Hand’ by Viola Schmidt. Photography courtesy of the Swedish School of Textiles.

10. Atelier Arunika: Picche chest of drawers

With its rounded, pill-shaped drawers and playful proportions, Picche brings an unexpected sense of personality to a familiar piece of furniture. 11. Impakt – Centre for Media Culture: Code 2026: Terms of Intimacy

As digital technologies become increasingly integrated into our personal lives, the boundaries between connection, convenience, and surveillance are becoming harder to define. Code 2026: Terms of Intimacy, presented by Impakt – Centre for Media Culture, explored these tensions through seven works examining how technology collects and uses our voices, attention, and bodily data.

‘The Affirmation Machine’ by Simon Pillaud was part of ‘Code 2026: Terms of Intimacy.’ Photography courtesy of Impakt.

The exhibition addressed subjects ranging from biometric consent to AI companionship, questioning who controls the information we share with increasingly personalized technologies. Part of the sixth edition of Code, an international program bringing together artists, researchers, and policymakers, the exhibition translated abstract questions about privacy and autonomy into physical and interactive experiences.

Together, the works asked whether technologies that promise greater intimacy and connection might also be quietly reshaping the people who use them.

The Biggest Mule Deer Bucks of All Time, According to Boone & Crockett Records

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The all-time top typical mule deer in Boone & Crockett records is the Doug Burris Jr. Buck, taken in Colorado's San Juan National Forest in 1972 with a score of 226 4/8 inches. To crack the top five in either category, a typical must measure at least 216 inches and a non-typical must surpass 324 inches.

  • The B&C minimum for entry is 180 inches typical and 215 inches non-typical for mule deer.
  • Cracking the top five requires a typical scoring at least 216 or a non-typical surpassing 324 inches.
  • The number one typical, the Burris Buck, has held that position for over 50 years.
  • Not all record bucks were taken by hunters. The Arizona Cougar-Killed Dead Head was found, not shot.

The Boone & Crockett Club has been keeping meticulous records of the continent’s most impressive big game animals for more than a century. In that time, thousands of mulie bucks have made the books, but the top five spots for either the typical or non-typical category are rarely touched. One of the oldest entries dates to the turn of the century, for example, and the most recent top-five record book buck is still three decades old.

The minimum score for entry into the B&C books is 180 for a typical mule deer and 215 for a non-typical. But if you want to break into the top 5 you’ll need to bag a typical measuring at least 216—or a non-typical that surpasses the 324-inch mark . Good luck with that, but in the meantime you can at least read up on the current record book bucks below.

Typical

1. The Doug Burris Buck Jr. Buck

Doug Burris traveled from San Antonio, Texas to hunt mulies in Colorado’s San Juan National Forest in 1972 and ended up killing what is still the number one typical mule deer in the B&C books. According to lore, he rattled in six good bucks that day before stalking through a gambel oak thicket to shoot the 226 4/8 inch beauty pictured below. Today, the mount can be viewed at a Cabellas store in Delores County, Colorado.

2. The Svenson Buck

Little is known about this buck or the hunter who shot it. But we do know that his name was Lars Svenson, and he harvested the iconic mulie buck with a .32 cailber rifle in 1948, somewhere near the Saskatchewan River. Lars apparently had no interest in getting it scored. Thankfully, his grandson did. The official B&C score is 214 4/6 inches. The rest is a mystery.

3. The Hoback Buck

The Hoback Buck was killed in a Canyon south of Jackson Hole, Wyoming in 1925. B&C says that whoever shot it was a meat hunter, completely unconcerned with the massive antlers atop the buck’s head.

Thankfully, an unnamed ranch hand found and saved the buck’s discarded rack, which hung on the wall of a popular Jackson Hole saloon for years before B&C got ahold of it and issued it a score of 217 inches. That score stood as number one until the aforementioned Burris buck came along in 1972. Today, the Hoback Buck’s horns are hanging for all to see in the Jackson Hole Museum.

4. Arizona Cougar-Killed Dead Head

The most recent mule deer in our top-ten list wasn’t taken by a human hunter, and it’s the only Arizona mulie to ever make B&C’s top-ten typical list. The buck’s head was picked up in 1994 by Steven Stayner, who was searching for deer along the rimrock of Cococino County at the time. According to B&C, it was killed by a lion, but its rack was still in impeccable shape when Stayner found it. With an official score of 216 6/8, it is currently on display at Johnny Morris’ Wonders of Wildlife National Museum and Aquarium in Springfield, Missouri.

5. Tie—The Ray Talbot and Gary L. Alberston Bucks

Idaho’s lone top-ten mulie was shot by the sheriff of Franklin County in 1961. Ray Talbot reportedly killed the buck near Worm Creek. Since then, it’s been showcased at an Idaho Fish and Game office in Boise, the Boone & Crockett headquarters in Cody, Wyoming, and at the Museum of Natural History in Washington, D.C.

Little is known about the Gary Alberston buck, except that it was killed in Uinta County, Wyoming one year before Talbot got his Idaho record. Both racks score exactly 215 5/8 inches.

Non-Typical

1. The Broder Buck

Ed Broder shot his record-setting buck while hunting near Edmonton, Alberta in the fall of 1926. According to Boone & Crockett, he drove a Model T Ford to the terminus of a rural road and then hired a horse and sleigh to take him to a hunting camp on the south shore of Chip Lake.

He hiked out of the camp he was sharing with two friends at 1 o’clock in the afternoon, following deer tracks and toting a .32 Winchester Special. When he saw the buck pictured below, he trained the rifle on its spine and dropped it with a single shot.

Decades later, one of Broder’s sons sold the famous rack to an antler collector, unbeknownst to his siblings. The 355 2/8 inch rack brought six figures, but Don Broder spent six days in jail for selling it without permission from his fellow heirs.

2. 339-Inch Giant Taken by Unknown Hunter

Information about the hunt that took this incredible mulie buck is speculative at best. According to Boone & Crockett, the mount was given as a gift to a lieutenant governor of British Columbia who served from 1892 to 1897, and the buck might have been killed by a Native American hunter with a bow in the Okanagan Valley.

It wasn’t scored by B&C judges until 1955, after spending time on display at the International Hunting Exhibition in Vienna, Austria. With a combined 47 points (24 on the right side and 23 on the left), it’s the second-biggest non-typical mulie of all time.

3. The Hunsaker Buck

Utah’s Hunsaker buck may sport the gnarliest and most unique set of antlers ever grown by a North American cervid. It was taken in 1943 in Box Elder, County by Alton Hunsaker, who was hunting from horseback in Baldhead Canyon near the state’s southwest corner. Like Ed Broder, Hunsaker took his B&C record with a trusty .32 Winchester Special. At 330 and 1/8 inches, it’s still the largest ever recorded in Beehive State history—and the number 3 non-typical mulie buck of all time.

4. The Fauria Buck

Hunter Clifton Fauria shot this non-typical giant with a .270 in Nye County, Nevada in 1955, but he died a year later and it wasn’t measured by Boone & Crockett until 1991. All told, it has 46 scorable points—22 on one side and 24 on the other—which landed it a 325 1/8 inch score. It reportedly weighed in at 300 pounds after Fauria shot it in the Stoneberger Basin—which lies in Austin-Tonopah Ranger District near Austin, Nevada. It’s still the biggest mulie ever taken in Nevada.

5. The Murphy Buck

Arizona’s Kaibab Plateau is a legendary mule deer destination, and the so-called Murphy buck only adds to the lore. In fact, five of the top 10 Arizona non-typicals were taken on the Kaibab. William L. Murphy shot his on November 14, 1943, and the massive rack stayed under the radar for 30 years before his son-in-law finally sent it in to B&C for scoring. The deer had an astonishing 43 and 4/8-inch inside spread and scored 324 and 1/8.
