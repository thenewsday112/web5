AI boom hits a wall on Wall Street as OpenAI cuts fundraising target while Meta gets a credit warning over its cash flow, spending and monetization

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The seemingly endless gusher of capital that’s been showered on Silicon Valley during the AI boom may be coming to an end soon as Wall Street pushes back on massive spending plans.

Following a brutal selloff in tech stocks over the past week, OpenAI slashed the target on its latest fundraising round to $20 billion from $30 billion, sources told the Financial Times. That also cut its expected valuation to $1.25 trillion from $1.4 trillion.

The funding round was also upstaged by Anthropic, according to the report, as OpenAI’s arch-rival prepares to go public next month. Ahead of its IPO, Anthropic filed an S-1 on Monday that showed revenue has soared and its cash flow turned positive in the first half of this year.

OpenAI didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.

The timing couldn’t have been worse. Quarterly earnings reports from hyperscalers this week spooked investors. Alphabet saw its cash flow sink deeper into negative territory in the third quarter, with the Google parent also joining Meta and Amazon in charging ahead with massive spending plans. On Thursday, Alphabet fell about 12%, its worst day on record.

To be sure, there were other positive signs as well, in addition to Anthropic’s bullish S-1. Microsoft reported strong cloud growth and stayed cash flow positive, though it said its capital spending will accelerate, while AI chip maker SK Hynix blew away earnings forecasts.

But the narrative had already turned bearish, especially after AI debt fears sent shares and bonds of “neocloud” companies tumbling earlier this month.

At the same time, OpenAI was seeking more capital just as other AI giants tapped financial markets heavily. This past week alone, Elon Musk said SpaceX closed $40 billion in debt financing, Meta announced plans to raise $95 billion in equity, and Microsoft raised $25 billion in a bond sale.

The prospect of hyperscalers continuing to hemorrhage funds was too much for S&P Global, which cut its outlook on Meta to “negative” from “stable” late Friday, citing negative cash flow and uncertainty around capital expenditure ​plans.

The credit ratings agency maintained Meta at AA- but warned ⁠the social media leader’s leverage could top 1.0x as soon as 2027, which is the downgrade threshold for the current rating. That’s up from about 0.1x in 2025 and primarily reflects its strategy of leasing data center capacity rather than from its debt issuance.

Meanwhile, S&P sees Meta’s capex reaching $140 billion this year, topping $225 billion in 2027, hitting $252 billion in 2028, and then flattening out from there. As a result, it expects free operating cash flow to remain negative: -$22 billion this year, -$71 billion in 2027, and -$39 billion in 2028.

Of course, other hyperscalers are spending heavily and running cash deficits, but S&P also said Meta’s ability to monetize its AI investments is more limited than that of its peers.

“While we believe the company intends to monetize these investments via improved advertising tools that support faster ad revenue growth, as well as from the development of new revenue streams stemming from its introduction of agentic apps such as Muse, it is unclear when these revenue streams will generate material contributions that exceed their rising costs over the next few years,” S&P said.

Meta’s rivals, however, are monetizing their data centers by leasing compute capacity, including to frontier model labs and enterprise customers, it added.

In a statement to Fortune, a Meta spokesperson said the company disagrees with S&P’s assessment of its AI monetization capabilities, pointing to “significant product and business opportunities” ahead.

“Meta is already delivering stronger engagement, better recommendations, and greater returns for businesses as a result of AI investments,” the spokesperson continued. “We believe the technology and infrastructure we’re building now will open new revenue streams, create long-term shareholder value, and bring us closer to delivering personal superintelligence to everyone.”

The bearish environment on Wall Street also comes as rising bond yields have sent borrowing costs sharply higher, the political backlash against data centers is expected to help Democrats retake Congress, and the Iran war could re-escalate after midterm elections.

Still, some bullish voices aren’t convinced the AI boom is about to go bust, and even think some retrenchment is a good thing.

According to market veteran Ed Yardeni, the AI investment boom faces financial, physical, and political constraints. If any of these constraints worsen, stocks could dive further, especially considering that AI is now a major driver of corporate earnings and GDP growth, he said in a note this week. 

“However, we view these constraints as healthy for the durability of the AI boom!” he added.

For example, if the supply of capital doesn’t flow as easily, it could lead to more efficient allocation, lowering the risk of overinvestment. Yardeni expects hyperscalers to retain access to financing—thanks to their strong balance sheets—and keep spending.

In his view, it all comes down to the U.S. economy, which he said has performed remarkably well over the past few decades. Even the midterm elections are unlikely to derail it, regardless of which party controls Congress.

“Further AI jitters therefore could set up buying opportunities rather than signal the end of the boom,” he said.

United Airlines CEO Accuses Boeing And Airbus Of Colluding To Avoid Building New Planes

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scott Kirby says Boeing and Airbus have quietly agreed not to develop new aircraft because neither faces enough competition to force its hand. I understand his frustration with the slow pace of aircraft development, but that is a substantial allegation, and the United Airlines CEO’s argument overlooks both an airplane his own airline ordered and the competition concerns he dismisses when discussing airline mergers.

Scott Kirby Says Boeing And Airbus Are Holding Back New Aircraft Development

I have already discussed Scott Kirby’s appearance on the Cheeky Pint podcast with John Collison and Ben Thompson. The full conversation is worth a listen, even if I disagree with some of his conclusions. As always, Kirby offers a remarkably candid look at how he thinks about the airline business.

One exchange that deserves a closer look concerns Boeing and Airbus, the lack of an all-new Boeing 757 replacement, and whether the aircraft manufacturers have become too comfortable with their position in the market.

Kirby Accuses Boeing And Airbus Of Quietly Agreeing Not To Build New Airframes

In the interview, Kirby argues that the Boeing–Airbus duopoly has weakened the incentive to invest in new aircraft. He says:

“I think the fundamental problem is Boeing and Airbus have a global duopoly, and they have quietly, not officially agreed, but they have quietly agreed: ‘Neither one of us will invest in new airframes.’”

He attributes that reluctance partly to investors and says he has spent more than two decades asking for a 757 replacement. His proposed remedy is another aircraft manufacturer to challenge the incumbents.

First, that is quite a serious accusation. Kirby does not present evidence of an agreement during this exchange. Two companies independently deciding that an expensive development program does not offer an attractive return is different from agreeing to avoid competing. I can accept that limited competition may reduce the urgency to invest. But that does not establish collusion.

What About The Airbus A321XLR?

There is also a rather large omission from this argument: Airbus has developed a functional replacement for the Boeing 757.

The A321neo family fills much of that commercial role, with the A321LR and especially the A321XLR extending it to longer routes. These are not identical aircraft with interchangeable capabilities in every operating condition and I concede that an A321XLR does not solve every requirement an airline might have for replacing both 757s and larger 767s.

But it is difficult to argue that Airbus simply refused to answer the 757 replacement question.

In fact, United announced its order for 50 A321XLRs in December 2019 specifically to begin replacing its Boeing 757-200 fleet. Its own announcement described the aircraft as an “ideal one-for-one replacement” for older aircraft on certain intercontinental routes.

Airbus advertises a range of up to 4,700 nautical miles for the XLR, opening longer routes with single-aisle economics (though it seems the actual range is much shorter…)

An aircraft does not have to be designed from scratch to represent innovation. Improving an existing platform’s efficiency and extending the routes it can economically operate can achieve the same goal. Kirby may want something substantially better–he mentioned the superior efficiency of delta wing design (a triangular wing shape) like the SR-71–I would welcome that too. But wanting a more ambitious airplane does not mean show collusion or that Airbus has not already offered a 757 replacement.

We Were Discussing The Boeing 797 Back In 2019

This debate also brought me back to two stories I wrote seven years ago.

In July 2019, I covered United’s push for clarity on Boeing’s proposed new midmarket airplane, commonly called the 797. I wrote:

“Boeing is still studying the business case for the NMA, but has shifted the bulk of its attention to the troubled 737 MAX program.”

That same story identified the A321XLR as Airbus’ answer to United’s replacement needs.

Two months later, I covered Delta’s interest in the proposed 797, including its substantial need to replace aging 757s and 767s. Boeing’s MAX crisis was already consuming attention and engineering resources. Meanwhile, Airbus was securing XLR orders.

As I put it then:

“But Boeing’s pain has been Airbus’ gain.”

That sounds like competition to me, even if Boeing failed to offer the product its airline customers wanted.

There is ample room to criticize Boeing’s priorities. But a company struggling with its existing aircraft programs may lack the resources or appetite to launch another enormously expensive one. It may also conclude that the potential market does not justify the investment.

Neither explanation requires a quiet agreement with Airbus.

United Airlines CEO Says Boeing And Airbus Need More Competition. What About Airlines?

Finally, one more thought on competition.

Kirby recognizes that when United has fewer suppliers to choose from, those suppliers may feel less pressure to deliver the products United wants. Another competitor could force them to work harder for his business.

I agree with that principle.

But when I apply similar reasoning to his proposed merger with American Airlines, Kirby wants us to focus on the benefits of a larger network and a better airline.

Aircraft manufacturing and airline operations have different economics. Adding a manufacturer is an immense undertaking, and combining airlines can create useful connections. I do not suggest that the two markets are identical.

Nevertheless, the underlying concern is common: customers lose bargaining power when credible alternatives disappear.

If United benefits from having another manufacturer compete for its aircraft orders, passengers can benefit from having American compete with United for their tickets. A larger route map does not eliminate that competitive pressure’s value.

Kirby is persuasive when explaining why he wants more choices as a customer. He is much less persuasive when explaining why we should accept fewer!

That said, I’d love to see Lockheed get back into the commercial airline business…coming soon, an L1011-800!

CONCLUSION

I would welcome a compelling new aircraft that advances what airlines can offer passengers. Kirby is right to push manufacturers to do better. But the A321XLR demonstrates that development has not stopped, and Boeing’s failure to launch the 797 does not establish an agreement to suppress innovation.

More fundamentally, Kirby understands why a customer should worry about too few competitors. I wish he would give that concern equal weight when the customer is buying a United Airlines ticket!

Deep Purple’s Ian Gillan Hospitalized Following Medical Emergency, Two Shows Postponed

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Deep Purple frontman Ian Gillan has been hospitalized following a medical emergency, forcing the legendary British rock band to postpone two concerts in Germany.

In a statement shared on Instagram Saturday (Oct. 31), Deep Purple announced “with great disappointment” that its concert at QI Arena in Leipzig that evening, as well as its upcoming performance at Sporthalle Hamburg, had been postponed.

“This comes as a result of a medical emergency involving Ian Gillan, which has resulted in his current hospitalisation,” wrote the band, which also features guitarist Simon McBride, bassist Roger Glover, drummer Ian Paice and keyboardist Don Airey.

Deep Purple did not provide further details about Gillan’s condition or indicate when he might be released from the hospital.

“We understand that this announcement comes at short notice and share the disappointment of all our wonderful fans that we are unable to perform in Leipzig and Hamburg.”

The band added that it is working to find a “suitable time to reschedule” both concerts. Tickets purchased for the original dates will remain valid for the rescheduled performances.

Deep Purple has been on the road for its SPLAT! world tour since late summer, with additional dates scheduled across Europe, the United Kingdom, South America and Mexico through late December.

Gillan has served as Deep Purple’s lead vocalist during multiple stints, first joining the band in 1969. He is the voice behind some of the British rock group’s best-known hits such as “Smoke on the Water”and “Highway Star.”

Gillan’s hospitalization comes just weeks after former Deep Purple bassist and vocalist Glenn Hughes underwent 10-hour open-heart surgery. Hughes, who announced his retirement from touring earlier this year, shared an update in mid-October, saying he was recovering at home following the procedure.

Personal Consumer Issues • Re: Google passkey makes the use of Yubikey obselete?

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →


While overall I'd agree a separate FIDO2 hardware key is the best at protecting a private key. I think it helps to keep some perspective and realize the browser is the weakest link in the passkey chain. Basic session cookie theft is usually the easiest but there are whole classes of attack which don't need to compromise or copy the private key:
https://cybernews.com/security/passkey- ... ty-defcon/
It seems that the main session stealing attacks involve malware, MiTM, and XSS. As a website user, I would utilize anti-malware SW on the client side, lock down everything possible in my browser, and allow only HTTPS traffic. How then would I be subject to session hijacking?
Last week's discovery of the eMudhra intermediate CA certificate compromise was a facepalming disaster. In theory, it was vulnerable for over a year and a half and nobody noticed. With this screwup, anybody could make a fake certificate for well known domains and pretend to be the real website, and as far as your browser/https is concerned, it is perfectly fine. I saw demonstrations of people creating their own google subdomains to show how easy/bad this was.

So if somebody nefarious discovered this a year ago and decided to exploit it, they had plenty of time to figure out something. DNS would be the biggest obstacle for a wide-spread attack, but you could imagine casting smaller nets and going after smaller groups that might be vulnerable by combining with other open exploits or social engineering.

Also, virus and malware detection is a cat and mouse game. Defenders are always a step behind a new attack.

The bigger point is that nothing is 100%. So don't get overconfident. But this is also not to scare you or anybody away from using a computer. Stick to good practices, and you'll probably be okay. At that point, chances are the weak point will shift to your financial institution, where things like password/account recovery, or calling by phone, or mailing in paperwork, might ultimately where you get shafted. Fraud is built into the financial system which is why transactions can be clawed back and companies buy insurance.
