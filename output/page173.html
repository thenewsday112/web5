U.K. Sanctions Cryptomus for RU Money Laundering

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The United Kingdom has issued economic sanctions against Cryptomus, a chameleonic cryptocurrency platform that experts say serves as a major conduit for payments related to ransomware, narcotics, darknet markets, fraud and cybercrime services that are helping Russia circumvent existing financial restrictions.

Heleket’s support agent on Telegram claims, despite all evidence to the contrary, that it is not affiliated with Cryptomus. Image: Richard Sanders.

On October 8, the U.K. issued sanctions (PDF) against Cryptomus — previously known as Certa Payments and Xeltox Enterprises — for processing payments that benefit the Russian government and its financial institutions. The sanctions freeze any Cryptomus assets at U.K. banks, and bar British companies from offering it any business, virtual or otherwise. Also targeted in these sanctions were the virtual payment platforms VexPay, TokenSpot, and the Tsunami Payments service.

As explained in our December 2024 investigation, “How Cryptocurrency Turns to Cash in Russian Banks,” Cryptomus has been a key payment processor for dozens of Russian cryptocurrency exchanges and websites hawking cybercrime services. The company has changed its name several times since emerging in 2022, most recently to “Heleket” last year after Canada levied a record CAD $176 million fine against the platform for anti-money laundering violations.

The blockchain data-crunching firm Chainalysis said the U.K. sanctions target the broader infrastructure supporting Russia’s wartime economy.

“Cryptomus and Heleket have emerged as major payment hubs for illicit activity,” Chainalysis wrote in an October 8 blog post. “Together, these services received more illicit funds across tracked categories than the mixers in our dataset and the number of illicit counterparties surged to more than 900 in a single month in late 2025.”

The Cryptomus support bot on Telegram, responding on Oct. 8 to an inquiry by saying the platform was suddenly unable to process transactions.

In April 2026, the blockchain analysis firm TRM Labs found Cryptomus had processed more than $7 billion in transactions since its inception in 2022, including transactions associated with child sexual abuse material vendors, terrorist financing networks, and human trafficking operations.

TRM noted that Cryptomus’s transaction volume cratered after the platform instituted new “know your customer” or KYC rules, and that the cryptocurrency platform responded to the Canadian fines by launching Heleket as a parallel service that continued to offer no-KYC payment processing.

Richard Sanders is a blockchain analyst and investigator who spent months mapping out Cryptomus’s payment infrastructure and transacting with its customers. Sanders said the U.K. sanctions on Cryptomus are important because Canada’s fine against the platform was ultimately unenforceable and uncollectible.

“A fine doesn’t stop a cryptocurrency payment processor from processing payments,” Sanders wrote on LinkedIn. “Sanctions kill the operation. The distinction matters.”

As our 2024 investigation noted, Cryptomus shared a Vancouver address with dozens of other foreign currency dealers and money transfer businesses — none of which appeared to be physically located there. Following the CAD $176 million fine for alleged money laundering violations, Cryptomus appealed the penalty, launched Heleket as a parallel service, and kept on processing large volumes of sketchy transactions, Sanders said.

Sanders said the sanctions also are notable because they are the first known instance of a “Five Eyes” country (referring to the Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the U.K. and the U.S.) sanctioning a cryptocurrency operation incorporated in another Five Eyes jurisdiction.

Where do entities like Cryptomus and Heleket derive their authority to offer international financial services? The answer appears to involve fake financial institutions overseen by a phony regulatory body positioned in a country that is not widely recognized as a sovereign nation.

Our 2024 investigation found the sole shareholder of Cryptomus’s parent company Certa Payments was a 25-year-old Ukrainian woman who also headed several other firms, including the WS Management and Advisory Corporation, which bills itself as the regulatory body that exclusively oversees licenses for cryptocurrency, foreign exchange and online gaming businesses incorporated in the disputed region in northwest Africa known as the Western Sahara.

That entity’s website — crasadr.com — says it assists applicants with bank setup and formation, online gaming licenses, and the creation and licensing of foreign exchange brokers. One of Certa Payments’ former sites — certa[.]website — also shared a server with 12 other domains, including rasd-state[.]ws, a website for the Central Reserve Authority of the Western Sahara.

Microsoft Patches More Than 1,400 Security Flaws

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Microsoft today issued updates to fix more than 1,400 security vulnerabilities in its Windows operating systems and other software. Today’s epic patch batch dwarfs September’s record of nearly 1,000 fixes, which was already about ten times the normal monthly patch load from Redmond.

Just one of the weaknesses patched this week is considered a “zero day,” or already exploited in the wild. CVE-2026-96951 is a security feature bypass in the Windows Shell that affects Windows 10 and 11 systems, as well as some Server versions.

Windows 10 users might be wondering if they can still expect security updates after this month. Microsoft had been slated to stop shipping updates for Windows 10 systems in October 2025, but ended up adding another year of free updates after considerable pushback. A few months ago, Microsoft quietly extended that support for another year (until October 2027).

At least 78 of the flaws fixed this month are rated “critical,” meaning they could be exploited with little or no action by the user, and most of those critical bugs allow an attacker to execute code of their choice on the vulnerable system.

While Microsoft said there are no signs anyone is exploiting it yet, a security feature bypass in the .NET Framework (CVE-2026-108398) was publicly disclosed prior to today, potentially giving attackers advance time to work out how to exploit the bug.

Satnam Narang at Tenable said the number of flaws fixed this month far surpasses the previous record-setting year 2020, when Microsoft patched 1,261 CVEs.

“We’ve now crossed the 4,000 CVE mark with two months left to go,” he said.

Microsoft credits large language models for the high volume of bug discovery, and presumably AI gets credit for many of the patches used to fix these flaw. Meanwhile, security experts have been warning that these unusually large patch volleys from Microsoft are placing extra strain on corporate security teams, which often have to test individual updates before deploying them across a network to ensure the fixes don’t cause problems for other software.

Tyler Reguly, associate director of security research and development at Fortra, notes that just 26 Microsoft vulnerabilities appeared in the “Known Exploited Vulnerabilities” (KEV) catalog maintained by the U.S. Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) so far this year, and security teams would do well to prioritize patching those 26 vulnerabilities first.

“Security leaders should focus on exploitable risk, and ask whether their teams have the clarity, capacity and support to act,” Reguly said.

Which vulnerabilities deserve the most scrutiny in your organization? The folks at Cisco Talos have a useful roundup of the more noteworthy flaws that Microsoft fixed today, and of course the SANS Internet Storm Center has a reliable roundup that indexes vulnerabilities by severity, CVE number and affected software.

#758: Q&A: “I’m 26 and No Longer Motivated by Money. Am I Unemployable?”

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“You’re ready for career change now.”

That’s what I told Lucas near the end of this episode. He’s 35, he and his wife have two young kids (including a daughter with special needs), and he’s weighing a career change from IT consulting into financial planning that would cut his income by roughly 60%.

Before we get to Lucas, Joe and I start with Maria. She’s 26, her net worth is about 10 times her annual spending, and she’s no longer motivated by money, which has her worried she’s becoming unemployable. I think it makes her exactly the kind of person I’d want to hire.

In between, Matt from Utah asks us to weigh in on target date funds. I’m a fan; Joe insists he isn’t against them, he just doesn’t think you should use them.

By the end, Joe and I land in the same place on both career questions: the money you’ve saved is what buys you the freedom to do work that’s worth your time, whether that means a career change or holding out for the right employer.

Listener Questions

The questions we answered this episode

+

Anonymous (let’s call them “Maria”) asks: I’m 26 years old, living in the United States, and my net worth is roughly 10 times my annual spending. There is plenty of advice on how to work your way to financial independence, but what I can’t find is how to work after achieving it. While I haven’t reached full financial independence yet, I have enough that I am no longer motivated by money. I don’t find working on someone else’s schedule acceptable, and I want to determine what projects I work on rather than having an employer decide. It feels like I have a negotiation advantage that other employees don’t have, but potential employers can simply avoid hiring me in favor of workers who are motivated by money and forced to work on someone else’s schedule. I am not at a place in my career where entrepreneurship is a good option yet. Because of my investments, I don’t think I can accept a job doing anything other than what I want to do, which is clearly not what my coworkers are doing and not what employers expect of me. I’m starting to worry that I am becoming unemployable.

Matt asks: I have a question about target date funds. I know Joe is not a fan, and I’ve heard him rant about that many times over the years. However, several people I respect—such as YouTuber Rob Berger, Bogleheads leader John Luskin, and financial advisor Peter Lazaroff—invest most or all of their money in a target date or all-in-one fund and speak positively about them. I know Joe disagrees, but these are people I trust. I don’t invest in a target date fund myself, but I’m intrigued by the idea. I would love to hear your point of view on them.

Lucas asks: I’d love your perspective on the timing and financial risk of a career change. My wife and I are both 35. We have a two-and-a-half-year-old daughter who has special needs and a six-month-old son. My daughter will likely need lifelong support, so we’re trying to plan responsibly for future care costs that are still hard to estimate.

We both work in IT consulting at large professional services firms. Our combined compensation is about $375,000 annually, including salary, bonuses, and ESPP. We spend roughly $110,000 per year today, but we expect that number to rise to about $130,000 starting in 2027 as daycare and other kid-related expenses increase.

Over the past four or five years, we’ve paid off our mortgage and saved an average of about $5,000 to $6,000 per month into our taxable brokerage account. We still get our full 401(k) matches, max out our HSA and dependent care FSA, and have a little over $840,000 across our 401(k)s and IRAs. By the time I expect to make a career move, we think we’ll have about 12 months of necessary expenses in cash reserves, and we also plan to purchase a new vehicle before then.

The complication is that I’m considering a transition into financial planning around late summer 2027. I’m comfortable starting near the bottom of the ladder and expect my income could fall by roughly 60% initially. That would bring our household income down to around $250,000, just as we have two kids starting in daycare and our expenses are at their highest. I’m not really happy in my current role, and I feel confident that financial planning is the right long-term move for me, but I’m struggling with whether this is the responsible timing. Should I make the switch in 2027, accepting a temporary step back in income, or stay in IT consulting another year or two to build a bigger cushion?

As far as the timing of the transition, I’ve already taken major steps over the past few months to position myself for the switch, including completing a financial planning externship, networking with local CFPs to gauge the market in my area, and studying for my Series 65. I’m also taking my last block of paternity leave in January and owe my company 30 business days after returning. By that time, it will be performance discussion season, bonuses are paid out in late May, and 401(k) matches are in late June, so it’s worth staying into the summer.

What blind spots do you see, and am I accounting for the right things, especially given our children, higher near-term expenses, and the uncertainty around our daughter’s long-term care needs?

Key Takeaways

The biggest ideas from the conversation

+
  • Not chasing money can make you more employable: When I hire, I look for two things: a drive for mastery and a belief in the mission. Someone who isn’t there just to collect a paycheck is exactly who I want on my team. Money motivation would be the wrong fit.
  • Small businesses reward results over seat time: Joe pointed out that big corporations often care whether you’re at your desk from 8 to 5, while small businesses care whether the work gets done. My caveat: in a tiny company, one or two personalities shape your entire experience.
  • Look for founders who are humble but confident: The best small-business leaders know what they’re bad at and hire people better than them, without letting ego get in the way. A quick test before any job or career change: would you want to spend an eight-hour airport layover with these people?
  • A good target date fund is harm reduction: Joe argues most people can learn to build a simple three-fund portfolio, and that many target date funds are packed with high-fee leftovers. I see a low-cost one from Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, or T. Rowe Price as a smart default for anyone tempted by crypto or individual stocks.
  • Your savings exist for moments like this: Lucas has spent years paying off his mortgage and building a taxable brokerage account. To me, funding a career change into work he actually wants is exactly what all that saving is for. Waiting “one more year” just delays the clock.
  • Make your career change while your kids are young: Joe started his financial planning career when his kids were small and says getting that pain out of the way early was worth it. Time, not money, is the bigger currency in any career change, and leaner years can still be rich ones.

Designing silicon in the loop with real workloads

As we enter the next phase of Super Intelligence, inference is becoming the dominant workload and that’s where efficiency, latency, and cost really matter. Azure Maia 300, our next generation AI accelerator from our Maia family, advances the inference efficiency frontier. Maia 200 is deploying across the United States, Europe and Australia and delivering up to 1.7x more tokens per watt for built-in AI capabilities in Microsoft Teams, Copilot and Cowork experiences compared with leading GPU infrastructure.

Azure Maia 200 systems in a datacenter

AI is not only about accelerators. Agentic workloads create entirely new demands on CPUs. Azure Cobalt 200 systems in a datacenter

Building an open, resilient AI fabric

Compute creates intelligence and networks make it usable at scale.

Working with industry partners, Microsoft developed Multipath Reliable Connection (MRC), rethinking Ethernet for AI. Its multi-plane architecture scales beyond 100,000 GPUs with two network tiers, reducing power, complexity, failure points, and cost. At this year’s OCP Global Summit, Microsoft announced Project Tessera, the evolution of the Azure Integrated Hardware Security Module (HSM), into a fully open-source initiative developed in collaboration with Marvell®, Oracle, and Open Compute Project (OCP). Project Tessera advances our broader commitment to open, industrywide standards for platform security.

The motivation behind Project Tessera is straightforward: the entire industry benefits when we collaborate and advance hardware security together.

Project Tessera carries forward the workload directly attached to the HSM architecture introduced by Azure Integrated HSM, making the architecture, specifications, protocols, drivers, software, firmware, and reproducible build environments open source for the broader community. Over time, the initiative will advance toward a fully open-source silicon implementation, while making hardware broadly available, enabling organizations across the ecosystem to adopt, integrate, and contribute to the platform.

Explore the Tessera Integrated HSM GitHub

This approach builds on the model Microsoft established with other industry collaborators on Caliptra, a fully open-source silicon root-of-trust that has since been broadly adopted across the hardware ecosystem. Through Project Tessera, industry leaders including Microsoft, Marvell®, Oracle, and the Open Compute Project will apply that same model of transparency and collaboration to advance hardware key protection with the industry.

The cybersecurity landscape is rapidly changing, and protecting keys and credentials has never been more important. We are now facing two generational transformations that are fundamentally reshaping how we view the cybersecurity landscape:

  1. AI is increasing the speed and scale of cyberattacks

    AI-orchestrated attacks are increasing the scale, speed, and sophistication of attacks, giving adversaries new ways to operate and compressing the time between vulnerability discovery and exploitation. These capabilities amplify risks such as credential theft, key exfiltration, and signing abuse.

    Keys and credentials are high-value control assets. When compromised, they can enable attackers to impersonate trusted entities, bypass security controls, authorize malicious operations, expose sensitive data, and undermine software security defenses. Protecting these assets requires infrastructure with layered, hardened protections capable of withstanding sustained attacks across multiple concurrent attack vectors.
  1. Quantum computing is changing the cryptographic risk horizon

    Quantum computing promises transformative advances, but it also presents a risk to many of today’s cryptographic algorithms. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could break widely used public-key cryptography, undermining digital signatures, authentication, and identity verification.

    Post-quantum cryptography was once viewed as an important but distant technology shift. Advances in quantum computing have changed that perspective as organizations recognize the urgency, scale, and complexity of the transition. HSM-backed signing infrastructure will be among the first critical systems to migrate, requiring HSMs that support post-quantum cryptography and are interoperable with classical HSMs to support migration.

These emerging threats motivated the development of Azure Integrated HSM, which was designed to meet rigorous security standards for hardware-based key protection, while also accelerating Microsoft’s transition to post-quantum cryptography through scale deployments of quantum resilient integrated HSMs.

Azure Integrated HSM introduced a workload-attached HSM that performs cryptographic operations closer to the workload while keys remain continuously protected within the HSM security boundary. This architecture strengthens key protection, improves cryptographic performance, offers crypto-agility, and provides a simpler operational model.

Tessera is an evolution of Azure’s first integrated HSM model, incorporating insights gained from deploying Azure Integrated HSM at scale, and advances the technology through collaboration in the Open Compute Project, with the goal of broadly addressing the challenges of key and credential management in the era of AI and quantum computing.

Eliminating key management anti-patterns

In central HSM deployment models, HSM infrastructure is separate from compute resources, requiring cryptographic operations to traverse the network. The resulting latency and operational dependencies can lead enterprises to adopt workarounds that improve performance or availability but unintentionally weaken key protections. Some of these common key-management anti-patterns include:

  • HSM keys may act as certificate authorities and endorse externally generated keys. These keys can then serve as trusted delegates without receiving the same hardware protection.
  • HSM generated keys may be created as extractable and exported from the HSM in wrapped form. When used outside of the HSM the keys may not receive assurance levels equivalent to a FIPS 140-3 Level 3 HSM and become more susceptible to exfiltration or misuse.

Other anti-patterns include caching keys outside of HSMs for resilience, using software-held keys to reduce network latency to remote HSMs, sharing keys and security domains to simplify governance, and relying on proprietary protocols for interoperability. These practices intend to prioritize operational flexibility but can make key protection, policy enforcement, and auditable provenance less consistent across environments, undermining the security value HSMs are designed to provide.

The goal of Project Tessera is to advance the integrated HSM model to address these challenges in the industry, without compromising on security. The project will deliver Tessera Integrated HSM, a fully open-source integrated HSM designed to consistently enforce FIPS 140-3 Level 3 key protection throughout the key’s lifecycle.

By combining strong hardware-rooted protection with an open-source implementation and open protocols, Tessera Integrated HSM creates a path toward greater transparency, interoperability, and innovation across the HSM ecosystem. The native support for quantum-resilient cryptography will help ease the industry’s transition to post-quantum cryptography.

Figure 1: Tessera Integrated HSM

Demonstrating an open, interoperable HSM ecosystem

Today at the Open Compute Project Global Summit, Microsoft and Marvell® demonstrated the Tessera Integrated HSM transfer protocols establishing HSM partition cloning and resiliency rings across a Tessera Integrated HSM and a Marvell® LiquidSecurity® 2 HSM. The demonstration showed how two heterogeneous HSMs can operate in a resilient and unified security domain. Non-extractable keys within the security domain always remain protected within the HSMs and are available only to the authorized HSM partitions participating in the security domain and remain under the sole control of the partition owner.

The Tessera Integrated HSM protocols use post-quantum cryptography to protect communication between HSMs. This enables authorized workloads with a Tessera Integrated HSM to use both classical cryptography and post-quantum cryptographic services while their keys remain secured within the HSM boundary and can only be authorized for use by the key owner.

Figure 2: Partition cloning example

The Project Tessera specifications, protocols, software libraries, drivers, firmware, and hardware are available through the Tessera Integrated HSM GitHub. Going forward, this repository will add more open-source silicon, new hardware designs, and enhancements developed with the community.

Expanding Project Tessera through confidential computing

The early collaborators working on Project Tessera within the OCP community are exploring how the Tessera Integrated HSM key protection architecture can extend beyond systems with dedicated integrated HSM hardware. As part of this work, the project is making a minimal, unikernel-based Tessera firmware stack open source, designed to run within hardware-isolated confidential computing environments.

This implementation uses the same firmware stack as the hardware integrated HSM, extending a common security architecture across HSMs and confidential computing environments. In this model, cryptographic keys are protected within a confidential computing container, which provides cryptographic services to securely bound workloads, including worker containers and virtual machines. The initial implementation leverages AMD SEV-SNP and integrates with open-source virtualization technologies such as OpenVMM, with the Open Compute community working to extend support to other confidential computing architectures.

The complementary deployment models in Project Tessera extend strong key protection across systems with different hardware capabilities and assurance requirements. For platforms without dedicated integrated HSM hardware, confidential computing provides broadly deployable, hardware-isolated key protection with a minimal trusted computing base.

Project Tessera provides the highest level of hardware assurance, protecting keys within a dedicated, tamper-resistant security boundary designed to meet demanding certification requirements, including FIPS 140-3 Level 3 and Common Criteria.

Figure 3: Protection profiles for assurance levels

The deployment models share the same Project Tessera firmware and programming model, giving organizations a consistent foundation for cryptographic security across their infrastructure while providing a natural path to integrated HSM and higher levels of assurance in newer systems.

Together, these models make strong key protection accessible across a broad range of systems with different security assurance needs, all built on a common, open architecture. As the Open Compute Project community continues to advance Tessera Integrated HSM, we see an exciting opportunity for broader industry collaboration to strengthen key protection, expand secure key management, and accelerate innovation across the ecosystem.

AWS Weekly Roundup: Prime Day 2026 by the numbers, Claude Haiku 5.5, Amazon Nova 2.5 Sonic, and more (October 13, 2026)

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some numbers are hard to picture until you sit with them for a moment. Last week we published the Prime Day 2026 metrics, and the one that stuck with me is 2.3 quadrillion: the daily requests Amazon ElastiCache peaked at during the event. Prime Day ran back in June, so this post always lands as a retrospective, and that gap is part of what makes it worth reading. By the time the numbers reach you, the only evidence that any of it happened is that nothing went wrong.

The rest of the week pulled in a different direction: smaller, cheaper, and more constrained. Claude Haiku 5.5 arrived on Amazon Bedrock at roughly 75 percent less than Haiku 4.5, Amazon Nova 2.5 Sonic made voice agents quicker on their feet, and Strands Box showed up to put real boundaries around what an agent is allowed to do. Now, let’s get into this week’s AWS news…

Headlines
All the numbers: Amazon Prime Day 2026 powered by AWS is the latest entry in a tradition that goes back to 2016, and the scale is still difficult to hold in your head. Over the four days of Prime Day, Amazon DynamoDB processed more than 59 trillion requests while peaking at 192 million requests per second, and kept single-digit millisecond responses throughout. AWS Lambda handled over 2.3 trillion invocations per day. Amazon EBS peaked above 24.8 trillion I/O operations, moving more than an exabyte of data daily. Amazon ECS launched an average of 158.3 million tasks per day on AWS Fargate, a 47.7 percent jump over last year, and AWS Graviton powered up to 49 percent of the Amazon EC2 compute that Amazon.com used.

The number I keep coming back to, though, isn’t a throughput record. The team ran more than 44,000 AWS Fault Injection Service experiments ahead of the event, more than six times what they ran in 2025. Those are deliberate failures injected on purpose, months in advance, so that the trillions of requests above turn into an ordinary shopping afternoon. If you’re preparing for your own peak moment, AWS Countdown Premium is built for exactly that kind of planning.

Last week’s launches
Here are some launches and updates from this past week that caught my attention:

  • Claude Haiku 5.5 is now available on AWS – You can now use the fastest model in the Claude 5.5 family on Amazon Bedrock and Claude Platform on AWS, at around 75 percent less than Claude Haiku 4.5 for most tasks. It’s the first Haiku with effort controls, so you can tune cost against intelligence per task instead of fixing one setting for a whole workload. It pairs naturally with Claude Opus 5.5: Opus plans and makes the judgment calls, and because Haiku is fast and cheap, you can run many Haiku subagents in parallel for routing, classification, and small code changes across many files.
  • Amazon Nova 2.5 Sonic for voice agents – You can now build real-time voice agents on a speech-to-speech Amazon Nova model with better reasoning, instruction following, and tool-calling accuracy, plus lower latency. That combination matters for multi-step work: a support agent can look up an order, check return eligibility, and start the return inside one conversation. It keeps expressive voices in seven languages, controllable turn-taking, and a 256K context window, and pairs with Strands Bidi Agents, now generally available, to get a production voice agent going in a few lines of code.
  • Amazon GuardDuty RDS Protection now detects data exfiltration and destruction – Amazon GuardDuty now looks past login anomalies to the queries themselves on Amazon Aurora PostgreSQL and Amazon RDS for PostgreSQL. RDS Protection for data activity builds behavioral baselines and uses ML-based anomaly detection plus threat intelligence to flag unusual data access, with no agents, extra infrastructure, or database configuration changes. This closes a real gap: once an attacker has valid credentials from phishing or a misconfiguration, their queries can look indistinguishable from your application’s.
  • AWS Lambda supports OAuth for self-managed Apache Kafka event sources – You can now authenticate Lambda Kafka consumers through your enterprise identity provider, such as Amazon Cognito or Okta. Event source mappings previously supported only SASL/PLAIN, SASL/SCRAM, and mTLS, which left teams in regulated industries unable to use Kafka ESM at all. Now you get the automatic scaling, batching, error handling, and event filtering of Kafka ESM with the same identity governance you apply everywhere else. It works with self-hosted clusters and managed offerings including Confluent Cloud, Aiven, and Redpanda.
  • Amazon Redshift adds Apache Iceberg materialized views – You can now pre-compute expensive joins and aggregations once in Amazon Redshift and store the result as an Iceberg table in Amazon S3, registered in the AWS Glue Data Catalog. You create them with familiar SQL, and the results are immediately queryable by any Iceberg-compatible engine, including Amazon Athena, Apache Spark, Trino, and Snowflake. Redshift keeps them current by recomputing only what changed. There’s a good deep dive on the AWS Big Data blog if you want the details.

Announcing AWS Lambda Web Functions

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Today, we’re announcing AWS Lambda Web Functions, a new form factor that lets you run standard HTTP web applications on Lambda without rewriting your code into an event handler. You can take a standard port-listening server built with Express, Hono, Fastify, Next.js, or any Node.js framework, deploy it with a single command, and Lambda automatically handles scaling, multi-region distribution, and active CPU billing. No adapters, no infrastructure to manage, and no framework lock-in.

With this launch, AWS Lambda now offers three distinct form factors, each built on the same Firecracker isolation technology and designed for a different programming model.

Lambda Event Functions are what you already know as Lambda functions. They use the event-driven, single-concurrent handler model with wall-clock billing. We’re introducing the “Event Functions” name to highlight that programming model: your function receives an event, processes it, and returns a response. The event-driven programming model is ideal for event processing, queue consumers, and invocations triggered by other AWS services.

Lambda Web Functions use an HTTP-native programming model with multi-concurrent execution and active CPU billing. Your code listens on a port and handles standard HTTP requests. That programming model is ideal for web applications, API backends, and interactive HTTP workloads where multiple concurrent requests and I/O-heavy processing are the norm.

Lambda MicroVMs provide isolated, stateful sandbox environments with customer-managed lifecycles for running untrusted or user-generated code.

The three complement each other. An application might use Event Functions for asynchronous backend processing, Web Functions for the user-facing API and server-side rendering layer, and MicroVMs for interactive code execution features, all within the same AWS account and sharing the same operational model.

Why Web Functions
Modern web applications need to serve global audiences with fast, consistent response times while giving developers the freedom to use standard frameworks and iterate quickly. Building this today typically requires assembling multiple infrastructure components, configuring multi-region deployments, setting up latency-based routing, and tuning billing models to avoid paying for I/O wait. Developers often find themselves adapting their application code to fit a specific compute model rather than deploying the HTTP server they already have.

Lambda Web Functions removes this complexity. Each execution environment handles multiple requests simultaneously (up to 128 concurrent requests per environment), which means a burst of incoming traffic can share warm environments instead of mapping each request to a new one. Web Functions meter the CPU time your code uses, so time spent waiting on a database query or an API call consumes little CPU. Memory is metered for as long as the execution environment exists. For I/O-heavy web workloads like server-side rendering, API backends, and AI inference endpoints streaming token responses, this can reduce compute costs.

Let’s try it out
To get started, I wrote a simple Express.js server. This is the same code I run locally with node index.js, no Lambda-specific adapters or wrappers required.

import express from 'express'; const app = express(); const [host, port] = process.env.AWS_LAMBDA_HTTP_ENDPOINT.split(':'); app.get('/', (req, res) => { res.json({ message: 'Hello from Lambda Web Functions!' }); }); app.listen(port, host, () => { console.log(`Server running on port ${port}`); });

The only difference from running locally is the AWS_LAMBDA_HTTP_ENDPOINT environment variable, which Lambda sets to the host and port your server must listen on. To run locally, set it yourself, for example AWS_LAMBDA_HTTP_ENDPOINT=127.0.0.1:3000 node index.js.

With my code ready, I deployed using the AWS Command Line Interface (AWS CLI):

aws lambda-web deploy --name my-web-app --code ./my-app

One command. No prior resource setup. The CLI bundled my source, created the Web Function, published its first revision, attached a Web Function endpoint, and returned a live HTTPS URL in under a minute.

A revision is an immutable snapshot of the code I deployed. The endpoint is the HTTPS URL that routes traffic to a revision, or splits traffic across revisions. Those are the resources I use to iterate and to control how traffic moves to new code.

I tested it immediately:

$ curl https://<domain-id>.<shard-id>.lambda-web.us-east-1.on.aws/ {"message":"Hello from Lambda Web Functions!"}

Next, I wanted my application to serve users in Europe and Asia with lower latency. I redeployed with multiple regions:

aws lambda-web deploy --name my-web-app --code ./my-app \ --regions us-east-1,eu-west-1,ap-northeast-1

Lambda Web Functions replicated my code to all three regions and configured latency-based routing automatically. Each request now routes to the Region with the lowest latency for the caller, on a best-effort basis, through one global URL (<domain-id>.lambda-web.global.on.aws).

When I updated my code and ran the same deploy command again, the CLI detected the existing function, uploaded the new revision, and pointed the endpoint at it. Total turnaround from code change to live: seconds.

For production workflows that require a more gradual rollout, I can shift traffic between revisions with a single API call:

aws lambda-web update-web-function-endpoint \ --function-name my-web-app \ --endpoint-name dev \ --auto-deployment-mode Disabled \ --revision-weights '[{"revisionId": "<current-revision-id>", "weight": 90}, {"revisionId": "<new-revision-id>", "weight": 10}]'

This sends 90% of endpoint traffic to the current revision and 10% to the new one, giving me a canary deployment without additional tooling.

Better together with Amazon CloudFront
For applications that combine static and dynamic content, Web Functions works well alongside Amazon CloudFront. You can select a Web Function endpoint as the origin for your CloudFront distribution. Static assets are served from the cache while dynamic requests route to your Web Function, giving you CDN performance for static content with serverless compute for the dynamic parts.

Web Functions improves the private-origin experience with CloudFront. With this launch, you attach CloudFront origin access control (OAC) to a Web Function endpoint instead. CloudFront signs requests to the origin, and the endpoint allows only your distribution to invoke it. You get the same private-origin pattern CloudFront already provides for Amazon Simple Storage Service (Amazon S3), without exposing the endpoint or adding custom authorization to your app.

Now available
AWS Lambda Web Functions is available today in US East (N. Virginia), US East (Ohio), US West (Oregon), Asia Pacific (Tokyo), and Europe (Ireland). Web Functions run Node.js 24 (nodejs24.x) on Arm64 at launch and support Amazon Virtual Private Cloud (VPC) connectivity, allowing your function to access resources that exist within a VPC.

To get started, deploy your first Web Function in under a minute:

aws lambda-web deploy --name hello-world --hello-world
