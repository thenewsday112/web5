The manager is not yet in Alex Ferguson and José Mourinho’s league, but his conspiracy theories have reached new levels

English football loves the word “dossier”. It doesn’t love dossiers themselves, of course; they’re for nerds – unless, that is, they’re a “secret dossier”, which have a thrilling redolence of spy dramas, invisible ink and dead drops. Non-secret dossiers, though, are inherently risible: call something a “dossier” in English football and you’re immediately signalling: this is absurd poindextering. Dossiers were preposterous when Don Revie was – imagine the pretension! – drawing up scouting reports on opposing players, and they are preposterous today as Mikel Arteta collates images of rival managers stepping outside their technical area.

Arteta, it should be said, did not use the word “dossier”; rather it was swiftly applied to what he had called “a massive storage of images”. During Arsenal’s 2-0 win against Everton last Saturday, he received his second booking of the season for leaving his technical area. This, it turns out, is another part of the vast conspiracy against Arsenal. Yes, Arteta accepted, he left his technical area (44 times before being booked, according to the former referee Graham Scott), but other managers do it too. “There are managers who have the record in the league, 36 times in 10 minutes [they] leave the touchline – no yellows,” he said. “I am clipping all of that … so if somebody comes to me I can say: ‘I am not in the record yet.’ Somebody else has the record so let’s see. Then it’s 36 yellow cards in 10 minutes for the managers.”

“Members Only: Palm Beach” star Hilary Musser is working on a new TV project that could incorporate the area’s luxury real estate—and says she would also serve as a producer.

While the future of the Netflix hit remains uncertain, Musser says she is already pursuing another opportunity, with casting among the steps she is working on.

“I’m working on a different show right now,” she says. It's a project “where I would also be a producer.”

Musser is keeping most of the details private for now. She says the network has not been determined, but the Palm Beach County setting could provide an opening to feature the industry in which she has built her business.

“In Palm Beach County, we’re famous for our real estate,” she says.

“Members Only: Palm Beach” star Hilary Musser is working on a new TV project that could incorporate the area’s luxury real estate—and says she would also serve as a producer. (Hilary Musser)

However, she does not envision creating a series focused entirely on property. Instead, she says real estate could be one element of a broader concept.

The project comes as Musser considers what she wants to do following her appearance on “Members Only: Palm Beach.” The series has not been canceled nor renewed.

“It doesn’t really matter to me if the show gets renewed,” she says.

Her introduction to that series came through fellow cast member Taja Abitbol, whom she met while serving on a fundraising committee.

During a cocktail party, Abitbol asked whether she would be interested in interviewing for a TV show about country club life in Palm Beach.

Musser agreed, having previously passed on other opportunities to appear on camera.

She says she was approached about a Palm Beach housewives show in 2005, but declined after being filmed because she worried about the potential impact on her marriage and young son.

This time, she decided to embrace the opportunity.

During filming, Musser says she did not prepare dialogue or attempt to adopt a different personality. With a real estate business to consider, she wanted to present herself as she normally would.

“I didn’t prepare what I was going to say,” she says.

Alongside her TV appearance, Musser was also working on a waterfront spec home that she says was completed in June and is now offered for $42.5 million.

The property is a development she built to sell. Separately, she describes her personal home as smaller but similarly finished.

According to Musser, the spec home sits along the Intracoastal Waterway, across from the Palm Beach property being developed by billionaire Ken Griffin.

Inside, she says the emphasis is on distinctive finishes and an uncompromising approach to quality, with leather-wrapped refrigerator handles, leather-lined kitchen drawers, Italian marble, and a separate chef’s kitchen.

“Every door is 12 feet high,” she says.

For Musser, those details reflect what she believes wealthy buyers want: a property that offers something different from other luxury homes, with spaces that provide their own reasons to stay in.

She describes a dining room that forms part of the wine room, along with a kitchen, living room, and theater that each deliver a different experience.

“Every room is a destination,” she says. “A place where you can go and enjoy your friends and your family.”

Her own lifestyle helps guide those decisions. Rather than approaching a development solely as something to sell, she considers whether she would enjoy living there herself.

“And I build every house as if I’m going to live in it,” she says.

Musser initially obtained her real estate license to improve her access to properties for her development business.

She felt desirable opportunities were being claimed before reaching the market and wanted to put herself in a better position to find them.

After working with a friend for a couple of years, she earned her broker’s license. Representing clients was not originally the goal, but requests from people in her community led her to begin showing and listing homes.

She says her real estate business’ gross revenue quadrupled last year, and she regards her TV experience as a success from a business perspective.

Still, the work behind her property business remains far less glamorous than the waterfront homes might suggest. Musser points to negotiating contracts, preparing properties for showings, following up with agents, and waiting for the right buyer.

Hanwha Chooses Chainlink Data and CCIP for Global Tokenization Platform

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

TL;DR: The proposal covers more than representing an asset with an on-chain token. A strategic partnership does not establish that the platform is fully operational, that collateral has already changed hands or that tokenized instruments have universal legal recognition.

Hanwha Investment & Securities announced on October 29 that it would adopt Chainlink data standards and the Cross-Chain Interoperability Protocol for a global real-world asset tokenization platform. The financial firm and Chainlink also formed a working group on collateral and institutional use cases.

The announcement and the numbers

The most concrete starting point is the dated disclosure from Hanwha Investment & Securities. It offers a specific basis for assessing Hanwha Investment & Securities’ Chainlink partnership without turning the story into unsupported price speculation.

The proposal covers more than representing an asset with an on-chain token. Hanwha described the need for credible market data, interoperable transfer rails and standards that allow eligible tokenized instruments to be used as collateral across jurisdictions and venues.

There is a practical market question underneath the announcement: how quickly can institutional real-world asset tokenization change real activity, rather than merely expand a list of proposed services? For investors, the distinction matters because product news and actual changes to liquidity or demand are not interchangeable.

Why the development matters

The question for market participants is what Hanwha Investment & Securities’ Chainlink partnership establishes today and what remains uncertain. An immediate reaction is not the same as evidence of a durable economic outcome.

A large securities firm exploring production infrastructure is a different signal from a consumer token launch. Banks and asset managers generally need workflows that fit compliance, custody, servicing and risk controls before committing significant capital.

Representing an asset on a blockchain is the relatively straightforward part of tokenization. Making that representation useful requires legal rights, reliable valuation, restrictions on eligible holders, custody and a clear process for settlement and corporate actions.

From a market perspective, the first task is to separate the scale of the announcement from the scale of economic activity that has actually occurred. A company may report a large addressable market, an institutional name or a future capability, but those are not equivalent to settled transactions or recurring fee income. This is why the primary disclosure is a better starting point than a trading narrative attached to it.

Risks the headline does not resolve

None of this removes the need for independent assessment. The published evidence identifies a development, while its risk and long-term economics are still open to examination.

An institution moving a tokenized security still needs assurance that records of ownership, transfer restrictions and valuation remain consistent. Data infrastructure connects contracts to trusted inputs; interoperability infrastructure links permissioned or public networks where assets and liquidity may reside.

A strategic partnership does not establish that the platform is fully operational, that collateral has already changed hands or that tokenized instruments have universal legal recognition. Actual utility depends on market participants agreeing the same operational standards.

Institutions may use public, private or hybrid networks, and each choice creates different expectations about access, settlement finality and oversight. Interoperability attempts to connect those environments without pretending they have identical rules.

The milestones to watch

The immediate checklist is relatively straightforward: look for verifiable implementation details and avoid interpreting a stated target as an outcome that has already occurred.

Progress will be visible in named issuance programs, supported asset classes, custodial integration and live collateral transactions. The partnership should be evaluated by implemented workflows, not by an automatic assumption about LINK token demand.

Investors also need to separate changes in infrastructure from changes in token demand. Revenue may accrue to a company, a protocol treasury or a service provider without necessarily creating a one-for-one benefit for holders of a related token. Market impact depends on adoption, competition and the design of whatever fees or incentives are involved.

A durable signal would be consistent follow-through: actual usage, transparent reporting and a credible explanation of risk. Short-term token-price moves can be driven by macroeconomic conditions or leverage and may say little about whether the product or initiative succeeds over months and years.

Trump DOJ’s California noncitizen voter list riddled with errors

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

More than 40% of people flagged by the Trump administration as suspected noncitizen voters in California aren’t on the state’s current voter rolls, according to a new analysis filed in federal court Saturday, raising fresh questions about the Justice Department‘s efforts to intervene in ballot counting days before the midterm elections.

Researchers with UCLA’s Voting Rights Project examined a list of 20,930 people identified by federal officials as potentially ineligible voters. They found that 8,880 names, or 42%, could not be matched to California’s current voter file.

The analysis also identified at least 22 people who appear to be U.S. citizens, with researchers warning that the number of citizens included on the list could be considerably higher.

The findings were submitted in litigation brought by the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights, which is challenging the administration’s efforts to use federal immigration data to identify suspected noncitizen voters.

The Justice Department demanded that California election officials separate ballots associated with the listed individuals and withhold them from counting until their citizenship can be verified. Federal prosecutors have also warned election officials of potential criminal consequences for knowingly processing ballots cast by noncitizens.

California officials rejected the demands, questioning the list’s reliability and warning that separating ballots could disrupt an election already underway.

On Friday, the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals rejected the administration’s emergency request to force California to set aside the disputed ballots. The court concluded that the government had not demonstrated that such extraordinary intervention was legally justified.

A federal district judge had previously rejected the request, warning that it could disenfranchise eligible voters and create chaos in the election system.

The Justice Department maintains that its efforts are intended to prevent illegal voting, which is prohibited in federal elections. The administration has defended its data and argued that counting ballots from ineligible voters would undermine legitimate votes.

But the new analysis raises questions about whether the government’s list accurately identifies people who are registered to vote, much less whether they are ineligible.

The dispute comes as millions of Californians have already cast ballots ahead of Tuesday’s midterm elections.
