Hormone Therapy May Boost Weight Loss on Wegovy, But Not as Much on Zepbound

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
New research shows that using hormone therapy with a GLP-1 during menopause may lead to greater weight loss. Tatsiana Volkava · Moment/Getty
  • A study presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of The Menopause Society found that women using menopause hormone therapy (MHT) alongside semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) experienced greater weight loss than those taking semaglutide alone.
  • Tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) combined with MHT showed only modest early differences in weight loss, with no statistically significant difference at later time points.
  • Certain hormone therapy medications are FDA-approved to treat menopause symptoms such as hot flashes and vaginal dryness, but not for weight loss.
  • Experts recommend individualizing treatment decisions based on symptoms, medical history, and health goals rather than adding MHT solely to enhance weight loss with GLP-1 medications.

Females using menopause hormone therapy (MHT) alongside GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic or Wegovy may experience greater weight loss, according to a study presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of the Menopause Society.

Weight gain is a common concern among middle-aged women. While much of this is associated with aging, hormonal changes during menopause can also contribute to changes in body fat and its distribution.

GLP-1 medications have been shown to be effective for weight loss. However, the results may vary by sex and reproductive stage.

For example, studies have found that women and adults under age 55 may experience greater weight loss with these medications.

A previous small observational study in postmenopausal women found that using MHT alongside semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, was associated with greater weight loss.

However, whether hormone therapy influences weight loss outcomes with different GLP-1 medications remains unclear.

To investigate this question, researchers compared weight loss in middle-aged women receiving either semaglutide or tirzepatide, with or without MHT. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

Hormone therapy linked to greater weight loss with semaglutide

The study included 3,855 women with an average age of 57 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 36 kg/m2. 

The semaglutide cohort included 2,379 individuals, while the tirzepatide cohort included 1,476 individuals. 

The average follow-up in the semaglutide cohort, both with and without MHT, was 26 months. The average follow-up in the tirzepatide group was 17 months for those using MHT and 16 months for those not using it. 

The researchers found that in the semaglutide cohort, those using MHT alongside the GLP-1 medication had significantly greater weight loss at 9 months than those not using MHT.

These differences were sustained at 12 and 15 months and at the final follow-up. Those using MHT were also more likely to reach up to a 30% total body weight loss than those not using it. 

In the tirzepatide cohort, those using MHT had a modestly greater weight loss at 3 months and were more likely to achieve more than 5% total body weight loss by the final follow-up. However, no significant difference in weight loss was observed at later time points. 

These findings suggest that MHT use may be associated with greater weight loss among middle-aged women taking semaglutide. However, the researchers did not observe a sustained, statistically significant difference in weight loss among those taking tirzepatide.

Betul Hatipoglu, MD, Director of the Diabetes and Obesity Center at the University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center, who was not involved in the study, told Healthline that the findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that people should be careful not to interpret them as evidence that hormone therapy should be added to GLP-1 treatment specifically to enhance weight loss.

“This is an association, and the study provides an opportunity to discuss how menopause, aging, changes in body composition, and metabolic health can be present,” she said. 

Should women use hormone therapy with GLP-1 medications?

It is important to keep in mind that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved certain hormone therapy medications to treat menopause symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats, and vaginal dryness. However, these medications are not FDA-approved for weight loss or obesity management. 

The study authors noted that larger studies are needed to confirm their observations and to inform individualized strategies for middle-aged females. 

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB-GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who was not involved in the study, noted that weight gain is one of the most common concerns she hears from women during menopause. Beyond affecting self-esteem and quality of life, excess abdominal fat can increase the risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease. 

“Finding effective, sustainable ways to manage weight is important for women’s long-term health and healthy aging,” she said.  

“I would like to see prospective, randomized studies examining GLP-1 medications with and without hormone therapy to determine whether hormone therapy directly improves weight loss and helps women maintain it over time. In the meantime, treatment decisions should be individualized and guided by a healthcare professional.”

How to decide whether GLP-1 medications or hormone therapy are right for you

Ross advised that the decision should be based on your individual symptoms, health history, and treatment goals.

“Hormone therapy can significantly improve disruptive menopausal symptoms, including hot flashes, sleep disturbances, and mood changes. By helping women feel better and more energetic, it may also make it easier to exercise and manage their weight,” she said. 

Hatipoglu noted that GLP-1-based medications, on the other hand, are effective treatments for obesity and can improve important aspects of metabolic health. Their use should be based on an individual’s weight-related health risks, medical history, and treatment goals.

“For a woman who has both bothersome menopausal symptoms and obesity, it may be entirely appropriate to consider both treatments. But I would not recommend starting hormone therapy solely to improve the weight-loss response to a GLP-1 medication based on the evidence currently available,” she said. 

“It is also important to remember that successful weight management during and after menopause is about more than the number on the scale. Preserving muscle mass, maintaining bone health, improving metabolic health, and supporting long-term physical function are equally important.”

You should speak with your healthcare professional about the risks and benefits of all treatment options.

This Is How Fast You Should Walk to Help Lower Your Risk of Death

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
People who walk faster may have better underlying health. Lol2368/E+/Getty Images
  • A study found that walking speed can help predict people’s risk of dying from any cause.
  • Both a clinical test and a test done in daily life were able to predict mortality risk.
  • Experts say walking speed predicts death risk because it’s an indicator of overall health.
  • If your walking speed slows, it’s a good idea to talk with your healthcare professional.

New research shows that how fast you walk, whether measured in a clinical test or through daily activity, can help predict your risk of dying from any cause.

The study compared two ways of measuring walking: a short walking speed test conducted in a clinic and the step rate people take in their everyday lives, measured with a wrist-worn device.

Both measures were linked to mortality risk and showed similar ability to predict it.

The findings, published on October 23 in JAMA Network Open, could help doctors and researchers choose the best method for assessing health risks in different settings.

Clinical walking speed vs. everyday walking

The study was part of the Whitehall II accelerometer substudy in the United Kingdom.

It included 4,067 participants, mostly males, with an average age of about 69 years. About one-third of the subjects were female.

The researchers collected data from January 2012 to December 2013 and followed up through August 2024.

To measure clinical walking speed, participants completed a standard walking test. They walked a marked 2.44-meter course at their usual pace. The test was repeated three times, and the average speed in meters per second was used for analysis. Participants who normally used a cane were allowed to use it during the test.

For free-living walking measurements, participants wore a wrist-worn GENEActiv accelerometer for 9 consecutive days. This device recorded movement at a high frequency and used special software to detect walking episodes and count steps.

From this data, two key measures of walking cadence were calculated: the median walking cadence (the middle value of steps per minute during walking periods) and the 95th-percentile walking cadence (a higher step rate indicating brisk walking). The study also calculated the average number of daily steps.

The researchers additionally gathered information on factors known to affect mortality, such as:

  • age
  • sex
  • education
  • race and ethnicity
  • marital status
  • smoking
  • alcohol use
  • diet
  • body mass index (BMI)
  • hypertension
  • chronic diseases

National death records were used to identify deaths during the follow-up period.

Statistical methods were then used to estimate the risk of death associated with walking speed and cadence while accounting for other risk factors.

The models were adjusted step-by-step: starting with no adjustments, then adding sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, and finally health-related factors.

The ability of walking measures to predict mortality was assessed using a statistical measure.

The study also examined differences between males and females.

The researchers additionally conducted analyses to test how well the results held up when they excluded early deaths, looked at subgroups by age and health status, and used different definitions of walking cadence.

Faster walking speed linked to 23% lower risk of death

During a median follow-up of nearly 12 years, 739 participants died.

The average clinical walking speed of participants was 1.2 meters per second. The median walking cadence during daily life was about 75 steps per minute, and the 95th percentile walking cadence was about 112 steps per minute.

The study found only a weak correlation between clinical walking speed and free-living walking cadence, meaning the two measures do not strongly reflect the same walking behavior. However, both measures were independently associated with a lower risk of death.

After adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle, and health factors, for every standard increase in clinical walking speed, the risk of death was 23% lower.

Similarly, higher median and 95th-percentile walking cadences were associated with 17% and 18% lower risks of death, respectively.

When examining how well these walking measures predicted mortality, each on its own showed modest predictive ability.

Adding clinical walking speed to a model with other known risk factors slightly improved mortality prediction, while adding free-living walking cadence also improved prediction, albeit to a similar degree to that of clinical walking speed.

Combining clinical walking speed with free-living walking parameters provided a small boost in predictive ability, but the effect was not significant after accounting for detailed health factors.

The study also found that walking speed and cadence had similar predictive power for both men and women, although the overall prediction models performed slightly better for women.

Several sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. Results were consistent when excluding early deaths, focusing on healthier or older participants, and using different ways to define walking cadence.

Overall, this study suggests that clinical walking speed tests and free-living walking cadence measurements from wrist-worn devices are both useful in predicting all-cause mortality risk. They provide similar information but reflect different aspects of walking behavior.

The choice between methods may depend on the purpose of the assessment, available resources, and the context in which the measurement is taken.

Faster walkers may live longer because they are healthier

Neha Sabharwal, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and director of rehabilitation specializing in geriatric mobility and functional decline at Vintage Faire Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Modesto, CA, said that walking speed helps predict longevity because it’s an indicator of someone’s overall health.

“Walking is something most of us do without thinking about it, but it requires several body systems to work together,” she told Healthline. “Your heart and lungs have to keep up, your muscles need to produce enough force, and your brain has to coordinate each step while keeping you balanced.”

Sabharwal, who wasn’t involved in the study, said that in her work, she pays attention to when someone starts walking more slowly than before.

“It is easy to dismiss that change as part of getting older, but there may be something else going on,” she said. This could include muscle weakness, pain, reduced endurance, balance problems, or underlying medical conditions.

According to Sabharwal, the current study is valuable because it suggests that useful information about people’s movement can be obtained outside a clinical setting.

However, she cautioned against misinterpreting the findings to mean that simply walking faster will make you live longer.

“People who naturally walk faster may already have better underlying health,” said Sabharwal.

“I would be less concerned about whether someone walks faster than their neighbor and more interested in whether they are walking slower than they did six months ago,” she said.

What to do if your walking speed slows

Theodore Strange, MD, chairman of medicine at Northwell Staten Island University Hospital and senior vice president of the Metro Market Region at Northwell Health, shared some insights for those who may notice their walking speed has declined. Strange wasn’t involved in the study.

“I mean, if it’s slowing down because a week or two ago, you were walking up a flight of steps with no problem, and now, you’re having some shortness of breath, I think it should be evaluated,” he told Healthline.

Strange added that if you’re experiencing aches or pains that weren’t there before, you should consult a healthcare professional, as they can make exercise more difficult.

It’s also essential to manage any chronic diseases that you have so that you can exercise as well as possible within the constraints of your condition, he said.

“And I think slow and steady wins the race here,” said Strange. He suggests monitoring your walking with a device like a pedometer and progressively increasing your steps.

“Everybody talks about 10,000 steps a day. I think the most recent studies show probably 7,000 to 8,000 is adequate,” he said. You can start where you are and work your way up gradually.

Strange further noted that physical therapy can sometimes be helpful if you have issues with gait mechanics, balance, or strength. Additionally, you might benefit from exercise that is tailored to your condition, for example, if you’ve had a stroke.

Finally, Strange advised stretching before and after exercise to keep your muscles moving.

“I go back to adequate nutrition and hydration being important,” he said. You want to make sure you are adequately supporting your body’s ability to exercise, said Strange.

6 to 8 Cups of Tea Daily May Reduce Heart Disease Risk by 11%

Recent research has found that regularly drinking tea may help reduce your risk of heart disease. Guido Mieth · Moment/Getty
  • Regular tea drinkers may have an 11% lower risk of vascular disease, including a 37% lower risk of ischemic stroke and a 17% lower risk of heart attack, according to a large study.
  • Both black and green tea may support heart health when consumed without added sugar, though the findings show an association rather than prove that tea prevents disease.
  • People who drank tea regularly, exercised, and didn’t smoke had the lowest risk of vascular disease.
  • Drinking very hot tea was associated with a 16% higher risk of vascular disease.

People who regularly drink tea may have an 11% lower overall risk of vascular disease, which includes conditions such as stroke, heart disease, and peripheral artery disease. This is according to a study published on October 21 in the Journal of Internal Medicine. 

Both black tea and green tea may offer heart health benefits. However, the study shows an association rather than proving that drinking tea prevents cardiovascular disease.

The lowest overall vascular risk was seen among tea drinkers who were also physically active and didn’t smoke. 

Tea may help lower vascular disease risk by 11%

The study followed 485,934 people ages 30–79 from the China Kadoorie Biobank for an average of 9 years. 

The researchers found that regular tea drinkers had an 11% lower overall risk of vascular disease compared with those who did not regularly drink tea. They also observed a 37% lower risk of ischemic stroke and a 17% lower risk of heart attack.

There was no clear link for hemorrhagic stroke or pulmonary heart disease.

Among people who drank tea at least weekly, the lowest estimated risk was seen in those consuming approximately 6–8 cups per day. However, the findings do not establish this as a recommended daily intake.

Amit Blumfield, MD, a cardiologist with Manhattan Cardiology in NYC who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that it is important to remember this is observational data.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining the effects of drinking black and green tea on lipids and blood pressure showed only modest, non-significant benefits. 

The study also had limitations, including self-reported tea consumption and the possibility that other dietary and lifestyle factors influenced the results.

“Overall, tea seems to, maybe, have some cardiovascular benefits without a significant risk, so I favor tea consumption,” he said. 

Michelle Routhenstein, a preventive cardiology dietitian at Entirely Nourished who was not involved in the research, emphasized that the findings do not establish a cause-and-effect relationship between drinking tea and a lower risk of vascular disease. 

“Tea can be a helpful addition to a heart-healthy lifestyle, but it is not a substitute for addressing individual cardiovascular risk factors,” she said.

The researchers also found that drinking “burning hot” tea increased the risk of vascular disease by 16%, consistent with previous research raising concerns about the consumption of very hot beverages.  

“I recommend enjoying your tea’s potential benefits by letting it cool to a comfortable sipping temperature before you drink it,” said Routhenstein.

Black tea vs. green tea: Which is better for your heart?

The study examined black, green, Oolong, and other tea types. 

The researchers found that black tea showed a more consistent association with a lower risk of vascular disease, which is contrary to some earlier research. However, they noted that these results don’t establish black tea as superior to green tea, and they don’t mean you should switch tea types. 

“Both black and green tea are great choices for your heart health,  and neither is definitively better than the other,” said Routhenstein. 

“I recommend choosing whichever you enjoy and consuming it consistently, ideally without much added sugar,” Routhenstein noted. 

Blumfield also noted that adding sugar or non-sugar sweeteners may change the potential health effects of drinking tea, so the findings should not automatically be extended to sweetened tea beverages.

Other health benefits of drinking tea

Some older research suggests there may be additional health benefits to drinking both black and green tea. These benefits may include: 

  • prevention of chronic diseases like cancer and cardiovascular disease
  • diabetes prevention
  • metabolic health
  • reduced arthritis risk

Routhenstein said that tea may also support focus and alertness, improve hydration status, and potentially play a role in metabolic health. 

“Enjoying tea can be a simple way to incorporate beneficial plant compounds into your day,” she added. 

While drinking tea has been considered a health-promoting habit since ancient times, more research is needed to confirm its various health benefits.

A CDC report shows an increase in drug-resistant superbugs across the United States. Abraham Gonzalez Fernandez · Moment/Getty
  • Drug-resistant bacteria and fungi cause roughly 9.7 million infections and 50,800 deaths annually in the United States, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).
  • Hospital-acquired infections account for over 42,000 deaths and cost more than $12 billion.
  • Overuse of antibiotics — particularly for viral infections where they offer no benefit — creates selective pressure that allows resistant bacterial strains to survive and spread.
  • You can prevent antibiotic resistance by practicing good hand hygiene, using antibiotics only as prescribed, and not requesting antibiotics for viral infections.

New data show a substantial nationwide increase in potentially deadly infections caused by drug-resistant pathogens. 

The updated Antimicrobial Resistance (AR) Threats Report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) states that drug-resistant bacteria and fungi cause approximately 9.7 million infections in the United States each year. This is up from 2.8 million in a similar report from 2019. 

The estimated deaths in the United States from these drug-resistant pathogens have also risen from 35,000 to 50,800. 

The new report also noted that infections that commonly occur in people in the hospital account for more than 42,000 deaths and cost the U.S. healthcare system more than $12 billion. 

These findings are based on 2023 data. Officials from the CDC said that the increase in drug-resistant infections from 2019 partially reflects a real rise in infections caused by certain resistant bacteria and fungi. However, they also noted that data sources and methodologies have changed, and direct comparisons between the two reports shouldn’t be made. 

“These numbers are alarming by any measure but are particularly sobering when infectious diseases physicians connect real patients with these statistics in their everyday experience,” Wendy Armstrong, MD, President of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), said in a statement. 

19 drug-resistant pathogens linked to infection increase 

The CDC report highlighted 19 drug-resistant pathogens responsible for the 9.7 million annual infections. Four of these pathogens are classified as urgent threats, 13 as serious threats, and 2 as concerning. 

The CDC also maintains a watch list that currently includes six pathogens. 

The four pathogens classified as urgent threats are: 

  • Candida auris
  • carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter 
  • carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE)
  • drug-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae

William Schaffner, MD, a professor of the Division of Infectious Diseases at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, told Healthline that the reason for this increase in superbugs is, “Overuse of antibiotics; using them for too long or in circumstances where they do not offer a benefit.”   

“An example of the latter is the antibiotic treatment of common viral respiratory infections — antibiotics have no effect on viral infections. The widespread use of antibiotics kills off those bacteria that are susceptible to antibiotics, but then can leave a few bacterial survivors that are resistant to the effect of the antibiotics, resulting in the spread of these resistant strains,” he said. 

Monica Gandhi, MD, a professor of medicine at the University of California, San Francisco, agreed and added, “This widespread use leads to selective drug pressure on the pathogen to evolve resistance to these antibiotics, making them harder to treat.”

“The risk to public health from these bugs is substantial. In fact, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates up to 5 million deaths from these bugs worldwide every year, making these a substantial public health threat,” said Gandhi. 

Candida auris tops the list 

Candida auris tops the list of urgent threats.

Cases have increased nearly tenfold since the first detection in hospitals 10 years ago. This includes a more than 50% jump between 2022 and 2023. 

Candida auris is a fungus that is almost completely resistant to one class of antifungal medications. It has also shown emerging resistance to the other two classes.

This fungus is often found on people’s skin, spreads rapidly in healthcare settings like hospitals, and affects some of the most vulnerable individuals. 

CRE is increasingly difficult to treat

CRE is a family of multidrug-resistant bacterial pathogens. They have become increasingly difficult to treat and can share resistance with other bacteria. 

These bacteria also pose a significant threat to people in hospitals, especially those with devices such as ventilators and catheters. A recent study found that CRE cases at U.S. hospitals increased by 18% between 2019 and 2023. 

While those in hospitals are especially vulnerable to CRE, 67% of the cases in 2023 were in the community. However, some of these cases may have occurred in people with recent exposure to healthcare. 

Untreated neisseria gonorrhoeae may lead to other conditions

Neisseria gonorrhoeae is the bacterium that causes gonorrhea, which is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections.

The CDC report noted that only a few antibiotics can effectively treat this infection.

Without effective treatment, gonorrhea can lead to:

  • pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
  • ectopic pregnancy
  • infertility

Preventing drug-resistant superbugs

There are ways you can help prevent these superbugs: 

  • practice good hand hygiene 
  • use antibiotics only as prescribed 
  • keep up with vaccines

“[One] way individuals can help prevent the evolution of these superbugs is to not ask for antibiotics from their providers or urgent care settings in the setting of a viral infection,” said Gandhi.

What experts and organizations are doing to reduce superbugs

Experts and organizations like the CDC are working together to find ways to reduce the spread of these superbugs. 

A few of these include: 

  • supporting enhanced antibiotic stewardship
  • improving infection control
  • investing in new drug development

“Professional organizations and hospitals are educating their doctors and staff about the antibiotic resistance problem and have created reviews of practice patterns to encourage the appropriate use of antibiotics,” said Schaffner. 

Gandhi added that organizations should institute procedures, such as “antimicrobial stewardship programs,” to ensure that providers do not prescribe antibiotics for non-bacterial infections and narrow antibiotics as soon as possible. 

The Pioneering Antimicrobial Subscriptions to End Upsurging Resistance (PASTEUR) Act aims to revitalize antibiotic and antifungal development by changing the way the federal government pays for novel antimicrobials.  

This act was reintroduced in Congress in February and is currently under review. 

The focus on reducing the spread of superbugs and developing new antibiotics is important. 

“You really cannot have all the benefits of modern medicine, even outside the realm of infectious diseases, without effective antibiotics. It all goes away,” Haley Ann Pritchard, MD, medical director of infection prevention and antimicrobial stewardship at Eskenazi Health Hospital, said in a statement.

Taking Fish Oil to Boost Brain Health? A 2-Year Study Questions the Benefits

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Research suggests that fish oil supplements may offer no benefit for Alzheimer’s prevention. Anastasiia Sienotova/Moment/Getty Images
  • A 2-year clinical trial found that high dose omega-3 supplements didn’t improve memory, thinking skills, or brain structure in older adults at increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease.
  • The supplements raised omega-3 levels in participants’ blood and in the fluid around the brain, yet their test scores and brain structure matched those of the placebo group.
  • Experts recommend a broader approach to supporting brain health, including regular exercise, quality sleep, and a dietary pattern such as the Mediterranean diet, which emphasizes fish.

A clinical trial found that taking high dose omega-3 supplements for 2 years did not help memory or thinking skills in older adults at increased risk of Alzheimer’s.

Researchers at Keck Medicine of USC led the study, which was published in eBioMedicine. Participants took either a daily high dose supplement of omega-3 fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) or a placebo.

Fish oil is one of the most familiar ways people get omega-3s. However, the supplement in this clinical trial wasn’t made from fish, Hussein Yassine, MD, director of the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health with Keck Medicine of USC and the study’s lead researcher, told Healthline. This article uses the broader term omega-3 supplements.

The researchers found that taking supplements raised omega-3 levels in participants’ brains, yet it didn’t change their memory, thinking skills, or brain structure.

Dung Trinh, MD, an internist at MemorialCare Medical Group and chief medical officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, California, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that the findings challenge the common belief that omega-3 supplements, such as fish oil, can help prevent Alzheimer’s disease or improve memory.

Millions of older adults take omega-3 supplements. In a 2017–2018 federal survey, 21.8% of U.S. adults age 60 and older reported taking an omega-3 fatty acid supplement in the past 30 days, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

Getting omega-3s from food or supplements is important since the body cannot make them.

Omega-3 supplements, memory, and Alzheimer’s risk

Keck Medicine researchers enrolled 365 adults who did not have dementia, and whose ages ranged from 55 to 80, with an average age of 66.

Subjects obtained little DHA from their diets and had at least one risk factor for dementia. Of the 365 people who enrolled, 225 completed the trial, according to the study. Nearly half (47%) carried APOE4.

Participants took either 2,000 milligrams (mg) of DHA or a placebo every day. Neither participants nor researchers knew who received the supplement.

The supplement in the trial contained more than 90% DHA and about 1% EPA, Yassine said.

Researchers measured:

  • size of brain regions tied to memory
  • DHA levels in cerebrospinal fluid, the liquid around the brain and spinal cord, after 6 months
  • memory and thinking skills over the course of the trial

Researchers found that high dose omega-3 supplements raised DHA levels in the fluid around the brain and spinal cord by 17%.

Both groups in the trial scored about the same on memory and thinking tests. Brain structure appeared the same in both groups.

The supplement also raised omega-3 levels in the blood. Over two years, participants’ omega-3 index, a measure of EPA and DHA in red blood cells, rose from 4.9% to 11%.

“That’s a massive increase, but that doesn’t translate into clinical outcomes,” Yassine said.

DHA reached the brain equally well in people with and without the gene variant APOE4, which is linked to a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease, according to the study.

Earlier trials couldn’t tell whether DHA failed to reach the brain or reached it without helping, the study’s authors say. This trial answers that open question, since the DHA got there.

“This tells us that just because a supplement reaches the brain doesn’t necessarily mean it will improve brain function or prevent cognitive decline,” Trinh said.

Long-term benefits of omega-3 supplements are unknown

The lack of measurable change in memory and thinking may come down to how the body uses omega-3s, according to Yassine.

He said it’s “really important to distinguish between omega-3 metabolism and omega-3 supplements.”

A supplement can raise omega-3 levels, yet the body may still break them down before they reach the right places. Obesity, smoking, and a lack of exercise can all reduce how much omega-3 gets into the brain, he said.

Some fish and fish oil supplements may offer more than omega-3s. These sources may also include other nutrients such as lutein, vitamin E, and selenium, Yassine said.

A supplement can raise the level, but if you don’t change the holistic program, just going and increasing the level by itself may not be sufficient,” he said.

Researchers tend to fall into two camps on how to read the results, according to Yassine.

Some see the findings as evidence that supplements don’t work in isolation. Others argue the trial would need a longer follow-up or sicker participants to answer the question.

Trinh pointed to the study’s length. “It’s also important to recognize that Alzheimer’s disease develops over decades, so a two-year study may not be long enough to determine whether DHA supplementation has any long-term preventive benefits,” he said.

Effects of omega-3s, fish oil on brain health

Fish oil supplements contain two omega-3 fatty acids, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and DHA, according to the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH).

Foods such as fish and flaxseed also contain omega-3s, according to the National Institutes of Health’s Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS).

Research suggests omega-3s may help reduce inflammation, support heart health, and promote childhood growth and development.

DHA’s role in the brain

Some observational studies link diets high in omega-3s with a lower risk of cognitive decline and dementia.

Researchers say omega-3s may help preserve cognition by supporting brain cells in continuing to function and maintain their membranes, according to the ODS.

“Omega-3s are important for the brain, and we and many others have shown that having higher levels of omega-3s is associated with less Alzheimer’s pathology, better cognition, and many other protective outcomes,” Yassine said, in contrast to the trial findings.

Trinh agreed. “DHA plays an essential role in maintaining healthy brain cells, and there is evidence that eating fish as part of a healthy dietary pattern may support long-term brain health,” he said.

Research on omega-3s and brain health is ongoing, and there is insufficient evidence to draw firm conclusions about their effectiveness.

Who benefits from omega-3 supplements?

Some people may still benefit from a supplement, according to Yassine, including:

  • people who don’t eat fish, such as vegans, especially those with a genetic risk for dementia
  • people whose omega-3 levels test very low

Some neurologists test omega-3 levels, though the test isn’t standard, Yassine said.

People with nutritional deficiencies or certain genetic or metabolic traits may respond differently to omega-3 supplements, and researchers still need to learn who might benefit, Trinh said.

People who already take fish oil or other omega-3 supplements for other reasons, such as high triglycerides, should make those decisions with their healthcare professionals, he said.

Tips to support brain health as you age

Trinh said he tells his patients that “what is good for the heart is generally good for the brain.” He recommended the following healthy habits:

  • Regular physical activity: At least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity each week, as tolerated, including aerobic exercise such as brisk walking, swimming, or cycling, plus strength training
  • Quality sleep: Aim for 7 to 9 hours each night.
  • A brain-healthy diet: Adopting a healthy eating pattern such as the Mediterranean or MIND diet, which emphasizes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, and fish.
  • Managing heart health: Maintaining healthy blood pressure and cholesterol levels, managing diabetes, avoiding smoking, and limiting alcohol.
  • Staying mentally active: Reading, learning new skills, and doing meaningful activities.
  • Staying socially connected: Spending time with family, friends, and community.
  • Addressing hearing and vision loss: An important strategy that often gets overlooked.

“Overall, this study reminds us that there is no magic pill for preventing Alzheimer’s disease and that maintaining brain health requires a much broader approach,” Trinh said.

  • Young adults with obesity may face a higher risk of developing cancer before midlife compared to those at a moderate weight, according to new research.
  • Cancer risk increased progressively with weight, from 1.84% in people at a moderate weight to 2.62% in those with severe obesity.
  • Abdominal obesity (measured by waist circumference) was also linked to elevated cancer risk, even in people with moderate weight.
  • Weight management through diet, exercise, and medications may help reduce cancer risk.

Younger adults with obesity may be more likely to develop cancer before midlife than those who maintain a moderate weight, according to a study published on October 26 in the Annals of Internal Medicine. 

The risk for cancer became higher as weight increased. The highest rates were seen in those with severe obesity and abdominal obesity. 

This highlights the potential importance of weight management in the prevention of early onset cancer. 

Obesity in young adulthood increases risk of cancer

Rates of both obesity and early onset cancer are rising among younger adults globally. 

This study analyzed data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service database. The researchers identified 6,313,338 adults ages 20 to 39 who had no history of cancer and who underwent standardized national health screenings. 

The researchers followed the participants for an average of 11 years. 

Body mass index (BMI) was categorized into five groups ranging from underweight to class II obesity using the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for Asian populations. Waist circumference was also measured as an indicator of abdominal adiposity. 

During the analysis, the researchers adjusted for various factors such as: 

  • age 
  • sex 
  • smoking status 
  • alcohol consumption 
  • physical activity 
  • income 
  • residential area 
  • metabolic comorbidities, like hypertension, diabetes duration, glucose tolerance, and dyslipidemia

Over the follow-up period, there were 151,893 cases of cancer among the participants. The incidence rate increased progressively based on obesity categories, from 1.84% in “normal-weight” individuals to 2.24% in class I obesity and 2.62% in class II obesity. 

The strongest associations were seen in the following cancers: 

  • thyroid
  • endometrial
  • ovarian
  • colorectal
  • kidney
  • liver
  • pancreatic

Obesity was not associated with breast cancer diagnosed before the age of 50. 

Ketan Thanki, MD, board certified colorectal surgeon with the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in Long Beach, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, explained to Healthline that obesity itself causes states that promote cancer formation. 

Obesity-cancer connection is a global issue

This current study focused on the Korean population. However, the connection between obesity and cancer risk is a global issue. 

Previous research has shown that around 10% of all new cancer diagnoses in the United States are associated with obesity. Obesity also accounts for up to 50% of certain cancers, including endometrial and liver cancer. 

Waist size matters even at a moderate weight

The study also showed that waist circumference, which indicates abdominal obesity, may be linked to cancer risk that is similar to that of individuals with overweight. 

The study researchers used the thresholds set out by the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity. These thresholds put the waist circumference cutoff at over 90 cm (35.4 in) for males and over 85cm (33.46 in) for females. 

These are not universal cutoffs. According to the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), waist circumference measurements that can increase your risk of negative health issues are 101.6 cm (40 in) for males and 88.9 cm (35 in) for females. 

Previous research has also suggested an association between waist circumference and cancer risk, independent of BMI. One study showed a potential 11% increased risk of cancer in people with a waist circumference over the thresholds, even if they were physically active.  

People who have smoked may face higher cancer risk

The current study found that males had a higher relative association between obesity and cancer, while females showed higher absolute risk. 

The researchers also found that the absolute risk was higher in adults ages 30 to 39. 

Those who currently smoke or who have ever smoked were also found to have a higher risk of developing cancer than those who have never smoked. 

“Diet, exercise, and now medications can all be used very effectively to reduce one’s weight and, hopefully as a result, cancer risk,” Thanki said. 

He also noted that, due to differences between South Korean populations and those in other countries such as the United States, further studies will be needed to determine how this translates across other populations. 

“But I think this trend will likely extend across all cultures,” he said.

Why reading together is about more than sharing good stories

There’s a huge amount of reciprocal joy we take from this nightly ritual, beyond the practical benefits of providing a calming demarcation between the end of the day and the wind-down to sleep.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being. 

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

“Reading is a great way to support our brain health, develop important cognitive skills and boost our wellbeing. It’s something we ought to be encouraging from an early age, given the increasing evidence of its benefits, particularly to children, but really, it’s never too late to start, and it can bring benefits throughout our lives and into old age,” 

Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology, Cambridge University 

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

Among more than 10,000 US adolescents, those who started reading for pleasure early had fewer mental health problems, spent less time on screens and slept more. Although, per an earlier column of mine, I can’t vouch for the sleep side of things when it comes to early bird Henry. 

For many, reading can be a form of therapy that improves well-being. Bibliotherapy is a complementary therapeutic tool for emotional release and reduced stress and anxiety, with its roots traced back to ancient civilizations.

There’s even research suggesting a link between a love of reading and a lower risk of cognitive decline.

In a 2003 study of people aged 75 and over, reading was associated with a 35% lower risk of dementia over roughly five years.

A separate 14-year study from 2022 found that people who read at least weekly saw less cognitive decline, whatever their level of education.

A happy (and healthy) by-product of this love of being read to is that both my kids have now started reading to me at bedtime. White – not a dry eye in the house for the final few chapters of that one.

They’ve also gone off and re-read the Harry Potter books and “The Hobbit” to themselves. 

The research above links reading to many health benefits: better attention and memory, fewer mental health problems, more sleep, less time on screens, and greater empathy. I haven’t measured any of those impacts empirically in our house. Nor am I naive to the fact that as they get older, they’ll ultimately grow out of me reading to them, with school, extracurricular activities, their friends, and inevitably screens competing for more and more of their time.

Come what may, though, I know that a child who considers missing a reading session as the most cardinal of cardinal sins, and who scrambles for the chance to read aloud to their dad at bedtime is, at that moment, concentrating, empathizing, discovering and growing. 

Researchers are clear that studies espousing the benefits of reading show association rather than proof, and it’s evident that more experimental work and deeper research are needed to ensure the complex cause and effect stands on firm scientific ground. 

Regardless, it’s a healthy habit Amelia, Henry and I are very happy to keep, and with a bit of luck, I’ll get to pass it on to their kids one day.

  • The average number of calories U.S. adults consumed from coffee beverages increased by nearly 40% between 2003 and 2018, rising from 43 to 60 calories per day, even as the amount of coffee consumed remained relatively stable.
  • Non-dairy creamer became the largest source of calories added to coffee by 2018, contributing about 19 calories per day, more than double the amount recorded in 2003.
  • Previous research has linked moderate coffee consumption to a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers, but the new study did not examine whether adding sugar or creamer affects these potential health benefits.

Coffee consumption among U.S. adults remained relatively stable between 2003 and 2018, but the average number of calories consumed from coffee beverages increased by nearly 40%, according to a study published in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics. 

By 2017–2018, U.S. adults consumed an average of about 13 ounces of coffee beverages per day. Meanwhile, the average daily calorie intake from these beverages had increased from 43 to 60 calories since 2003–2004.

By 2018, non-dairy creamer had become the largest source of calories added to coffee, surpassing sugar or syrup and dairy milk.

Coffee consumption stayed steady, but calories increased

The researchers analyzed data from eight consecutive cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2003 to 2018. The study included 30,459 U.S. adults ages 20 and older who provided two 24-hour dietary recalls.

The researchers categorized coffee beverages based on whether they contained added ingredients. These additions included:

  • dairy milk
  • half-and-half
  • dairy cream
  • non-dairy milk
  • non-dairy creamer
  • sugar or syrup
  • sugar substitute
  • other coffee additions, such as cocoa powder or alcohol 

The researchers found that coffee intake remained stable, while the average calorie intake from coffee beverages increased. 

The absolute increase in caloric intake is about 17 kcal per day, which is only about 1% of a 2,000 kcal diet. 

Coffee consumption was also higher among men, non-Hispanic white adults, and people with higher incomes. Those who slept fewer hours or worked longer hours also tended to drink more coffee.

Non-dairy creamer adds more calories to coffee than sugar

In 2018, the most common coffee additions by weight were: 

  • dairy milk (21.4 grams per day)
  • non-dairy creamer (8.1 grams per day)
  • sugar (4.4 grams per day)

The researchers found no significant changes in the amounts of dairy milk, sugar or syrup, and sugar substitutes added to coffee between 2003 and 2018.

However, consumption of non-dairy additions increased during this period. Non-dairy creamer consumption rose from 3.37 to 8.08 grams per day, while non-dairy milk increased from 0.27 to 2.45 grams per day.

By 2017–2018, non-dairy creamer contributed the most calories among coffee additions:

  • non-dairy creamer (18.5 kcal per day)
  • sugar (16 kcal per day)
  • dairy milk (11 kcal per day) 

In 2003–2004, non-dairy creamer contributed an average of 7.7 calories per day, meaning its calorie contribution more than doubled by 2017–2018.

Kristin Kirkpatrick, president of KAK, Consulting, LLC, and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine in Cleveland, Ohio, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings didn’t surprise her. 

“The variety of coffee drinks, both at coffee houses and grocery stores, as well as options for creamers in the grocery store, make it easy to trade in black coffee for something closer to dessert,” she said. 

She also added, however, that not all non-dairy creamers are created equal. 

“Some are quite natural, with minimal ingredients, including no added sugars and no additives. Others are ingredient-heavy with either a large amount of sugar (real or artificial) and additives, so it’s really based on the type that is purchased,” she noted. 

How coffee additions may affect its health benefits

More than 60% of Americans drink coffee daily. Research suggests that drinking coffee with little to no added sugar or saturated fat may offer health benefits.

Previous research has associated moderate coffee consumption, typically around 3–5 cups per day, with a lower risk of several health conditions, including:

  • cardiovascular disease
  • diabetes
  • stroke
  • respiratory conditions
  • cognitive decline
  • cancer, such as uterine and liver

“These benefits make sense. After all, coffee beans are indeed a deeply hued plant, with multiple bioactive compounds that may strengthen health,” said Kirkpatrick. 

She also cautioned consumers to be mindful of any caffeine sensitivities they may have. However, the benefits of coffee have been seen in studies assessing both caffeinated and decaf options.

However, adding ingredients high in sugar or saturated fat could offset some of coffee’s potential health benefits.

Kirkpatrick noted that some studies suggest milk may enhance antioxidant activity. The current study did not examine whether coffee additions reduced its health benefits, but found that these ingredients contributed additional calories.

Other research has raised concerns about the potential health effects of consuming excessive added sugar and certain food additives.

“I suggest that my patients try to embrace a black cup of coffee as opposed to starting the day with sugar,” Kirkpatrick said. 

She suggested that certain additions, such as chicory root or cinnamon, may enhance your brew while still providing benefits. Plain milk can also provide protein and calcium without added sugar.

“Starting the day with [something] sweet may make it difficult to limit sugary foods later in the day. Starting the day with a more bitter approach may [be] an important factor in keeping sugar down, as bitter compounds may positively impact appetite-regulating pathways,” said Kirkpatrick.

The Connector Was Never the Work: Five Questions I Ask Before Estimating an Integration

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every integration ticket I have been handed started with the same sentence: we just need this system to talk to that one. It reads like a connector. It gets estimated like a connector. Then it turns into a project, because the connection was never the work.

The HTTP call is the easy part. What eats the weeks is unspecified behavior: what happens when both sides edit the same record, when the receiving system rejects a row for a validation rule nobody mentioned, and when a human edits by hand the field your automation is also writing. Those are business decisions wearing technical clothes.

This is the checklist I run before estimating one, adapted from a guide I wrote for my studio's clients.

First, name the shape

There are three shapes, and they cost very different amounts.

A one-way feed. Data goes from A to B and never comes back. Orders into accounting, leads into a CRM, events into a warehouse. Cheapest shape, because there is nothing to reconcile.

A two-way sync. Both systems can change the same record and both changes must survive. This is where the money goes. Every field needs an owner, every conflict needs a rule, and you have to stop the two systems bouncing the same update back and forth forever. Most "sync" requests turn out to be a one-way feed plus two or three fields that genuinely go both ways. Finding that out early is worth weeks.

A process that spans systems. A new order triggers a credit check, a stock reservation, a notification and an invoice across four tools, with conditions at each step. That is not a connection. It is a small application whose interface happens to be other people's software, and it should be scoped like one.

Most of my voice agent work is the third kind. The agent on the phone is the visible part; the value is where the outcome lands.

The five questions that size it

1. What does each end actually give you?

Two documented APIs with a sandbox is the good case. In practice you meet an API that covers the reads but not the writes, a rate limit that quietly caps how fast you can backfill history, or no API at all.

With no interface, the options are a file exchange, a read replica, the vendor's export, or, at the very bottom, scripting the UI, which is fragile and should be called fragile out loud. I confirm sandbox access and rate limits before agreeing to any date, because both routinely change the plan.

2. Who owns which field?

Source of truth is per field, not per system. The CRM owns the phone number. The billing platform owns the payment terms. Where two systems both let a human edit the same field, you either pick a winner or write a rule. Leaving it unstated is how you get a sync that flaps between two values while each team insists its system is right.

This shows up in code more literally than people expect. In VoiceDash, the Retell webhook upserts a call into a Conversation row, but the update block deliberately leaves out tags, notes, summaries and evaluations. Those fields belong to the analysis step and to the humans using the portal, and a late webhook must never overwrite them. I wrote up the full version of that in Undefined Is Not Null. The field map was not a document. It was which keys I allowed into an update: {} object.

3. How fresh does the data have to be?

Nightly batch and event-driven within seconds are different builds, not a config flag. Real time brings webhooks, queues, ordering questions and a replay path for events you missed while you were down. So ask what actually breaks if the data is an hour old. Often nothing.

Set freshness per flow, not per project. And if you do go event-driven, remember that a webhook tells you something happened, not that you have everything. You will want a backfill path anyway.

4. How is the same thing recognized on both sides?

Two systems almost never share an identifier. The same customer is a record in one and a contact in the other, matched on an email that has since changed or a phone number stored in three formats. You need an explicit matching rule, and you need a place for the records that do not match, with a person who checks it.

Data that does not reconcile is worse than missing data, because people keep trusting it. The accounting software I build at Null Studio lives and dies on this; more in A Stored Balance Is a Rumor.

5. What happens when a transfer fails?

Not if. A destination will be down, a record will be rejected, a credential will expire. The difference between an integration that survives and one that quietly stops is the failure path:

  • retries with sensible backoff
  • idempotency, so a retry does not create a second invoice
  • a queue that holds what did not go through
  • an alert that reaches a named human
  • a reconciliation report somebody actually reads

The cheapest idempotency I know is a unique constraint on the other system's ID. VoiceDash keys conversations on @@unique([agentId, platformCallId]) and upserts against it, so a webhook delivered twice lands on the same row, and the backfill job that re-reads calls from Retell looks rows up by the same key instead of inserting:

await prisma.conversation.upsert({ where: { agentId_platformCallId: { agentId: agent.id, platformCallId: call.call_id }, }, create: { /* full row */ }, update: { /* only the fields this source owns */ }, });

It also writes a raw WebhookLog row before parsing anything, so when a payload surprises me I can see what actually arrived instead of guessing.

That failure path is roughly half the engineering, and it never appears in the estimate. Someone also has to watch the exceptions after launch; monitoring voice agents in production is the same problem with phone calls attached.

When no-code is the right answer

I build custom integrations for a living and still recommend no-code tools often. If volume is modest, the mapping is simple, and a missed record costs an apology rather than money, use one.

The signs you have outgrown it:

  • Volume. Per-task pricing that was trivial at a hundred records a day is a line item at fifty thousand.
  • The logic became a program. Branches, loops and lookups in a visual editor are still software, just without tests, version control or review.
  • Error handling beyond a red icon. Retries, ordering, partial failure and reconciliation are engineering.
  • The data cannot go through a third party. Regulated or contractual data often settles it on its own.
  • Nobody can safely change it. The person who built it left, and now it runs a process nobody wants to touch.

The sensible middle: no-code at the edges for low-stakes notifications, a properly built spine.

What AI changes, and what it does not

The build side compresses a lot. Field mapping, transformation code, adapters, retry handling and the pile of tests an integration needs are the mechanical work coding agents do well under review.

Some integrations get replaced by an agent that just does the task. Careful with that framing: an agent that reads your CRM, checks availability and books an appointment is an integration project wearing new clothes. All five questions still apply, and two apply harder because nobody is clicking the button: idempotency, so a retried booking does not become two, and permissions, so it can only touch what it should. I argued the second one in Give an AI Agent Write Access One Verb at a Time.

What AI does not compress is deciding who owns which field and how fresh it must be. That needs the people who run the operation, and they are usually the critical path.

How I scope one now

  1. Read both ends' docs before estimating.
  2. Write the field map. Every field that moves, which direction, how often, and which side wins a conflict. That document is the project.
  3. Set freshness per flow.
  4. Design the failure path first. Retries, idempotency, a reject queue, one named person on the alert.
  5. Build the thinnest end-to-end slice with real data. One record type, all the way through, in production conditions.
  6. Run it next to the manual process and reconcile before anyone switches the manual step off. The diff finds the rules nobody wrote down, which is the same lesson as rewrites failing in month seven.

An integration is not a wire between two boxes. It is an agreement about who owns what, how fast it travels and what happens when it does not arrive. Make those decisions on purpose and you get a boring system that runs for years. Skip straight to the connector and you get one that works in the demo and needs someone watching it forever.

What is the worst field-ownership fight you have been dragged into? I would love to hear how it got settled.

What I Built

One Project, Many Stories turns one set of project facts into three editable drafts: a portfolio case study, a hiring summary, and a demo outline.

You supply the problem, your contribution, features, tradeoffs, and outcomes. Gemma shapes the writing for each audience. Source references stay in a separate review view, so copied and downloaded drafts are clean.

For the Side Hustle theme, I’m exploring a tool for developers and freelancers who repeatedly write about their work. It could also support a project storytelling service. Whether people would pay is still a hypothesis—I haven’t validated demand or revenue.

How I Built It

The app uses React 19, TypeScript, and Vite 7, with Vercel functions calling Google’s Gemini REST API. The server keeps the API key and uses the open-weight model gemma-4-26b-a4b-it.

Gemma writes structured, audience-specific drafts with source IDs. The app checks the response format, source references, summary length, and links to confirmed demo actions. There’s no mock fallback if generation fails.

An early version mostly rearranged the input passages. The results felt too much like the form, so I changed it to write distinct drafts while keeping the original notes available for review.

What’s tested: the latest completed checks passed 36 unit tests, 19 Chrome browser tests, and the production build. Automated generation tests use mocks. Separately, a real synthetic request completed the deployed browser → backend → Google → output flow.

What still needs work: that real response omitted a qualification about testing and measured revenue from the hiring summary. Source IDs don’t prove factual accuracy, so every draft needs human review. Local Ollama inference and generated demo narration with confirmed actions have not completed real-model testing.

Drafts are editable, and changed facts mark existing drafts stale. Evidence links aren’t fetched. Project content stays in browser memory; there are no accounts or database.

Why Does Open Innovation Matter?

I want developers to be able to inspect and adapt the model integration, prompts, and validation rules.

Gemma provides an open-weight foundation, and the app also includes a local Ollama adapter. That offers a path toward running inference under your own control, although that local path is still unverified.

The working demo sends notes to Google. I’m not claiming offline privacy, lower costs, or better quality than a closed model. The value of openness here is the ability to experiment with the writing workflow and inference setup as the project develops.
