The great British actor, Sir Patrick Stewart, passed away yesterday. While Stewart began his career as a stage actor and performed in many Shakespeare plays, he is best known for his portrayal of Captain Jean-Luc Picard, in Star Trek: The Next Generation. Thanks to his impressive performance, Stewart and the character he played became science fiction icons.

Stewart brought extraordinary depth and perception to the role. And the character is an interesting contrast to the more instinctive and aggressive, less cerebral, Captain Kirk in the original Star Trek Series. Perhaps more than any other character, Stewart's Picard epitomized Star Trek's progressive liberal values: Enlightenment rationalism, technological optimism (highlighted by his defense of the rights of the android Data), racial and ethnic equality, and commitment to peace and diplomacy.

In many ways, Picard and TNG promoted these ideas to an even greater extent than the original series. Whereas Captain Kirk was often belligerent and acted on instinct, Picard sought diplomatic solutions to conflict, and tried to avoid doing anything drastic without careful deliberation. In the original series, the Klingons and Romulans are mostly depicted as evil and irreconcilable enemies of the Federation, in TNG the Klingons have become allies and there is hope that there will be peace with the Romulans, as well. In multiple episodes, Picard takes the lead in working to maintain and improve Federation relations with these and other previously hostile alien societies.

There is much to admire in this ideology and Picard's advocacy of it. But, as I have pointed out in a 2016 article (see also this more recent piece), Star Trek's ideology has a dark side, as well: its uncritical embrace of socialism. This is also reflected in the Federation's seeming ideological homogeneity. Picard also exemplifies this negative aspect of Star Trek ideology, with his thinly veiled disdain for private enterprise, and people who seek to make a profit.

As depicted by Stewart, the Picard character also highlights another key tension in Star Trek ideology, this one relating to cultural diversity. Picard is supposed to be French; but other than his noticeable British accent, he seems little different from other human Federation characters. The "Doylist" real-world explanation for this is that Stewart himself was British, and trying to get him to speak with a French accent might have had bad results. But the "Watsonian" in-universe effect of this is to accentuate how the human characters in Star Trek have largely lost their cultural distinctiveness. French, British, and other human cultures have largely blended together, by the 24th century (when the show is set). Star Trek may champion diversity. But, culturally and politically, the human characters are mostly people who look different, but largely think alike on almost all major issues. The few dissenters are generally depicted as either villains or fools.

As  I have previously noted, this ideological homogeneity and uncritical acceptance of Federation ideology gets challenged more in later Star Trek series, beginning with Deep Space 9. DS-9 and later series depict more ideological conflict among humans, and raise serious questions about Federation ideology. But Stewart's Picard remains an iconic depiction of Federation ideals.

As always in assessing actors, we should not assume that the character necessarily reflects the views of the actor. We can and should recognize Picard's shortcomings and those of Federation ideology, without blaming Stewart for them. Indeed, his performance deserves credit for providing a valuable window into these issues.

The more recent Picard series is not as good as TNG. But I would argue the blame for that rests with the producers and their problematic plotlines, rather than Stewart.

In addition to his justly famous Star Trek role, Stewart also starred in the X-Men movie series (which I won't comment on, as I know little about it). His first significant science fiction role was playing Gurney Halleck in the 1984 movie version of Dune.

Patrick Stewart as Gurney Halleck in Dune (1984). (Universal Pictures)

 

The 1984 Dune movie is an incoherent mess, and not as good as the more recent Denis Villeneuve version (see my reviews of the latter here and here). But Stewart did a great job with his role, and really brought Halleck to life.

In sum, Sir Patrick Stewart was a great actor, and well deserves his status as an SF icon. He will be greatly missed.

The Packers can’t run block

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
(Photo by Amy Lemus/NurPhoto via Getty Images) | NurPhoto via Getty Images

Stating the complete obvious, but the Green Bay Packers’ running game has reached a level of hopelessness that seems almost statistically impossible. Coming into Thursday night, the Carolina Panthers ranked a paltry 30th in EPA allowed per rush. If there was ever going to be any sort of “get right” game for the offensive line, it was going to happen against Carolina. They had just been pummeled by Tampa Bay four days earlier, facing 48 carries and allowing a 94th percentile rushing success rate to the Buccaneers. They were tired, they were on the road, and they are a bad run defense. And it didn’t matter.

Despite starting left guard Aaron Banks returning to the lineup last night, he struggled more than anyone else. In watching all of the Packers’ 25 running back carries, I credited Banks with seven errors or lost blocks that lead to the play not working as intended (either leading to the tackle or disrupting the intended play design). That’s a rate of 28%. His fellow guard Laken Tomlinson, playing on the right side now after Banks’ return, was credited with four errors. Emergency third-string center John Williams was credited with three, including a really bad whiff near the goal line.

The running game generally requires everyone to do their job to generate positive plays, and if you always have someone messing up, you just can’t get those four, five, or six-yard gains that you need to reliably sustain drives on the ground.

48 Years Ago, a $1.98 Mask Helped Turn a $300,000 Horror Film Into a Hollywood Hit

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Forty-eight years after Halloween (1978) terrified moviegoers, one detail about John Carpenter’s breakthrough horror film remains astonishing: Michael Myers’ infamous mask cost just $1.98.

Released October 25, 1978, Halloween was made for approximately $300,000 and eventually earned more than $70 million worldwide, according to the Library of Congress. The modest independent production helped establish a formula Hollywood would spend decades trying to replicate.

Next: 40 Years Ago Today, One of the ‘Greatest Horror Remakes Ever Made’ Hit No. 1

How a $1.98 Mask Became a Horror Legend

Michael Myers’ expressionless face wasn’t the result of an expensive Hollywood effects department.

Production designer Tommy Lee Wallace purchased a Captain Kirk mask from a Hollywood costume shop and transformed it into something considerably more disturbing.

Photo by Getty Images

Carpenter recalled the process on the official Halloween website.

“It didn’t look anything like William Shatner after Tommy got through with it,” the filmmaker explained.

The crew widened the eye openings, changed the coloring, and created the pale, emotionless face that audiences still recognize.

The production’s financial limitations extended beyond its villain. Jamie Lee Curtis reportedly earned $8,000 for portraying Laurie Strode, while Nick Castle received $25 per day to play the masked killer.

John Carpenter Turned Financial Limitations Into Suspense

Photo by Getty Images

Rather than rely on elaborate effects, Carpenter built tension through carefully controlled camera movements, shadows, and an unsettling musical score.

In a Song Exploder interview, Carpenter explained why he composed the soundtrack himself.

“When we made ‘Halloween’ back in ’78, we didn’t have any money for a composer, so I always knew that I was going to be the composer.”

The director had only three days in a recording studio to complete the music.

“Everything else I was going to fake it, just improvise it,” Carpenter recalled.

That distinctive piano melody became inseparable from Michael Myers.

The Horror Movie That Changed Hollywood’s Expectations

According to the Library of Congress, Halloween inspired numerous low-budget slasher films seeking to reproduce its financial success.

Carpenter initially had considerably smaller ambitions.

“I was just trying to make a little horror movie,” he explained in an interview about the production.

Instead, the film launched a long-running franchise and helped establish Curtis as a leading horror actress.

Nearly five decades later, Halloween remains a reminder that memorable filmmaking doesn’t necessarily require a blockbuster budget. Sometimes the most frightening thing in Hollywood starts with a two-dollar mask.
