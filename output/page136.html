Work boot and apparel brand BRUNT Workwear has announced a commitment to donate $1 million of work boots to trade school students nationwide in 2027, as demand for skilled workers continues to grow. The commitment expands BRUNT’s longstanding pledge to donate 100,000 pairs of boots to trade school students by 2030.

“America needs the next generation of builders, electricians, plumbers, and skilled tradespeople more than ever,” says Eric Girouard, founder and CEO of BRUNT Workwear. “These are the people who will build our homes, maintain our infrastructure, and keep this country running. We want to do our part to make sure they’re equipped to succeed. We’ve already donated approximately 25,000 pairs of boots to students across the country, and this $1 million commitment is another step toward our goal of donating 100,000 pairs by 2030. As BRUNT grows, our commitment to the trades is only going to grow with it.” 

Taking a boots-on-the-ground approach to putting the people who wear its products at the center, BRUNT often names products after real tradespeople and incorporates direct customer feedback into product development. The gear brand shares workers’ stories through its Bucket Talk podcast and brings the trade community together through weekly BRUNT Garage events.

Through the existing trade school boot donation program, BRUNT strives to help ease an upfront expense, while welcoming them into a community that understands, supports, and celebrates this workforce. 

“BRUNT Workwear’s boot donation program is truly changing the face of vocational education in Massachusetts one pair of boots at a time,” says James Hachey, director of the Center for Career & Technical Education at Somerville High School.

“Particularly for students in high-need communities, having the proper and safe work boots can mean the difference between opportunity and exclusion. Students not having the same access to required safety gear can create an immediate barrier to hands-on career and technical learning. Donations like BRUNT Workwear’s help remove that barrier, thereby ensuring all students can safely participate, build the necessary skills, and prepare themselves for lasting careers.”

Starting January 1, 2027, trade schools nationwide can find more information and apply for work boot donations at bruntworkwear.com. Selected schools will receive boots throughout 2027.

Mortgage rates are a key factor in the home-buying process, and online shopping activity suggests consumers are paying close attention to borrowing costs. With rates around 7.5%, Zonda’s Homebuyer Outlook Clicks Index, which tracks NewHomeSource online new-home shopping activity, has fallen to its lowest level since 2022.

Of course, the relationship between rates and shopping activity is not one-to-one. The job market, broader economy, stock market, geopolitical uncertainty, and consumer confidence can all influence whether prospective buyers start searching for a home. The stability of mortgage rates can matter as well.

Still, the Homebuyer Outlook data suggests 7% may represent an important psychological threshold for consumers.

When mortgage rates first moved above 7% in 2022, online shopper activity weakened noticeably. Another significant pullback occurred as rates climbed well above 7% in late 2023. The latest data shows a similar pattern, with the Clicks Index dropping in September as mortgage rates approached 7.5%.

That relationship matters because online shopping often represents an early step in the purchase funnel. Fewer consumers browsing new-home options can translate into a smaller pool of prospects moving toward community visits and eventual purchases.

Importantly, Zonda’s Homebuyer Outlook shows that the right communities can still outperform even in a challenging market. For builders, that puts the focus beyond simply generating traffic and toward understanding which buyers remain engaged, what they can afford, and how closely a community’s location, product, and price point align with those consumers.

The insights in this article were taken from more in-depth research reports published in Zonda’s Homebuyer Outlook subscription.

Coding Dojo co-founder unveils ‘Idea to Launch,’ a 30-day program to help aspiring founders build with AI

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Richard Wang says he gets inspiration from mentoring aspiring startup founders. (Idea to Launch Photo)

Richard Wang, co-founder and former CEO of coding bootcamp Coding Dojo, is launching Idea to Launch, a 30-day program that helps would-be founders turn a startup idea into a working product using AI coding tools and hands-on engineering help.

Nearly four years after selling Coding Dojo to Perdoceo for $70 million, Wang was looking for a way to keep doing work that had a measurable impact on people’s lives. He found it in mentoring aspiring founders.

Wang thinks AI is about to do for software what smartphones did for video. Cheap cameras and cloud platforms gave rise to the influencer economy, and he believes AI will push the cost of building a product close to zero.

“We’re going to create this whole new builder economy that’s about to come in the next three to five years,” he said. “Baby boomers are gifted with cheap housing. I think Gen Z is going to be gifted with a cheap price to build.”

He sees plenty of demand. Citing a Shopify-Gallup study and a Zapier/Harris poll, Idea to Launch says 60% of Americans have a business idea but only 9% launch one. Wang said the reasons people hold back include fear of failure, not knowing how to get started, and not wanting to give up a paycheck or benefits.

Unlike accelerators such as Techstars or Y Combinator, Idea to Launch doesn’t take equity, and participants don’t have to quit their jobs. Over 10 evening sessions, founders get coaching, run customer interviews, build a product with AI tools such as Lovable, Replit and Cursor, and plan a launch. Each founder also gets 10 hours of one-on-one help from professional engineers.

Wang said people without technical backgrounds can now vibe-code 80% to 90% of a product on their own. The hard part, he said, is connecting it to other services, setting up the database correctly and making it secure.

“We’ll provide that type of knowledge with real engineering support,” he said. For engineers who have never done customer discovery or marketing, the program fills in the business side.

Security researchers have found serious vulnerabilities in apps built on vibe-coding platforms. A 2025 scan found 170 of 1,645 apps built with Lovable exposing user data, and another case this year exposed nearly 19,000 user records. Wang said the program’s engineers will make sure security holes are fixed “before they launch or before they get onto the first customer.”

The first cohort starts Jan. 11, followed by three more through April. Each is capped at 15 to 20 founders. Tuition is $3,500 for the inaugural cohort, down from the normal $5,000. Sessions will be held in person in Seattle or Bellevue, and Wang said he expects to pick a space within the next month. He is self-funding the venture.

Wang, who immigrated from China at 13 and was the first in his family to attend college, became Coding Dojo’s CEO in 2015. He left in 2017 for Trupanion and returned in 2019. The company grew to $50 million in peak annual revenue, according to Wang, and had 194 employees at the time of the sale, according to GeekWire’s earlier reporting. It operated campuses in several states before shifting online during the pandemic.

Wang sees Idea to Launch expanding to other tech hubs, then online, and eventually licensing the program globally, much as Coding Dojo grew to multiple campuses. If the first cohorts go well, he can envision a follow-up program, Launch to Traction, to help founders find more customers.

He doesn’t plan to invest in participants’ companies. Having spent years building and selling Coding Dojo, Wang said he knows how hard that road is and would rather “cheer them on from the sidelines.” He said taking VC money at Coding Dojo created pressure to grow 100% a year, something he calls “startup steroids.”

For Wang, the point isn’t to find the next unicorn. It’s to give more people a shot at building something now that AI has made it cheaper and easier.

“I think everyone should try at least once just to bring their idea to reality,” he said.

As marketers chase citations and visibility in AI search, some are repeating an old SEO mistake: optimizing for search systems rather than the people who use them.

From keyword stuffing to content farms and scaled link-building schemes, SEO has a history of tactics that delivered short-term visibility but little lasting value. Today’s fixation on AI visibility risks following the same pattern.

AI search visibility matters, but it shouldn’t come at the expense of useful, original content. Google continues to emphasize creating valuable content for users and warns against tactics such as scaled content abuse.

Rather than chasing every new AI optimization hack, focus on what has always made SEO valuable: serving users better than anyone else. That’s the foundation of human-first SEO.

The role of human expertise in AI-assisted SEO

Much of the online conversation treats AI as a threat to SEO. But AI is a tool, and its value depends on how it’s used.

Research from Graphite found that 86% of high-ranking pages in Google Search were written by humans, compared with 14% generated by AI. AI-generated pages also tended to rank lower than human-authored content.

Research from iPullRank reinforces the importance of human judgment. When researchers removed evidence from real SEO scenarios, many AI models still confidently provided a diagnosis rather than acknowledging they lacked enough information. AI can accelerate workflows, but human experts remain essential for validation and decision-making.

The pages winning in search tend to include elements AI can’t independently produce:

  • Original experiences.
  • Unique industry insights.
  • Opinions and viewpoints.
  • Proprietary data.
  • Expert analysis.
  • Real use cases.

Use AI to accelerate research, prepare briefs, organize ideas, and transcribe interviews while letting human expertise provide the substance.

4 reasons why originality matters more now

The biggest risk of AI-generated content is that it becomes indistinguishable from other content. Sameness will limit your organic growth. 

1. Algorithms are built to filter sameness out

Search engines have spent years fighting low-value, repetitive content. The pace of Google’s algorithm updates this year continues to reinforce that battle. 

Google repeatedly encourages content creators and brands to create non-commodity content that adds value through firsthand experience, expert insight, and unique perspectives. If the content you’re creating can be summarized within the current top five results, you’re already losing.

2. AI systems don’t need to cite common knowledge

Some marketers assume that publishing more content increases the chances of being cited. In reality, AI systems will blend common information without citing every source. 

If your blog posts are repeating information that already exists, with no added insight or perspective, what incentive is there for an AI system to reference it? Or include it within its training data?

Originality will increase your content’s citation potential. Repetition will do the opposite. 

3. Humans reward differentiation 

Even in an AI-first world, humans are still making buying decisions. Searchers click when they want more depth or proof, whether it’s on ChatGPT or a Google AI Overview. 

Some may disagree with me, but I don’t believe the click economy is disappearing. I think it’s changing. AI can answer straightforward questions exceptionally well, so yes, you may lose the click on those queries. But when searchers are closer to a decision, they want context, examples, and real-world application, and that’s when they click through to seek human expertise. 

Differentiation creates curiosity. Curiosity creates the click.

4. Consumers are growing skeptical of generic AI

According to Gartner, 65% of consumers believe brands produce too much AI-generated content in their marketing materials. Even more alarming, 57% say this content has made them less trusting of brand messaging overall.

When every article sounds the same and every social post follows a similar pattern, consumer trust erodes. Today’s consumers don’t want a perfectly crafted message. They want evidence, proof, and the confidence that a real person is standing behind the information. Research from the Harris Poll, presented by Quad, shows that 80% of Americans say brands that feel human and imperfect stick with them more than AI-generated perfection.

Consumers increasingly assume content is AI-generated until proven otherwise. That means that authenticity has become a real differentiator. 

So, how can we prevent repeating the same patterns? 

Introducing human-first SEO

The future of SEO is human-first. It’s what SEO should be and always has been. 

Early SEO saw the listicle craze, with authors listing their own brands as No. 1 or No. 2. Then search engines evolved, and it no longer worked. This story is repeating itself with AI search. Anthropic’s usage policy already includes policies against deceptive campaigns and artificial activity.  

Stop chasing every “new” GEO/AEO/AIO hack. History has shown brands and marketers that these may provide a short-term boost, but in the long run, they can hurt both your visibility and your brand’s reputation. 

Instead, go back to tried-and-true, human-first SEO built upon these four pillars. 

  • Experience: Provide firsthand knowledge that AI models cannot manufacture. Share your brand’s lessons learned, implementation challenges, successes, and real-world observations.
  • Opinion: Take a position. Not every piece of content needs to be controversial, but valuable content usually requires interpretation. Readers want insights that explain what happened and why it matters. 
  • Proof: Support your claims with original data, whether that’s customer stories, internal research, experiments, or other evidence. 
  • Identity: Real people build trust. Showcase your experts. Strong bios, professional histories, research, speaking engagements, and community involvement all create authority signals that neither machines nor users can easily ignore. 

Interestingly, these four pillars mirror what Google has consistently encouraged for years and now includes in its AI optimization guide. 

Human-first SEO use cases

Replace scaled AI content with expert content

Although it’s tempting, especially when you see it working for others, remember that using AI to produce hundreds of pages modeled after a competitor or other source will likely deliver short-lived results and hurt you in the long term. Why? Because competitors and other sites can create the same content just as easily. 

Meanwhile, marketing teams that invest in expert reviews, SME insights, and unique data earn visibility and keep it.

One of my B2B supplier clients and I watched a competitor publish more than 300 AI-generated how-to guides over a few months. As many of us have seen, this resulted in increased visibility for some time.

Citations began to decline, rankings dropped, and organic visibility started to disappear. Within six months, this competitor had seen its strategy rise and fall. To stop the bleeding, this competitor removed all AI-generated content. 

Instead of following their lead, our SEO strategies held their course, with blog posts that spoke directly to customers’ common issues and questions and by incorporating their team into the discovery process.

Rewrite generic manufacturer product descriptions

Many ecommerce sites still rely too heavily on descriptions provided by the manufacturers. Even when those descriptions are well written, how do you stand out if other sellers are using the same copy? 

Fortunately, human-first content can solve this problem. Add any of the following to help your product pages stand out from the rest.

  • Actual customer questions and answers.
  • Use cases.
  • Results from your own product testing.
  • Comparisons.
  • Real customer stories and reviews.

These elements and others like them provide value that generic product descriptions cannot.

Reframe your ‘how-to’ and ‘what is’ content

SaaS companies are frequent offenders of publishing large volumes of “what is” and “how-to” content that is similar to many others. The real content opportunity lies within their product teams, implementation specialists, customer success managers, and other internal teams.

Instead of a how-to article, develop content based on insights from your development and implementation teams, including common challenges they’ve faced, issues they’ve worked through with customers, and integrations that work best. Those types of insights are difficult to replicate and highly valuable to your buyers.

Each of these examples comes back to the same question: What can you say that no one else can? The checklist below can help you answer that. 

The human-first SEO checklist

Use these strategies to shift your SEO program toward a human-first approach. 

  • Run a content quality review. Ask yourself if AI could create the same content from current top-ranking pages. If yes, then you’re at risk of not appearing.
  • Create an expert content workflow. Use AI for transcription, structure, and outlines, but source the substance from your subject matter experts. 
  • Build a presence beyond your website. You and your team must be visible outside of your website to earn trust. 
  • Adjust your KPIs. Focus on share of voice, branded search, and citations. The attribution funnel has changed, and traffic source and rankings can’t be your priority.
  • Make your SMEs visible through bylines, bios, LinkedIn activity, podcasts, guest writing, conferences, and more. All of these provide authority and trust signals for your brand.
  • Invest in original data, whether that be surveys you conduct or internal datasets you compile. 

The new competitive advantage: Being human

The more AI-generated content that exists, the more valuable human expertise becomes. When everyone can create content faster, speed ceases to be a competitive advantage. 

SEO has never been about who can publish the most content. Success belongs to the brands whose content contributes something others can’t. 

  • Real experience.
  • Real opinions.
  • Real proof.
  • Real people.

Search engines are rewarding these. AI systems rely on them. Users are actively looking for them. The irony of SEO is that the more advanced AI platforms become, the more important human-first thinking is. 

That’s why SEO success shouldn’t be defined by who uses AI the most. It should be defined by who stays unmistakably human.

What happens when your company is acquired by Salesforce? Johnny Rodgers, the former principal engineer at Slack, says Slack and Salesforce had such different ideas about how work should be done that it felt like being lost in an enterprise process manual.

  1. Slack: “Put it in a channel.” Salesforce: “Open a ticket.”

  3. Slack treated friction like a bug. Salesforce treated it like a workflow.
  4. Slack wanted work to feel less bureaucratic. Salesforce had enough bureaucracy to require its own onboarding.
  5. Slack sold a new way of working. Salesforce sold into functions that already had budgets, org charts, and opinions.

  7. Slack assumed the seller used the product. Plenty of Salesforce reps apparently treated Slack as a place to send DMs.
  8. Slack culture said, “Change how the company works.” Salesforce culture said, “Which business unit owns this?”
  9. Slack expected the acquisition to change both companies. Salesforce had 80,000 employees and other ideas.
  10. Slack CEO Stewart Butterfield: If Salesforce changes Slack and Slack doesn’t change Salesforce, congratulations, you didn’t integrate a company. You digested it.

The Only Time I Felt Allowed To Be Overweight Was When I Was Pregnant

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Westend61/Westend61/Getty Images

I'd never gone 24 hours without thinking about my weight. Never a day where I didn’t feel guilty for overeating and what the scale would look like the next morning. Forever overweight and forever ashamed.

Then I got pregnant. I arrived at my new high-risk OB-GYN, the one I had to search for because my BMI alone made me automatically high risk. No preexisting medical conditions, no concerning family history… just FAT. And before I could even wrap my head around the fact that I was growing an actual human being, my new doctor told me that because of my BMI, I should only gain 11 pounds over the next nine months.

Eleven. Pounds.

I was supposed to grow a whole ass baby, and only gain 11 pounds? Historically, I could gain 11 pounds overnight if I put my mind to it! I thought pregnancy was the one time I could finally embrace getting bigger. I actually envisioned stepping on the scale at the doctor's office without fear, maybe even getting a little golf clap from my doctor for gaining a few pounds. Like the prize pig at the county fair.

I understood there were medical reasons behind the recommendation, but I couldn't help feeling defeated. Here I was, finally experiencing something I had dreamed about, and somehow, the conversation had already come back to my weight. Even when my body was literally supposed to get bigger, there was a limit.

I felt ashamed. And that shame quickly turned into anxiety. I'd spent my entire life fearing weight gain for myself, but now I was terrified it could hurt someone else: my baby. Instead of embracing my growing body, I spent my first trimester afraid of what would happen if it grew too much.

By week 12, stress and morning sickness had caused me to lose 10 pounds. I stepped on the scale at my next ultrasound, secretly excited and doing some quick math. Did that mean I could gain 21 pounds now? Did the math carry over? I was already calculating how to earn more weight gain. But when I spoke with my doctor, he didn't mention the weight loss at all. He wasn't proud, he wasn't mad. Not a peep.

And somehow, this felt like a green light to me. Like actually, maybe he isn’t THAT focused on the scale and more focused on the baby (duh). After that appointment, I felt my jaw unclench, and something in my brain shifted. I had made it through the first trimester, the baby was healthy and I had lost weight. For thatI was not punished nor rewarded. It was finally, just a number.

So I announced my pregnancy to family and friends and a sudden weight was lifted (and soon gained). Eventually, even the morning sickness let up and I reverted back to the old me. And just like that, I was back in my safe space, scrolling DoorDash.

I started eating whatever I wanted and felt no shame. I ate mozzarella sticks for a snack at 10am. Bagels before bed. I would end every meal with a “treat” of Phish Food ice cream or a couple peanut butter cups for good measure. It’s called palate cleansing.

It felt like FREEDOM. I didn't have to suck in my stomach or hide my rolls. No worrying about posing for a picture to make myself look smaller or hide my sins. I didn't have to wonder if someone was looking at what was on my plate, because honestly I DARE YOU. I didn't have to promise myself I'd “be good tomorrow.” My belly was supposed to get bigger. My clothes were supposed to stop fitting. I was supposed to gain weight. It was like a fun topic of conversation with my girlfriends and people who were already moms… “eating myself through house and home, you know how it goes!”

Well, spoiler alert, I saw their 11 pounds and ended up raising them 50. And honestly? I wasn't that scared. I'd been that weight before. I knew what it was like to live in that body. The number I was supposed to fear reaching was a number I'd already seen on my own scale. But more than that, for the first time in my life, my growing body came with an explanation.

The craziest part? My doctor didn’t bring up my weight again until after I had the baby. Sure, it still made the occasional cameo, like when I needed an anesthesia consultation before birth because of my BMI. But it made my weight felt more like a supporting character instead of the entire plot. It felt like a huge relief.

Pregnancy didn't make me comfortable gaining weight. It made my weight explainable. Because the reality is, I didn't suddenly stop being plus-size. For once in my life I felt normal…like maybe this is how regular people go about their day. Like I was in a new tier of women, one where people were going to celebrate my growing belly. My dad would make us dinner on Sundays and say “Mary gets extra, she’s eating for two!” This is what dreams were made of.

Before I knew it, nine months had flown by and I'd given birth to my baby boy. Less than 12 hours postpartum, a visiting doctor looked at my legs and asked, “Do your legs typically look like this, or are you really swollen?”

And just like that, we were back, baby!

Nope, that's just what my legs look like. I'd barely had a minute to process giving birth before I was back to feeling self-conscious about my body. Nine months of freedom, gone in one sentence.

And that was just the beginning. Soon, doctor's appointments included questions like, “So, what's your plan for weight loss?” and suggestions about GLP-1 medications. My health was otherwise good, but once again, my weight was the conversation.

I remember thinking, how am I supposed to diet on no sleep? I'm just trying to keep a newborn alive, recover from childbirth, and make it through the day. Could my body just exist for a minute without needing to be fixed?

But still, I felt the pressure to lose weight, like my body was a project that was never finished. I even caught myself feeling relieved on days when I was so busy caring for my baby that I barely had time to eat.

I've spent my entire life trying to make my body smaller, and for most of my pregnancy, I finally got to celebrate getting bigger. I wish I could say that experience changed the way I see myself forever, but the truth is, I still think about my weight every day. I still feel the guilt, still do the mental math, still wonder what the scale will say tomorrow. My brain is programmed this way. But pregnancy gave me a taste of freedom I never knew was missing. For once, I got to exist in my body without feeling like I owed anyone an explanation.

Sometimes I wonder if I will ever feel that free again. After giving birth to two beautiful boys, I don’t long for another pregnancy, though I have to admit, nine more months of not obsessing over the scale sounds pretty damn appealing.

I don’t want another excuse to love my body, I just want to stop feeling like I need one.

The two-paragraph Sept. 18 news release from Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks raised more questions than it may have intended.

The department “is seeking help from the public to locate a hybridized female rocky mountain bighorn sheep that went missing from a private alternative livestock facility near Vaughn in early September,” the release from FWP’s Region 4 office in Great Falls stated. “The bighorn ewe is described as weighing about 120 pounds and is primarily white in color with a red ear tag.”

The news release stressed wildlife officials’ concern that the ewe could come into contact with wild bighorn sheep in the vicinity. Domestic sheep are often vectors for pathogens that can fatally infect wild sheep.

What the news release didn’t disclose is that the “alternative livestock facility” in question is owned by Arthur “Jack” Schubarth, who was convicted in 2024 of violating the federal Lacey Act in his pursuit to create giant hybrid bighorn rams. Schubarth pleaded guilty to helping smuggle genetic material from Marco Polo argali sheep from Kyrgyzstan into the United States in order to clone the ram and create a “super sheep” with huge spiraling horns and resistance to the pathogens that infect North American wild sheep. His quest was enabled by Montana hunting outfitter John Lewton, who lopped off the testicles of his clients’ trophy wild bighorns, kept the gonads on ice, and later sold them to Schubarth.

The idea, Schubarth later told court officials, was to use the genetic material to incubate hybrid sheep with horns larger than any North American rams.

Lewton was sentenced last week in federal court in Billings and received a $20,000 fine and a year of probation for his part in the genetic alchemy. As a condition of his federal-court sentence, Lewton is prohibited from outfitting or guiding hunters for a full year.

‘Trailer Park Chemistry’

The notion that a “Frankenram” could be produced with a combination of cloned embryos and artificial insemination isn’t an abstraction. In 2017, Schubarth actually produced what he called the Montana Mountain King, a pure cloned Marco Polo ram birthed to a surrogate bighorn ewe. Schubarth subsequently bred offspring of the Montana Mountain King to captive bighorn ewes on his farm west of Great Falls. It’s one of these ewes that has now gone missing, according to Fish, Wildlife & Parks.

Schubarth’s genetic hermetics has both baffled and impressed wildlife investigators, who told Outdoor Life that the sort of reproductive science that Schubarth practiced is normally limited to hospitals, universities, and other science centers with state-of-the-art facilities.

“The best way to describe his operation is trailer-park chemistry,” said a source familiar with the case who spoke on background. “He’s pulled off a pretty high-level genetics program with hardware-store gear and limited resources.”

Schubarth received probation for his guilty plea to the Lacey Act violations. In his petition to the federal court for leniency, Schubarth’s attorney, Jason Holden, noted his client’s contribution to the science of wildlife propagation.

“Jack is an 81-year-old non-violent man,” Holden wrote in 2024. “His crime, the cloning of a Marco Polo Sheep from Kyrgyzstan, has ruined his life, reputation and family. Jack did something no one else could, or has ever done. On a ranch, in a barn in Montana, he created Montana Mountain King. MMK is an extraordinary animal, born of science, and from a man who, if he could rewrite history, would have left the challenge of cloning a Marco Polo to only the imagination of Michael Crichton.”

Was the Sheep Sold?

The department’s all-points-bulletin asking the public to report a lone and roaming ewe may not produce any sightings. That’s because there’s a possibility that the ewe isn’t actually on the lam. She may have been sold to an out-of-state game farm, said sources who spoke to Outdoor Life on the condition that they wouldn’t be identified based on the speculative nature of their hunches.

Schubarth, who is on probation from the 2024 federal wildlife-trafficking charges, has been charged by FWP with failure to report the escape of an animal permitted in his commercial wildlife operation, according to charging documents at the Cascade County District Court in Montana.

The “game farm” permit requires that if anything escapes the permitted facility, owners must report within 48 hours to both FWP and the Montana Department of Livestock.

“According to our records, the ewe in question reportedly escaped on Sept. 2, and our game wardens heard about it the next week,” says Dave Hagengruber, FWP’s public information officer in the Great Falls regional office. “Wardens went out to talk with Schubarth in mid-September, and he basically said, ‘Oh yeah, I meant to tell you.’”

Schubarth told wardens the ewe likely escaped either over a fence damaged by a wind storm or through a gate accidentally left open at the end of his property, beyond lines of kennels of dogs that produce puppies sold in Schubarth’s pet store in Great Falls.

Assuming the ewe did leave Schubarth’s facility, it could pose a risk to the region’s wild sheep.

“Cascade is 30 miles from his farm, and there’s a sizeable band of [wild] bighorns there,” says Hagengruber. “The Birdtail Hills are 20 to 25 miles to the west, and there are bighorns in those hills. The Rocky Mountain Front, home to Montana’s most treasured bighorn sheep herd, is 60 or 70 miles west. Fortunately, this is not a ram, so it’s less likely to roam widely, but the threat of disease transmission is a significant concern. That’s why we want to hear of any sightings. It’s Jack’s property and his responsibility to locate and return to his facility, but we can’t have this hybrid sheep potentially contacting wild bands.”

Fate of a Freak

Schubarth insists he did not sell the ewe, and suspects the fugitive sheep was killed by predators within a few miles of his facility in the Sun River Valley.

“I haven’t heard anything about her, and to my knowledge Fish and Game hasn’t heard anything for five weeks now,” Schubarth said in an interview. “With all the people out looking, we haven’t seen it and no one’s called me. I’m assuming, with her lack of experience with predators, that a coyote got her.”

Schubarth confirmed the ewe is a hybrid.

“She’s mainly bighorn and probably three or four domestic crosses from the original cross that was 50 percent bighorn,” Schubarth said. “I added some more bighorn from that original [parentage]. So they’re probably 62.5 percent bighorn. They don’t have any experience of being wild. I’m pretty sure she would have watched coyotes walk right up to her” without trying to evade them.

Schubarth further confirmed that he still has “around 45 head of sheep, is all” remaining on his 200-acre game farm. “All my sheep are sold right now and just waiting to leave. I’ve kept them back because I just want the pleasure of birthing out the lambs. It’s something I like to do, and at 83 years old, I like to see new life.”

As for the descendants of the Montana Mountain King, Schubarth said that the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service required him to destroy progeny of the cloned ram back in 2024. The federal court also required Schubarth to dispose of all hybrid bighorn sheep by Dec. 31, 2025, and to stop “from engaging in breeding activity of big game stock while on supervision,” according to sentencing documents.

“We had 190 head of the hybrid Marco/bighorn here at the ranch and none of them got pneumonia in the three years I held them while waiting for a forever home for them. All were slaughtered,” he says. “I did sell some, 15 in all. There are some down in Texas, and every so often I see on Facebook somebody advertising a Marco Polo, but there’s no way they are more than about a quarter Marco” based on genetic dilution, he said.

As for the Montana Mountain King himself, Schubarth says the ram ended up at a zoological park in upstate New York. Indeed, last summer the Rosamond Gifford Zoo in Syracuse, New York, announced the acquisition of “Tilek,” the cloned argali at the center of Schubarth’s case. The ram had been held at an Oregon wildlife rehabilitation center for over three years before being moved to the Syracuse zoo.

Measured Remorse

Schubarth says “Tilek” would be about nine years old, but observed that he has not yet expressed the most distinctive trait of the Marco Polo subspecies of argali: its spiraling horns.

“He’s basically still 54 to 57 inches,” says Schubarth. “He ought to have 75 inches of horn on each side. But this year they put him in a larger grass pen so maybe he’ll grow more. For safety’s sake, they should have castrated him so no more genetics could get out there. Maybe they did, for all I know. I’ll never see him again. I don’t ever leave my ranch.”

Schubarth said that statements from prosecutors that he was motivated to create a hybrid ram with giant horns in order to upsell to commercial shooting operations are wrong. He was more interested in cross-breeding to create a bighorn race that might be resistant to what’s often called “M. ovi,” a respiratory disease that often results in catastrophic pneumonia-caused die-offs in wild sheep.

“My thinking was that here’s this wild sheep that has evolved with domestic sheep for thousands of years,” says Schubarth of the Marco Polo, native to the high alpine plateaus of central Asia. “The feds and Fish and Game made it out that I was selling to the hunt community and only motivated to create bigger horns. In actuality, I was after the immune system, and the reality is that even after several generations, these Marco hybrids still seem to be immune to pneumonia. I think that’s the real story.”

If he regrets anything about the misadventures of the Montana Mountain King, it’s that he trusted the lab that created the clone from genetic tissue smuggled from Kyrgyzstan.

“I’ve been happy all my life except for this last foolishness about the Marco Polo,” Schubarth says. “I wouldn’t have done it had the cloning company not told me it was legal. They lied after they took $32,000 from me. That was the cost of stockpiling the tissue and making the embryos for me.”

The rest of the genetic work was a cinch, he says. The surrogate bighorn ewes, most of which Schubarth bought from zoos in Canada and Colorado, were selected for their ability to carry the hybrid lamb.

“Laparoscopic work is easy,” he says, and artificial insemination is an established livestock practice. The trick is creating a genetically manipulated zygote.

“You take the egg and suck out the product and you put the material from the Marco Polo into the egg and implant it in a ewe. A little electricity, and poof, the thing starts to grow.”

Disease Alert

Schubarth says he will be entirely out of the game-breeding business by 2027.

“Fish and Game has accomplished what they always wanted: to drive me out of business,” he says. 

Meanwhile, he says the captive-shooting industry is thriving. He cites high-fence operations in Kansas and Texas that would pay top dollar for his hybrid bighorns while also insisting that he sold to fellow breeders, not trophy hunters.

“The feds made it out that I was the center of this industry based on shooting animals in pens,” he says, before acknowledging that most of his animals probably met that fate. “Without the interest of the hunting community, these animals would be worthless and only sold for meat. So yes, the industry revolves around the harvesting of these animals by collectors.”

Fish, Wildlife & Parks is less interested in the commercial than in the pathological outcome of the case. The state has struggled to establish bighorn sheep populations in prime habitat in the Missouri River Breaks and in the mountains of southwest Montana. Bands that thrive for years are often wiped out in a single season by pneumonia epizootics that often originate in domestic sheep.

State game wardens are authorized to shoot on sight wild bighorns that contact domestic sheep. The fear is that a traveling ram could become infected by a domestic sheep, and then take that pathogen back to the wild herd.

“We have spent many years and sportsmen’s dollars to restore and transplant wild sheep into available habitat in Montana,” says FWP’s Hagengruber. “We’ve had recent restorations in the Belts and the Breaks, and disease is always a big concern, and so is the ability of this hybrid ewe to breed with either domestic or wild sheep. So we take the possibility that there may be a hybrid domestic/wild sheep running around central Montana very seriously.”

Anyone who sees a lone sheep with a red ear tag in central Montana is urged to call FWP’s Region 4 office at 406-454-5840.
