Search and rescue personnel recovered the body of a missing hiker from California’s Mount Whitney on October 25 following a nearly weeklong effort complicated by early winter storms.

In a press release posted to Facebook, the Inyo County Sheriff’s Office (ICSO) said a motel in nearby Lone Pine had notified law enforcement on October 19 that one of its guests had not checked out and had left some of their possessions behind. The office attempted to contact the missing hiker through a law enforcement messaging system, but did not receive a response.

While hotel staff knew that the guest had planned to hike, deputies were unsure of their route until the following day, when they discovered the guest’s car parked at Whitney Portal. Upon checking with the U.S. Forest Service, ICSO found that the missing hiker had secured a permit to climb Whitney on October 18.

That same day, a helicopter search found a hiker’s body along the Mountaineer’s Route at roughly 13,600 feet. Shortly after that grim discovery, an early-season storm moved into the area, buffeting the Lower 48’s highest peak with strong winds and snow and forcing authorities to postpone the recovery mission.

On October 25, after the storm cleared, a California Highway Patrol helicopter was able to carry a SAR team from Inyo County Search and Rescue to Iceberg Lake, a glacial cirque located at about 12,600 feet. From there, rescuers climbed to the hiker’s body and assisted the helicopter in hoisting it off the peak before descending back to the lake for pickup.

The Inyo County Coroner’s Office has yet to publicly identify the deceased hiker.

The harder of the two most commonly climbed lines up Whitney, the Mountaineer’s Route is 5 miles one way and requires class 3 scrambling in places.

The fatality is the second to occur on Whitney this month. In early October, Kalem Peter Kazarian, a contractor who had appeared in a pilot for HGTV, was descending from Whitney’s summit when he fell and suffered fatal injuries. In January, another climber passed away on the “Final 400” section of the Mountaineer’s Route after continuing up the mountain solo while their partner turned back.

Christopher Sale Wren, 89, February 15

Christopher Sale Wren served as The New York Times bureau chief in Moscow, Cairo, Beijing, Ottawa, and Johannesburg. His creative life was deeply intertwined with a respect for nature, big expeditions and the many people he met on assignment. He passed at his home in Vermont on February 15, 2026, at age 89.

Wren began climbing as a student at Dartmouth, where he sometimes felt out of place, according to his daughter, Celia. A scholarship student whose parents were actors, he had little interest in fraternity life. In the outdoors, he found a bigger sense of kinship. He learned to climb with the college’s mountaineering club, practicing on campus at Bartlett Tower and on the nearby cliffs of Orford and Owl’s Head. According to Samuel C. Silverstein’s memorial in The American Alpine Journal, he once ended a public-speaking assignment on mountaineering by rappelling out of the classroom’s third-floor window.

In 1962, Wren joined the six-man team that made the first ascent of Denali’s Southeast Spur (Alaska grade 4, 65-degree ice). The expedition became the subject of his first major article for Look magazine, merging his climbing with his reporting into a career he chased around the world.

Twelve years later, while stationed in Moscow for the Times, he joined an American expedition to the Pamirs. On Lenin Peak (23,406ft), Wren and fellow climbers Jock Glidden and Allen Steck encountered members of a Soviet women’s team who had died in a storm. His reporting brought their tragedy to the Times front page, breaking the news of deaths Soviet authorities had kept secret.

For Jed Williamson, who was also on the Pamirs expedition, Wren’s contribution to mountaineering extended to his ability to tell its stories. “What he really brought to the mountaineering scene was his ability to write about it and put it out there,” Williamson said. “He had a really good sense of humor. He could turn a situation that one might not think was so humorous into one that was humorous.”

Even when faced with tragedy, Wren found a way to bring levity to dangerous moments. Celia remembered one story her father told about provisioning the Pamirs expedition. The American climbers wanted peanut butter, which he couldn’t find in Soviet shops. Instead, he packed tins of caviar for protein. “His teammates thought it was disgusting,” she remembers. “But he was a Renaissance man with an incredible sense of humor.”

Wren played guitar and banjo and wrote songs, two of which Johnny Cash recorded: Jesus Was a Carpenter and Gospel Road. “It was a fun thing that he did, and he was good at it,” Williamson said. “But he was never a chest pounder about that kind of thing.”

At home, Wren’s appetite for being outside became part of family life. During the family’s years in the Soviet Union, Celia remembered her father heading out on skis, pulling her and her brother behind him on a sled. He penned numerous books, from a biography of Johnny Cash to Walking to Vermont, an account of his roughly 400-mile walk home from Manhattan after retiring from The New York Times.

Williamson remembers that despite the breadth of his traveling and range of his accomplishments, Wren was down-to-earth.  “He wasn’t aloof or standoffish or anything like that,” he said. “He was happy to share.”

Late into retirement, he continued to ski and ride his mountain bike around the nearby lake. Celia remembered his ability to keep moving ahead. When recalling difficult experiences, he would often say, “Well, you know, that’s all behind us now.”

Most drivers know this experience. They’re en route somewhere when their smartphone map app starts pushing back their ETA. Soon, brake lights appear ahead as traffic slows to a crawl.

But how did the navigation app know about the traffic before the driver encountered it? Popular navigation apps like Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze use real-time location data from drivers’ phones to detect slowdowns and estimate traffic conditions.

Learn what’s happening and why the estimates aren’t always right.

How Do Smartphone Map Apps Detect Traffic and Predict Delays?

Map apps combine location data from many phones to understand traffic conditions on the road. Google explains that it uses combined location data for this, and Waze says drivers share real-time information just by driving with the app open. When enough drivers slow down in the same area, the app can show a backup on the map.

Location information plays a big role in this process, although apps also take steps to protect users’ privacy. Apple Maps’s privacy statement reads, “Use of your location is required for most Maps features to function optimally. Because your location can give away your identity, we convert precise locations to less-exact locations within 24 hours. When you use Maps for navigation, we convert precise locations from the beginning of your route to a less exact area.”

Apps also use past traffic information to predict delays before drivers encounter them. “To predict what traffic will look like in the near future, Google Maps analyzes historical traffic patterns for roads over time,” Google’s wevsite states.

These predictions help apps estimate arrival times and suggest alternate routes when traffic starts building.

“Our predictive traffic models are also a key part of how Google Maps determines driving routes,” the Google website continues. “If we predict that traffic is likely to become heavy in one direction, we’ll automatically find you a lower-traffic alternative. We also look at a number of other factors, like road quality. Is the road paved or unpaved, or covered in gravel, dirt or mud?”

Drivers can also report problems such as accidents and hazards through Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze, though the options vary by app and region. These reports help explain why traffic has slowed down. Google says it also uses data from local governments, which can flag road restrictions such as construction.

The colored lines on the map show where traffic is building. In most navigational systems, green means no traffic delays, orange means medium traffic, and red means delays, with darker red indicating slower speeds.

How Accurate Are Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze Traffic Updates?

Traffic updates are generally useful, but they aren’t always accurate. Apple, for example, says it displays incident markers only when it has a high level of confidence in the reports it receives, so a new incident may take time to appear. Coverage also varies depending on how much information the apps receive. On less-traveled roads, fewer drivers may contribute location data, making it harder for apps to detect slowdowns in real time.

Arrival times are estimates, too. Google says its predictions combine historical patterns with live conditions, but it also notes that historical patterns aren’t always reliable indicators of what the road will look like. Conditions can also change unexpectedly because of events like snowstorms.

Map apps can help drivers avoid delays, but their estimates aren’t guaranteed. Drivers still need to follow road signs and pay attention to the conditions around them.

About our reporting: Climbing reporter Holly Chen interviewed three free soloists and one additional speed climber in Boulder, researched fatalities in Accidents in North American Climbing, and pulled from her own experience free soloing the Second Flatiron to answer questions about the world of competitive free soloing. 

I stood waiting in front of a health food restaurant in south Boulder, Colorado. The Flatirons rose high above the building, their slabs of red sandstone jutting out of the mountainside like the spines of a prehistoric monster.

On September 3, 32-year-old climber Michael Reese broke the First Flatiron’s fastest known time (FKT) from the dirt at its base to the summit anchor, with no rope. He climbed the Direct Route (5.6,R; 1,000ft) of the East Face in 7 minutes 51 seconds, breaking what he believed would be the last minute barrier of this generation. The previous record was 8 minutes 33 seconds, set by Joe Kennedy in 2022.

Which one is Michael Reese? Everywhere I looked at the restaurant, less than a 10 minute drive from the Chautauqua trailhead, I saw La Sportiva and Scarpa running shoes, Salomon running vests, and folded ultralight running poles sticking out of ultralight backpacks. Any of these people could have just come off the climb.

The ease was the first thing I noticed about Reese. For someone who holds multiple FKTs in the Flatirons and one on Yellow Spur (5.8; 550ft) in Eldorado Canyon, I expected him to be cocky, confident, maybe a little elitist; he was anything but. Reese smiled easily, laughed easily, and spoke candidly, unperturbed by the recorder sitting between us. He was leaving for Yosemite the following day, and we exchanged stories of our experiences climbing in the Valley. When I joked about my personal best up the First Flatiron–somewhere shy of an hour, although I did not officially keep time–he congratulated me genuinely.

“You can do it,” Reese urged. “Get some racing shoes. Do some warmups … You’ll be going faster.”

As climbing has grown, it has increasingly legendized free soloing and going fast—but we’ve rarely seen these two disciplines combined and going without a rope. The Oscar-winning film Free Solo (2017) made the term so mainstream that it recently appeared in a Taylor Swift song, and speed climbing records have made headlines again and again. But these fantastical feats of risk and speed are usually in the hands of highly experienced climbers, such as Alex Honnold, the spectacular pendulum of Dean Potter, or Yosemite Search and Rescue members. Additionally, most of these races happen on cliffs that the average person can’t easily ascend, such as El Capitan.

Reese is a different kind of figure. A Boulder local, he is not sponsored, and not a professional. Reese started climbing at the University of Colorado (CU) Boulder in 2014. The first time he went up the First Flatiron, he said to himself, “If I get off this [alive], I’m never climbing again.” And the First Flatiron is far from inaccessible; with the base a 1.3-mile hike from the trailhead, for many people, the barrier to entry is only a pair of shoes.

The new FKT leaves me with an uneasy mix of admiration and doubt. A person’s risk is not entirely his own when he takes it in a busy place like Chautauqua Park, where hikers, runners, picnickers, and climbers share the same space. So is competitive free soloing on low fifth-class terrain something to celebrate or a toxic addiction to discourage? A fatal fall is always possible, and the barrier to entry is so low. In fact, anyone writing about it, including myself, is working on blurry ground where reporting and the glorification of risk are hard to tell apart. Does this recognition create a feedback loop that incentivizes more to partake in the risky sport?

To understand how competitive free soloing actually operates, I had to go back to the people who have been going fast on this rock for decades before Reese.

A speedy history

The history of speed records in the Flatirons runs through a small, unusual club called The Satan’s Minions Scrambling Club, which Bill Wright started in 2001. Three of its members told me they don’t “race” each other. Instead, they have “personal time trials,” to avoid the ire of the Parks & Open Space regulations, which prohibit races.

The club keeps books. Stefan Griebel, a longtime member, whom a mutual friend described to me as someone who holds “every record under the sun,” has a meticulously kept log book detailing every FKT on a Flatiron dating back to the 1980s. Today, members’ times are verified by Strava entries.

Griebel met Wright in 2003 and joined the club’s annual Flatiron scrambles, which have now run for about 22 years. Each one starts at a trailhead, free solos a Flatiron, and ends back in the parking lot. Buzz Burrell, a trail-running legend then 52, reset the trailhead-to-trailhead mark at 35 minutes and change. Griebel, who was half Burrell’s age at the time, couldn’t keep pace.

“[Buzz] just dropped every single person that day,” Griebel reminisced.

A year later, trail runner Dave Mackey took Burell’s trailhead-to-trailhead FKT down to 33:19. But being a trail runner, Griebel knew he would have the upper hand on rock. In 2010, Griebel closed the gap, and took the record to 33:05. The record changed hands to Mathias Messner in 2015 and Kyle Richardson in 2019.

In the spring of 2020, Reese returned to Boulder from a biotech master’s program at California Polytechnic State University. The COVID pandemic had canceled the triathlon nationals he’d spent months training for, and he had a season of fitness and nothing to spend it on. He decided to see how fast he could go on the Flatirons. It began as a text-message rivalry with Joe Mitchem, a friend from CU’s cross-country team, over Freeway (5.0; 600ft) on the Second Flatiron. Reese didn’t have Strava and didn’t know the records existed until the two of them realized they’d gone under the standing Freeway mark of about 6:20. That fall, he entered the club’s time trials and finished second.

Griebel remembers meeting Reese on the First Flatiron. The two soloed to the summit and trotted down together. “He can run uphill like nobody I’ve ever seen,” Griebel said.

The base-to-summit FKT, the one Reese prefers, was still Griebel’s in 2020, at 9:25. Reese told me the time stunned him. That summer, Reese broke it by seconds. Then Joe Kennedy, a California athlete, broke that in 2022 to 8:33. That sent Reese into harder training than he’d done in 2020 or 2021, and this September brought the record down to 7:51.

The club looks different today. It is still heavily male dominated; Griebel estimates eight men for every one woman participant. And membership is less casual. “We have some entrance requirements now,” Griebel said. “We don’t want to invite people into the club that aren’t okay with the risk and that are also very solid, obviously, on the rock.”

What the club shows is a culture more wary than toxic. It vets its members, keeps careful books, and wants to stay on good terms with the land managers. It also shows a feedback loop; each record pulled in the next challenger. Reese told me every broken record “ingrains it more as something to go for.” He felt inspired when he learned of Griebel’s record. Camaraderie and friendly competition keep people incentivized and returning. But this wariness only goes so far; while the Satan’s Minions can vet their members, they can’t actually stop others from trying competitive free soloing, too.

The death risk is never zero

The First Flatiron in Boulder, Colorado (Photo: Mia Anderson via Unsplash)

Reese said he has now climbed the First Flatiron 800 times. It taught him that the risk “is never zero,” but he believes it to be smaller than most people imagine.

Even inside the scene, no one agrees on what speed does to the odds of surviving a free solo. Griebel says that when he’s pushing hard, heart pounding and moving upward, he’s “less likely to make a mistake.” Kate Kelleghan, who holds the women’s Naked Edge (5.11b; 460ft) record with Laura Pineau, says free soloing only makes up about one percent of her climbing. “This is super high risk and low reward,” she told me. “I’ve always felt way safer and stronger on a rope.” John Alcorn, who holds the Diamond speed record, free soloing Casual Route (5.10a; 900ft), says going fast piles more risk onto something already dangerous. “You get one shot,” he told me, “and if you blow it, you’re dead.”

In his trip report, Alcorn wrote, “I accept the position that climbing without a rope is a pretty dumb thing to do, and I believe that it shouldn’t be glorified, normalized, or encouraged in any way.” When a friend’s wife called it “incredibly stupid” over dinner, he agreed.

Alcorn said the FKTs are “extremely competitive. We’re essentially all competing with each other. But all these guys are my friends.” He admitted that if someone took his Diamond time by a minute or two, “Oh, I’d be pissed.” Then he caught himself: “That’s just pure ego … one of the worst reasons to climb without a rope.”

According to Accidents in North American Climbing, there have been six fatalities in the Flatirons since 2016. All of them involved free soloists, although none of them involved speed climbers. That number puts the danger in soloing, not the speed.

Low fifth-class terrain pulls in runners, a demographic far larger than climbing. “The accessible, low-angle slabs of the Flatirons frequently lure inexperienced or ill-prepared scramblers,” wrote the ANAC editors in 2017.

Reese has noticed more crossover of hybrid athletes; people coming from a very high level running background. Alcorn saw the same shift from the trail running side: As running records multiply, people are “diversifying what counts as a goal,” and he finds it hard to say where running and scrambling stop and climbing begins. Griebel says the overlap between strong runners and people comfortable soloing is thin, and that overlap is the only reason he has ever held a record.

This summer Reese found a 20-year-old about 100 feet up at dusk, shaking, with no headlamp, his feet slipping. Reese guided him down a gully. By the time they reached the ground, it was too dark to see his own outstretched hand.

I have felt that same dread myself. In the summer of 2025, I took out-of-town friends up on a free solo of the second Flatiron. Near the top, three college-aged men were climbing without a rope, one in climbing shoes and two in running shoes. The leader, about fifteen feet above me, was shaking badly. I waved my friends to the left, afraid that if he fell, we would be in his path.

But inexperienced climbers aren’t always the ones to watch. In 2020, a man fell to his death on Freeway, even after he reportedly had climbed it “hundreds of times.”

The scrambling club’s precautions don’t reach the people who get in trouble because those people aren’t in the club. And just because one is solid on rock doesn’t mean they’ll never fall. Its members can be practiced and clear-eyed about the risk, but the rock belongs to everyone. “They don’t do it because they see somebody going fast,” said Griebel, referring to inexperienced scramblers. “They just see somebody soloing.”

Who’s watching?

The stereotype is that this is a content game. Climbers like Lincoln Knowles, who posts his free solos to a large and young following, make the impression that free soloing is about social media fame and monetization.

The record-holders I talked to disagree. Reese began timing himself in 2020 without Strava, unaware the records existed. “If there was no social media, no recognition of any kind, would you still do it?” I asked Reese. The answer was an unequivocal yes. “I’m more interested in doing the actual thing than the broadcasting of what I’ve done,” Reese said.

Alcorn never posted his Diamond record, a choice he called intentional. “I just don’t even want ego in the loop,” he said. Griebel’s Instagram account is private except to close friends. Reese’s posts reads more like a logbook than a boast.

Still, the pull of recognition isn’t zero. Reese allows that recognition probably motivates him now that it exists, but he still climbs for himself and not a like button. Alcorn admits that without a culture of records he probably wouldn’t have tried the Diamond record. Kelleghan sits in the middle. She has a following and has been paid, including for a watch ad, but says she has never climbed something just to post it.

Whatever these climbers are after, audiences both on and offline take their own lessons from the best in the sport.

On the Flatirons, free soloing and speed have become synonymous; on the Naked Edge, a short drive south, they still sit apart, for now. Some Boulder climbers are free soloing the Naked Edge already, Kelleghan said, and she knows at least one who has soloed it quickly, though not for a record. This climber, who Kelleghan refuses to name, believes the speed record will eventually belong to a free soloist. Kelleghan disagrees. A free soloist can’t push as hard, she argued, because “if you’re simul climbing, if you fall, it would be unpleasant, but you wouldn’t die.”

The people I spoke with showed that records have a way of recruiting. If someone does set a free solo speed record on the Naked Edge, will it stay an outlier, or become the standard, the way that free solo ascents became the standard on the Flatirons? Kelleghan is betting against it.

So for most people who hold these records, it isn’t a content game. But a motive doesn’t have to be public or monetized to be contagious. Each time a record falls, it tells the next climber what is possible. The risk the last person accepted becomes the new baseline.

The weight of a record

For all the attention these records draw, free soloing mostly regulates itself. Most people look at the First Flatiron and never think of climbing it, let alone without a rope. Alcorn points to an innate fear of falling “baked into us” and doesn’t expect the number of people doing this to explode just because a few records have been broken.

Some people chase records, but does that make this a toxic culture? From what I saw, it doesn’t. The record-holders I met were generous rivals, wary of their own egos, more interested in doing the thing than in being seen doing it. But “not toxic” isn’t the same as “not influential.” We are not solitary creatures, we have family and friends who love us, so a risk taken is never entirely one’s own.

Reese was as easy at the end of our conversation as at the start. By the end of our meal, I had decided we were friends, so as we said goodbye, an uneasy feeling settled in my stomach.

Walking to my car, I looked up at the Flatirons. A friend of mine, Bailee Mulholland, still holds the female trailhead-to-trailhead speed record on the First. She belonged to the same scrambling club Reese does now. In 2023, she died free soloing a 5.4 route on Ypsilon Mountain (13,520ft) a few hours north. I remembered Mulholland’s unwavering bubbly personality; she smiled easily and laughed easily, too.

I don’t think the scene she came from is toxic. But I know what losing her did to the people who loved her. The risk may belong to the one who takes it. The grief never does.
