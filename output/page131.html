Beloved actor Patrick Stewart has died at the age of 86. The veteran of stage and screen was best known for his many years as Star Trek's Jean-Luc Picard and X-Men's Charles Xavier, though he also had notable roles in video games, including The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion and Castlevania: Lords of Shadow.

"We announce with grew sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles, in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny, following a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer's Disease and Lewy Body Dimentia" a statement on his official Instagram reads. "He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter, and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all. We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace."

Stewart's role as Picard made him a household name, and following the conclusion of Star Trek: The Next Generation, he reprised the role in several Star Trek films as well as the series Star Trek: Picard.

Though best known for his live-action roles like Picard and Xavier, Stewart was also known for his iconic voice. Alongside the aforementioned games like Oblivion and Castlevania: Lords of Shadow (and its sequel), Stewart had voice roles in everything from American Dad to The Prince of Egypt. He also narrated the Ted movies, with his friend Ian McKellen taking over the role for the recent Ted series on Peacock.

A while ago, I swapped out my smartphone for a flip phone. I was getting tired of how distracted I was—doomscrolling, checking my emails—and I wanted to simplify things. If, however, you’re part of the small subset of hikers like me, who find they’re happier when their phone is off and in their pack, or if they’re just carrying a flip phone, you might give the Life Camera a try. No, the resolution isn’t anything to write home about, but it can capture your life on the trail in adequate detail, and if you don’t mind that retro vibe, it’s kind of fun. I’ll be carrying one on my next hike.

Sony fully unveils its massive, $80,000 Rialto 65 cinema sensor

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The things I’d do for a chance to shoot with this…

Sony

Earlier this year, Sony announced it was working on the Rialto 65, a module for the Venice 2 cinema camera that replaces the camera’s standard full-frame (840mm²) sensor with a massive 53.75 × 35.83mm (1926mm²) one that offers around 2.2 times the imaging area. Now, the company has fully revealed its specifications and price: a cool $80,000 (which, mind you, doesn’t include the camera body or a way to actually mount a lens onto the sensor).

When paired with a Venice 2, the Rialto 65 will be able to record 9.6K 3:2/1.5:1 open gate footage at up to 30p and 9.6K​ 2.20:1 footage at up to 50p, though the fastest framerates aren’t available in the highest-end Raw X-OCN XT format. Sony says it’s working on a few other readout modes: 9.6K 17:9 at up to 40p, 6.4K​ 3:2 at up to 60p from a full-frame-sized crop of the sensor, and 4.3K​ 17:9 at up to 100p from an APS-C crop, which will be added via software update.

Sony

Of course, if you’re buying a sensor this big you probably want to use all of it. While its diagonal is just short of actually measuring 65mm, this class of format has a rich history in filmmaking, which we go into in our article covering the original announcement. While it’s still quite a bit smaller than the full 70mm IMAX film that auteurs like Christopher Nolan use, Sony says it’s the “industry’s largest image sensor” in the digital realm.

Cinematographers will have to contend with finding lenses with large enough image circles to cover the sensor, and those are likely to be so varied and relatively esoteric that Sony hasn’t even bothered to include a lens mount with the Rialto 65 sensor block. Instead, you’ll have to get a third-party one that matches the lenses you want to use.

Sony

One interesting trick of the Rialto 65 is that it can be used directly attached to the camera, or extended away from the body, letting cinematographers fit it in smaller spaces. It comes with a 4.5m (14.8′) cable for this purpose, though Sony is selling a longer 12m (39.4′) one if you really need to get the imaging package far away from the body. That, however, won’t come cheap either, coming in at $9000.

Sony is also making a set of drop-in ND filters for the system, which have electronic contacts to automatically tell the camera body what filter is being used.

Alongside the announcement, Sony has partnered with two filmmakers to make shorts that show off the Rialto 65’s capabilities. While they’re definitely impressive, it’s also clear watching them that YouTube’s compression absolutely is not doing this footage justice, even if you set it to 4K.

While the price is jaw-dropping, especially since you then need to attach it to a roughly $60-70,000 Venice 2 system, it’s also not particularly surprising. The Rialto 65 is destined for the sets of the highest-end Hollywood productions, and the bulk of them are likely to be purchased by rental houses, rather than individual cinematographers. It’s also worth noting that it’s competing with the likes of the Arri Alexa 65, which is rental-only.

Sony says it’s tentatively planning to launch the camera in the first half of 2027.

Frank DeVito, president and Chief Operating Officer, XR Pay

Brands and agencies are spending more money than ever on featuring famous faces in advertising, with creators commanding bigger roles and paydays. But the creator economy’s next challenge isn’t finding new talent or making more content. It’s simplifying the business of paying creators.

Celebrity pay in advertising is at an all-time high, with payments in the United States projected to reach $2.06 billion in 2026, up nearly 70% over the last five or six years, according to XR Extreme Reach data. While creator advertising has become big business, the systems used to pay the people behind it haven’t kept up. The industry has scaled along with the dollars; the payment infrastructure hasn’t.

Consumer Appetite for Personal AI Goes Mainstream

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


This article is brought to you by Qualcomm Technologies.

For many consumers, much of the promise and appeal of AI is personal. Consumers are looking past generic tools and want to know how these technologies will help them in their daily lives. These trends coincide with the advent of new classes of wearable devices that, in addition to being able to leverage powerful frontier models, feature built-in intelligence that understand the context of a user. Where are they? What are they seeing and hearing? What are their habits, their preferences, their schedules?

It’s an astonishing pivot, from passive accessories that served mostly to send users smartphone notifications to proactive, intelligent companions. The smartphone is no longer the center of that universe; though it’s still incredibly important, in this personal AI paradigm, the AI is the UI. And it shifts the focus toward you, the user.

These new technologies are exciting. But what do consumers really want?

A Personal AI report, commissioned by Qualcomm Technologies, built on a survey of more than 12,000 people across key geographies worldwide (the U.S., U.K., Germany, India, Brazil, and China) focused on the rise of personal AI in intelligent wearables looks deeply at these developments.

The smartphone is no longer the center of that universe; though it’s still incredibly important, in this personal AI paradigm, the AI is the UI.

Survey respondents are less interested in single devices and are responding more to the idea of connected ecosystems. As people expand their roster of devices, it only makes sense. Instead of relying entirely on a smartphone and a laptop, respondents also have connected technology in their cars, as well as intelligent wearables like smartwatches, smart glasses, hearables, and other wearables like rings, pins, and pendants. They want those devices to work in concert, and the way to do that is to decentralize any single one of them. Instead, the AI agent that knows the user and works across all of those devices becomes the unifying factor.

Consider a typical workday: A user’s watch detects it is time to leave for a meeting and, with permission, their AI agent factors in calendar details, real-time traffic and battery status across their devices. In the car, it suggests the best route and briefs them on their meeting agenda. As they arrive, smart glasses surface a discreet reminder, such as the meeting location or the name of a new contact, while the phone stays in their pocket. No one device has to do everything; each plays the role it is best suited for, coordinated by a personal AI agent that understands the user’s context and acts only at the user’s direction.

With this in mind, simply adding more hardware to the mix isn’t sufficient. Even though respondents will be open to new form factors, they’ll demand more from their ecosystem of devices. They’ll be sensitive to points of friction and needless complexity, and they’ll require each new device to bring something new to the table — “incremental intelligence, convenience, or contextual awareness that their existing devices cannot deliver on their own what the report,” reads the report.

The usage trends are up. The report found that those who own wearables are already using AI frequently (60 percent use it at least weekly), which indicates that it’s becoming less of a fun experiment for early adopters and more of a critical mass habit.

The “always-on” nature of intelligent wearables appears to be winning respondents, too. The report found the following statistics on average continuous wear among users:

  • True wireless earbuds – 3 hours
  • Wireless headphones – 3 hours
  • Smart glasses – 5 hours
  • Audio glasses – 6 hours
  • Smartwatches – 9 hours
  • Smart rings, necklaces and pendants – 8 hours

Consumers want AI that is personal and understands their context — they’re looking for smooth, intuitive experiences that are marked by privacy, security, and user control.

Consumer Trust Is Hard-Won

But for all the excitement and engagement around intelligent wearables, there’s a gating factor: trust. As use cases for intelligent wearables expands to more personal and persistent experiences, trust is a necessity for Personal AI.

The top reported issues around adoption are privacy, security, and user control. The data points to a certain amount of caution around deeply personal use cases, like voice, health, and location, as almost 60 percent of respondents want to keep data from their wearables private, or at least insist on gaining approval before it gets shared.

In other words, even though premium features like high-quality audio, perception sensing, battery life, and comfort whet consumer appetites (and agentic AI capabilities drive upgrade intent), true adoption will come only with consumer trust. They want security, privacy, transparency and control.

Notably, the report found that respondents expressed more interest in adopting intelligent wearables when Snapdragon was introduced as the enabling platform. This reinforces the value of having an established, trusted technology foundation in the mix.

The Industry Remains Fluid

The report shows that consumer appetite for AI is high, with 60-70 percent of those surveyed expressing interest in advanced AI as they consider a new device. That’s a constant. But remaining purchase drivers differed between device categories.

Audio playback quality is the most influential purchase driver for true wireless earbuds and headphones. For smartwatches, it’s display quality and responsiveness. Respondents are less concerned about technical specifications than aesthetics like materials and finish when it comes to devices like smart rings and pendants. And for smart glasses, it’s all about the type of control, whether it’s voice, gesture, or touch.

The Rise of Personal AI in Intelligent Wearables

Artificial intelligence is entering a new phase, one that is deeply personal, continuously present, and seamlessly embedded into the devices we wear every day. This evolution, which we define as “Personal AI”, represents a shift away from generic, cloud-only intelligence toward AI that understands individuals in context: their habits, preferences, environments, and intent, in real time.

Alongside these purchase drivers is another key finding: Consumer appetite for Personal AI agents has gone mainstream. The report says that 70 percent of respondents “express strong interest in an AI assistant that can interact naturally through voice and seamlessly manage devices, apps, and everyday tasks.” With each individual as the center of their own ecosystem, voice becomes the most critical, natural method for interacting with agents.

The Opportunity

The industry for intelligent wearables is exciting. It’s clear that consumer demand for Personal AI is high, and this report dives deeply into the specifics of what respondents want out of it. They want AI that is personal and understands their context, and they want it to be proactive and helpful instead of merely reactive. They’re looking for smooth, intuitive experiences that are marked by privacy, security, and user control.

Intelligent wearables comprise much of the hardware of this “ecosystem of you.” Consumers are interested in ecosystems instead of single devices, and they’re looking for agentic AI capabilities that understand and act on their behalf.

Yet so much of the industry remains undefined. OEMs and technology makers have an enormous opportunity right now to innovate and iterate on the strong technological foundations that platform makers are building. And the roadmap for what consumers want is clear.

Why Those Flashy Halloween Contacts Are a Bad Idea

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We may earn a commission from links on this page.

Contacts with cat pupils or neon colors can be fun, but are you sure you want to put a piece of plastic from a costume store into your eye? Experts, like those at the American Academy of Ophthalmology, warn against it. These non-prescription contacts are technically illegal in the U.S. (even though plenty of companies sell them anyway). The CDC warns that the risks include "eye sores and vision loss."

Why costume contacts often aren't safe

Cheap Halloween contacts tend to be one-size-fits-all, but the AAO points out that contacts should never be one-size-fits-all. A contact that is too large or too small for your eye can cause corneal abrasions, corneal ulcers, and can result in infections like keratitis. Infections can, in severe cases, lead to blindness. A 2017 report from the FDA found bacterial contamination in 27% of costume contacts.

If you do wear contacts (Halloween or otherwise), the AAO recommends keeping an eye out for symptoms like redness, swelling, pain, or excessive discharge. Seek care as soon as you suspect an infection. Your eyes are more sensitive to infections than other parts of the body, so be cautious.

How to find safe costume contacts

Legally, contacts require a prescription, so if those colored contacts you're eyeing can be bought without showing a doctor’s prescription, they’re not legit. The real thing can be expensive, though: I found prices of $40 and up from lens.com, Discount Contacts, and Wicked Eyez.

These websites all let you order online, but you’ll have to provide your prescription information before you can check out. Fortunately, the Federal Trade Commission has ruled that providers must give you your contact prescription after a fitting so that you can take the prescription elsewhere. And they cannot charge you extra for this.

Once you’ve got the lenses, you have to know how to take care of them. If you wear contacts every day, you already know the drill. But if you’re just picking up a pair to use with a costume, here are the basics:

  • Wash your hands before handling the lenses.

  • Store them in contact lens solution, and keep the lenses and their case clean.

  • Don’t sleep or swim with them in.

  • And for Pete’s sake, don’t share them with your friends!

Just remember, the bloodshot look stops being cool after you take your zombie costume off.

WoW: Forever Won’t Have Dead Servers

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

World of Warcraft: Forever will avoid repeating WoW Classic's mistakes and will have tools to prevent "dead" or "unsustainable" server rulesets, Blizzard said.

In the past, various WoW Classic servers became "dead" over time, as players either stopped playing or transferred to other servers. Those massive drops in population had major ramifications for players left on the server being unable to find groups for raids or dungeons, as well as for the server's economy and auction house. In some extreme cases, Blizzard gave away free character transfers so players could leave their "dead" servers behind.

While Forever is going a different route with what are essentially mega servers tailored around specific rulesets, some players might still have anxiety when it comes to picking a ruleset that will be a healthy place to play long-term. 

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BVyhnixdPQg

In the latest episode of the WoW: Forever podcast, Blizzard said it has tools it can use if a ruleset's population becomes "unsustainable." Senior game designer Josh Greenfield gave the example of Forever's separate RP and (newly added) RP-PvP server rulesets. He said there were initially concerns about splitting up the RP community, fearing that one or both of the rulesets wouldn't be able to stand the test of time. He said Blizzard now believes "it will be fine," but does have options should the worst happen. 

"If someday down the line, if something happens and one or both of those populations can't quite sustain themselves, we have options if we do need to say link up their auction houses or allow them to group for instanced content," Greenfield said.

Greenfield said it's not something Blizzard will jump to unless absolutely necessary and that it's committed to its current separate-ruleset path. Blizzard debated the idea of rulesets during Forever's development and discussed letting players from different rulesets group together for instanced content or combining auction houses.

However, it landed on wanting to maintain distinct communities, in part because of the differences in server economies when it comes to PvE and PvP rulesets, with Greenfield using an example of how it's easier to farm for gold and items on a PvE server. That in turn opened up debates on whether these cross-ruleset players would be able to trade items with one another and the need for other restrictions.

"Ultimately, these are different mindsets, these are different rulesets," Greenfield said the team decided. "Let's keep them separate for now. That is a part of original WoW that we don't want to just throw out completely. But I think the tech is set up in such a way that if we ever did really feel like we needed to make a change, we could do so."

He said those options are merely "levers we can pull if we need to" and that it's important for Blizzard to "stay flexible." That's good news, and will likely give players worried about choosing the wrong ruleset some relief.
