This bike will stop your kid’s YouTube video if they’re not pedaling

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Kids watch a lot of YouTube. And that generally means a lot of sedentary, butt-in-seat time.

But what if they had to pedal to keep the content coming?

That’s the question entrepreneur couple Arnav Dalmia and Shivani Jain are asking with their new company/product Tovie.

Described as a “sensory-friendly bike that helps kids use movement to self-regulate,” Tovie is a peddling machine where kids sit on whatever chair/couch/etc is comfortable and get their feet going. (If this sounds familiar: Yes, it’s basically like those under-desk peddlers for office workers. More on that later.)

The reason Tovie piqued our interest is Dalmia and Jain’s proprietary “Pedal-to-Play” system, which “makes movement the controller,” they say. It works by connecting Tovie to a kid’s device via Bluetooth, and whenever they stop pedaling, the YouTube video they’re watching (or the video game they’re playing) stops working.

The goal is to keep kids moving their bodies but still allowing them screen time.

“Studies done by researchers have found that movement in general is a tool for self-regulation of the hormones in their bodies, to help the nervous system calm down,” Dalmia told Inc. “It helps get the excess energy out before they have a big meltdown.”

He added that with Tovie, he and Jain feel they are “creating a whole new category of children’s fitness.”

“People think that children can get exercise while outside playing, but not all kids are the same, and they have different circumstances. In winter, it’s too cold outside. If parents are busy preparing dinner, they can’t watch their kids playing outside,” he added.

This isn’t Dalmia and Jain’s first stationary exercise product. In 2013, they debuted Cubii, an under-desk elliptical, and to bring it to life, they raised almost $300,000 on Kickstarter.

They’re producing Tovie the same way. This time, the Kickstarter goal is only $50K, and with nine days left in the campaign, they’re about to cross that threshold thanks to just over 100 backers.

Once they’ve shipped the initial models to Kickstarter supporters, the couple hope to put Tovie in big-box stores like Target and Walmart–and then keep developing products.

“The Tovie bike will be the first of many bikes focused on children’s health,” Dalmia told Inc. “Being a dad myself, I know how important physical movement is. The goal is to encourage movement and get kids started with fitness to start good lifelong habits.”

When Your Parents Are Dying: Some of the Simplest, Most Difficult and Redemptive Life-Advice You’ll Ever Receive

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Death makes human beings seem like very small containers that are packed so densely we can only be aware of a fraction of what’s inside us from moment to moment.”

“Your children are not your children. They are the sons and daughters of Life’s longing for itself,” Kahlil Gibran wrote in his poignant verse on parenting. And yet we are, each of us, someone’s child — physiologically or psychologically or both — and they sing themselves through us as we sing ourselves into our longing for life, whether we like the melody or not.

Like a Zen koan, this fact becomes utterly discomposing when you begin thinking deeply about the fundamental, layered realities beneath the mundane, even banal factuality of the fact. Parents — the very notion of them. The notion that you — this immensely complex totality of sinew and selfhood, this portable universe shimmering with a million ideas and passions and little ways of being-in-the-world that make you you — began as a glimmer in someone else’s eye, a set of chemical reactions that became molecules that became cells in someone else’s body before they constellated into you. The notion that so many dimensions of your personhood, so many of the givens you take for granted in making sense of the world, were forged by someone other than yourself (and possibly other than the body that begot the cells that became you) — someone who occupies, in the cosmogony of you, this strange and staggering position of arbiter between the existence and nonexistence of the particular you that you are.

Kinship by Maria Popova. (Available as a print.)

The doubly discomposing experience of what happens when that arbiter crosses the threshold of their own nonexistence is what Mary Gaitskill addresses in her thoughtful, tender contribution to Take My Advice: Letters to the Next Generation from People Who Know a Thing or Two (public library) — the wondrous 2002 anthology by artist and writer James L. Harmon, inspired by one of his own spiritual parents: Rilke and his timeless Letters to a Young Poet.

Gaitskill writes:

My advice here is very specific and practicable. It is advice I wish someone had given me as forcefully as I’m about to give it now: When your parents are dying, you should go be with them. You should spend as much time as you can. This may seem obvious; you would be surprised how difficult it can be. It is less difficult if you have a good relationship with the parent or, even if you don’t, if you’re old enough to have lost friends and to have seriously considered your own death. Even so, it may be more difficult than you think.

With the sensitive caveat that there exist people “to whom this general directive does not apply” and her advice is not meant as a rebuke to those people, Gaitskill addresses those of us raised by fallible parents who, in one way or another, failed dreadfully at the deepest task of parenting — unconditional love:

If you’re a young person who has had a bad relationship with your parent, it’s a nightmare of anger, confusion, and guilt. Even if you hate them, you may still not want to believe it’s happening… Even if your parents have been abusive, physically or emotionally, they are part of you in a way that goes beyond personality or even character. Maybe “beyond” isn’t the right word. They are part of you in a way that runs beneath the daily self. They have passed an essence to you. This essence may not be recognizable; your parents may have made its raw matter into something so different than what you have made of it that it seems you are nothing alike. That they have given you this essence may be no virtue of theirs — they may not even have chosen to do so. (It may not be biological either; all I say here I would say about adoptive as well as birth parents.)

Art by Ekua Holmes from The Stuff of Stars by Marion Dane Bauer.

Being with a dying parent, Gaitskill notes, is a way of honoring the fact — so basic yet so incomprehensible a fact — that they will soon be gone, and with them will go your experience of being their child in the way you have known, a fundamental way in which you have known yourself. At the heart of this dual recognition is “the hard truth that we know nothing about who we are or what our lives mean.” She writes:

Nothing makes this plainer than being in the presence of a dying person for any length of time. Death makes human beings seem like very small containers that are packed so densely we can only be aware of a fraction of what’s inside us from moment to moment. Being in the presence of death can break you open, disgorging feelings that are deeper and more powerful than anything you thought you knew. If you have had a loving, clear relationship with your parent, this experience probably won’t be quite as wrenching. There may in fact be moments of pure tenderness, even exaltation. But you might still have to watch your parent appear to break, mentally and physically, disintegrating into something you can no longer recognize. In some ways this is terrible — many people find it absolutely so. There is another side to it, though: In witnessing this seeming breakage, we are glimpsing the part of our parents that doesn’t translate in human terms, that which we know nothing about, and which the human container is too small to give shape to.

Art by Dorothy Lathrop, 1922. (Available as a print.)

Because any emotional experience we have when facing another is always an emotional experience we have within, and about, ourselves — especially if that other gave rise to this self — facing this supraknowable quality is facing the limits of our own self-knowledge. Gaitskill writes:

Knowing your feelings is hard too because there’s so much emotion, it’s hard to tell which is truest. Part of you might want to leave right away; part of you might want to stay forever. That’s why I advised that you stay “for as long as you can.” What that means will vary with each person, with the needs of the parent and the other relations. A day might be enough, or it might take a whole month. If it’s a prolonged situation, it might be good to leave for a few days and come back. Those decisions are so personal they are beyond the scope of my advice — except my advice to pay close attention to yourself. If you feel, To hell with this, I’m getting out, don’t worry — there’s room for that. Maybe in fact you should leave. But before you do, be sure that voice is not shouting down a truer one. When your parents die, you will never see them again. You might think you understand that, but until it happens, you don’t.

Art by Margaret C. Cook from a rare 1913 English edition of Walt Whitman’s Leaves of Grass. (Available as a print.)

In a sentiment on the surface contradictory but in fact consonant with the deeper meaning of what artist Louise Bourgeois inscribed into her lifelong diary in her old age — “You are born alone. You die alone. The value of the space in between is trust and love.” — Gaitskill concludes:

They say that you come into the world alone and that you leave alone too. But you aren’t born alone; your mother is with you, maybe your father too. Their presence may have been loving, it may have been demented, it may have been both. But they were with you. When they are dying, remember that. And go be with them.

Complement this fragment of Take My Advice — which also includes novelist Richard Powers on the most important attitude you can take toward your life and philosopher Martha Nussbaum on how to honor your inner world — with Richard Dawkins on the luckiness of death, Marcus Aurelius on embracing mortality as the key to living fully, and Zen Hospice Project founder Frank Ostaseski on the five life-redeeming invitation to extend in facing death, then revisit this tender illustrated meditation on the cycle of life.

Canon says camera prices could rise in 2027 if memory costs stay high, and it’s not the only camera maker feeling the squeeze

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Canon no longer expects memory prices to ease next year as AI demand strains supply, and says it will look to pass higher costs on to buyers.

Photo illustration by Dale Baskin/Malte Mueller/fStop via Getty Images.

If you’ve been holding off on buying a new Canon camera, the company’s latest earnings call might give you something to think about. Speaking with investors after posting its third-quarter 2026 results, Canon said it no longer expects memory prices to come down in 2027, and that if costs remain elevated, it will look to offset them through higher camera prices. And Canon isn’t the only camera maker dealing with higher memory costs.

What Canon said

Memory costs have come up at every one of Canon’s earnings calls this year, so the topic itself isn’t new. What is new is the outlook: earlier this year, Canon said it expected memory prices to begin easing in the second half of 2027. Now, according to Canon’s Q3 Q&A summary, it doesn’t expect them to fall next year at all. The company says it has already secured the memory it needs for 2026, but expects to pay more for components in 2027, and that its policy is to offset those higher costs through price increases.

If memory costs remain high, Canon says it will raise camera prices to offset them.Mitchell Clark

It’s worth noting that this isn’t entirely hypothetical. Canon told investors in July that it had already raised some prices in response to rising costs, including memory, though it didn’t say which products or markets were affected. It says it will continue to weigh further increases against their effect on sales.

Why it matters to camera buyers

Memory is central to how modern digital cameras work. High-speed continuous shooting, pre-capture modes, subject-tracking algorithms and more all lean on fast, plentiful memory. As AI-related demand has driven memory prices up across the industry, camera makers have found themselves competing for the same production capacity as data center operators, which are effectively the 800-pound gorillas in this market.

As a strategy, securing its memory supply early bought Canon time. But it isn’t a long-term solution if the underlying costs persist.

The wider picture

One company warning about costs is one thing. Several camera makers dealing with the same problem at once is another. Other manufacturers have also flagged rising memory costs to investors this year, and some have already started raising prices. That includes the US, where Blackmagic Design has raised prices on some of its cinema cameras and storage products, citing memory costs, and US buyers have already absorbed multiple rounds of tariff-driven price increases since last year.

Securing memory supply for 2026 bought Canon time, but it expects to pay more for components in 2027.Dale Baskin

When multiple camera makers are facing the same cost pressure, the probability that any one of them absorbs it entirely, rather than sharing it with buyers, drops considerably. Ultimately, this isn’t a Canon-specific story but an industry pricing story that happens to be told clearly through Canon’s investor relations disclosures.

What to watch

Canon reports its full-year 2026 results early next year, and that call will be a useful checkpoint. By then, the company should have a clearer read on what it expects to pay for memory in 2027, and how much of that it plans to pass on to buyers. And because Canon sells more interchangeable-lens cameras than any other manufacturer, its pricing decisions are likely to be closely watched by the rest of the industry.

Andy Jassy’s prediction: Amazon could have two $1 trillion businesses within a decade

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told analysts Thursday that “some folks are sleeping a bit” on how much progress the company’s stores business has made. (GeekWire File Photo)

No company has ever had a business with $1 trillion in annual revenue. Andy Jassy says Amazon is on a path to have two.

Amazon Web Services and the company’s stores business could both reach that milestone in the next five to 10 years, the Amazon CEO said Thursday in the company’s third-quarter earnings release and on its call with analysts.

It’s “pretty rare” for Jassy to make a projection that far out, Morgan Stanley analyst Brian Nowak observed on the call, asking how he gets there.

Jassy’s answer started with AWS, which is at a $184 billion annual revenue run rate after growing 39% in the third quarter. He expects growth of more than 40% in both 2027 and 2028, and pointed to a $578 billion backlog of contracted AWS revenue.

The largest customers reserve capacity in huge blocks, sometimes as much as a gigawatt at a time, giving Amazon “very good visibility” into 2027, 2028 and part of 2029, he said.

Beyond that, he said, businesses are still early in using AI, the cost of running AI models keeps falling, and new uses keep emerging, such as AI models finding and fixing security flaws.

“You can’t predict with any certainty five to 10 years from now, especially at these types of growth rates,” Jassy said of AWS. “But it’s not a stretch for us to imagine that this could be over a trillion dollar business.”

Jassy’s forecast is a big jump from seven months ago. In March, Jassy told employees that AWS had a chance to reach at least $600 billion in annual revenue within about a decade, double his earlier expectation, according to Reuters.

Amazon’s stores business is bigger than AWS. Sales from Amazon’s online and physical stores totaled $309 billion over the past 12 months, or about $575 billion if Jassy is including third-party seller services and advertising in his trillion-dollar projection. We’ve asked Amazon to clarify.

For comparison, Walmart, the world’s largest retailer, had $713 billion in revenue in its most recent fiscal year.

Whichever numbers he’s using, Jassy said “some folks are sleeping a bit” on how much progress the stores business has made.

Jassy argued that Amazon’s stores business is gaining ground on competitors. Amazon added more than $90 billion in gross sales over the past 12 months, he said, and is adding about twice as much per quarter as its next-largest U.S. competitor.

Units sold are up nearly 15% so far this year, Jassy said. He noted that nearly 80% of retail sales still happen in physical stores, which he expects to shift online over time.

The bold prediction came on a rough day for the company. Amazon shares fell more than 7% in after-hours trading after its profit fell short of Wall Street’s expectations.

It’s not going to be cheap to get there. Amazon expects to spend about $220 billion on capital projects this year, mostly for AWS, and Jassy said the company will spend “meaningfully more” in 2027. He said Amazon has “a clear line of sight to attractive financial returns” and is earning back its capital spending faster than before.

The impact is showing up in Amazon’s finances. Its free cash flow, the cash left over after capital spending, was negative $42.4 billion over the past 12 months, compared with positive $14.8 billion a year earlier.

With its spending running ahead of the cash its businesses bring in, Amazon has raised $115 billion through long-term debt this year. Its interest expense more than tripled in the third quarter to $1.7 billion, cutting into its profit.

“We’re in the very early days of this inflection,” Jassy said. “Our strategy is working, and I expect it will for the foreseeable future.”

Map Showing Potential Origins of Martian Meteorite Teghaza 001

JPEG (1.22 MB)

Description

This global map of Mars shows 13 locations that match the mineralogy and chemistry of Teghaza 001, one of the most ancient Martian meteorites ever found on Earth. While scientists can never know for sure where a Martian meteorite originated, Teghaza 001 includes clues that link it to highlands in the southern hemisphere of the Red Planet.

The base map of this image was produced with data from the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter instrument aboard NASA’s Mars Global Surveyor mission. Scientists used data from NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter to identify areas of the map (noted with white circles) that have similar mineralogy and chemistry as Teghaza 001.
