Two trends are converging at an opportune time for new-build homeowners: the revival of front porches in home design and the hottest Halloween decorating trends that are centered, quite literally, on the front porch.

In new-home design, front porches are making a comeback alongside the growth of walkable neighborhoods and homeowners’ desire for community connection. Also, homes and yards are getting smaller, so homeowners are relying more heavily on outdoor spaces, like the front porch, to extend living space without increasing square footage.

According to the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB), porches rank as the most popular outdoor feature on new homes.

Meanwhile, Halloween is big business, and homeowners are getting serious about decorating. Over a third of consumers say Halloween is their favorite holiday, according to the National Retail Federation’s annual Halloween consumer shopping survey. They report that consumer spending on Halloween is expected to reach $13.5 billion in 2026.

Halloween decor used to be somewhat limited to front door wreaths, a jack-o’-lantern or two, or for more ambitious homeowners who had the space, lawn decorations and even some special effects. But the availability, square footage, and design of today’s front porch are offering new opportunities for homeowners who are Halloween fans too.

Sheltered from the elements, the front porch has enough space for decorative vignettes, wide front steps that encourage traffic flow, room to gather with neighbors and trick-or-treaters, and electrical outlets that make lighting and special effects easy.

Want to style your porch, but don’t have time to scour your local pumpkin patch, or are unsure of how to curate the bespoke pumpkin-inspired designs that are everywhere on social media?

Style support is available, very possibly in your neighborhood. The growing popularity of front porch decor has given rise to a niche industry: pumpkin concierges, a reportedly in-demand service in several regions of the country.

For a fee, pumpkin concierges work with local farmers to source pumpkins, then arrange elaborate, coordinated displays with seasonal greenery and other decorative accents, and some companies even return to remove everything at the end of the season.

The growing popularity of these services underscores how much attention homeowners are paying to their front porches, particularly during the holidays, and how intricate some of the seasonal designs are becoming, not only for the pros, but for DIY homeowners too.

Want to conjure Halloween magic on your front porch this holiday? Here are the spooky and stylish trends to watch.

​Layered, Luxe Pumpkins

Today’s front porches are intended to be additional living space, so homeowners are embracing many of the same design elements outside that they are inside, including layering materials and colors cohesively.

Rather than relying on a traditional orange-and-black color scheme, homeowners are using harvest-inspired curated palettes with sage, marigolds, and creams, or plums, deeper greens, and muted oranges for Halloween style.

Instead of one-off pieces of Halloween decor, Halloween front porches are being layered with textiles and accents that complement the existing porch furniture, like themed doormats, patterned pillows and throws, and seasonal candles.

Witchcore

Although Witchcore, a micro-aesthetic inspired by witchcraft, folklore, mysticism, and nature, isn’t specific to Halloween, several elements of this look are perfect for the holiday and for your front porch.

Assemble decor using twisted branches, wildflowers, and leaves, along with weathered and slightly spooky accents, like vintage books, lanterns, and apothecary bottles, that may or may not contain magic potions. Broomsticks, black cats, ravens, and cauldrons are nice Witchcore-inspired touches too.

Haunted Gardens

Moody, atmospheric haunted gardens are taking over front porches this Halloween, drawing on Gothic and Victorian influences for a hauntingly romantic look.

Think oversized planters with dark, spiky foliage, weathered urns, brass, dried flowers, and twisty, “creeping” vines. Cluster pumpkins of various sizes, and strategically place cobwebs for a hauntingly romantic look. Consider uplighting to create shadows, or weave twinkle lights through the porch, for an ethereal feel.

Old-School Halloween Theater

Design in general is leaning more heavily into the experiential, and Halloween, for its spine-tingling fun and jump scares, aligns perfectly with decor curated to assemble an emotional response, fun or fright, depending on your spooky goals.

​Consider your front porch a mini-stage for a theatrical, immersive trick-or-treat experience, with a defined, enclosed space for a backdrop, as well as easy access to electrical outlets for special effects.

Popular are fog machines, flickering lights, motion-activated skeletons, creepy soundtracks, and animatronic figures that spring to life as trick-or-treaters approach, if they dare. Make use of architectural elements, like porch columns, to enhance the decor, with wrapped cobwebs or tattered materials.

The latest move in the bond market may look like a story for Wall Street, but its consequences extend directly to the housing industry. Long-term Treasury yields have risen sharply as investors demand greater compensation to hold government debt, reinforcing the possibility that elevated borrowing costs could remain part of the economic landscape.

Several forces are pushing yields higher. Inflation remains persistent while the economy continues to show resilience, raising questions about how quickly price pressures will normalize. At the same time, investors are paying closer attention to federal deficits and the growing supply of Treasury debt.

Artificial intelligence is adding another dimension. Massive investment in data centers and related infrastructure could support economic growth while also keeping inflationary pressures firmer than they otherwise would be. That expectation can feed into higher long-term bond yields.

For housing, the most immediate concern is mortgage rates.

Higher Treasury yields generally put upward pressure on mortgage rates, creating another obstacle for prospective buyers already struggling with affordability. There is some relief from the mortgage spread, or the difference between the 10-year Treasury yield and the 30-year fixed mortgage rate. That spread, which historically averages about 1.7 percentage points, widened beyond 3 percentage points during the pandemic but has since narrowed to roughly 2.3 points. If the spread were still above 3 percentage points, mortgage rates would be closer to 8.3% rather than 7.6%.

For businesses, elevated rates translate into higher borrowing costs and interest expenses at a time when operating costs are already challenging. Consumer confidence could become even more sensitive as households react to negative economic headlines.

Is sharenting safe? 7 ways to mindfully post about your kids online

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

“Sharenting” is more common than ever. Discover why parents share posts about their kids online, the risks involved, and 7 tips for safer, more mindful posting.

Your kid loses their first tooth or says something hilarious at dinner, and your thumb is already halfway to the share button. For a lot of parents and caregivers, posting these moments feels like second nature.

Believe it or not, there's actually a name for this habit: sharenting. And while it’s often a simple way to stay connected, it also puts your child’s story online before they can choose to share it. If you’ve started to wonder about the risks, it’s a good time to take stock of your posting habits.

Here's a look at why parents share and what’s worth knowing about the risks, plus seven small shifts to help you post with confidence.

 

What is sharenting?

Sharenting is when parents or caregivers post about their kids on social media. It could be a single birthday photo, or years of posts documenting a whole childhood. Think videos, funny quotes, school updates, potty-training wins, and even ultrasound pictures from before the baby arrives.  

It's also widespread. Research reviews estimate that about three in four parents share photos, stories, or videos of their kids online, and most use their children's real names.

Parents have always shared news about their kids through holiday letters, birth announcements, and photos mailed to relatives. Social media just made the audience much bigger, the posts more public, and the record harder to remove.

Why do parents share their kids’ lives online?

Most sharenting comes from a good place. You're proud of your kid and you want the people in your life to know. Studies on parents who post about their kids point to the same reasons:

  • Staying close: Photos and videos let faraway family and friends watch your child grow. 

  • Celebrating and remembering: Posts mark your child’s milestones and give you something to scroll back through later.

  • Finding community: Other parents and caregivers can offer advice and a “me too” at 2am, when parenting feels isolating.

  • Feeling seen: A kind comment on a rough day reminds you that you’re doing okay.

💙 Feeling alone as a parent? Here’s how to deal with loneliness. 

 

What are the risks of sharenting on social media?

Sharing your kids online isn’t necessarily harmful. Still, a few risks are worth knowing about:

A lasting digital footprint. Screenshots, reshares, and archives mean a photo can outlast the original post. Many kids have years of photos online before they're old enough to understand who can see them. 

Privacy and consent. Young kids can't weigh in on what you post about them. Their public image takes shape long before they can agree to it, and that can be hard to undo later.

Personal information. Someone could piece together a full name, birth date, hometown, and school from a handful of posts. Over time, details like these may be misused for identity theft.

Location and background details. Location tags and details like a house number, school logo, or regular park in the background show where your child spends their time. On a public account, that can make their routine visible to strangers.

Facial recognition and AI. Photos on public accounts may be saved, scanned, or collected by AI tools and facial recognition software. Some of those tools turn ordinary photos into fake images.

How to practice mindful sharenting: 7 tips to balance safety and connection

You don't have to log off to keep your kid safe. A few small shifts protect their privacy and still let you share the moments you love. Start with one or two of these, whatever feels most doable, then add more as they become habit.

1. Take a mindful pause before you post

Before you hit share, take a breath and think about whether you want to post this moment. If you do, scan the photo and caption for anything that gives away too much, like a full name or a street sign.

For some structure, a short breathing exercise or meditation can help.

💙 Listen to Pause to Breathe with Prof. Megan Reitz on Calm.

2. Verify your privacy settings 

A couple of settings are worth checking before your next post. If your account is public, anyone can find and save your posts. Photos taken on your phone may also include the location where you took them.

Try: 

  • Switch to a private account and remove followers you don’t know.

  • Review your tagging settings.

  • Turn off geotagging in your camera and app settings.

Platforms can change their defaults without warning, so it’s worth redoing this check every couple of months.

 

3. Leave out identifying details

You can share the moment without giving away your child’s identity. Skip birth dates, school names, and daily routines. Use a nickname or first initial instead of your child’s full name.

Posting a day or two later makes it harder for anyone to know where your child is in real time.

4. Ask your child for their opinion

Once your child is old enough to weigh in, show them the post and ask if they're okay with you sharing it. This shows them that their opinion matters. If your child is too young to decide, the next tip can help you decide for them.

5. Picture your child as an adult

Imagine your child at 25, seeing what you’re about to post. Does it give away something private about them, such as a health issue or a hard time at school? What seems relatable now may feel too personal to them later.

If you're unsure, keep the more personal moments in a private family group or an offline album.

6. Review your past posts

Your past posts felt right at the time, and it’s okay if some show more than you’d share today. Scroll back through your posts for details like full names, school names, or locations.

Delete the post, archive it, or move it to a private album if it feels too revealing. 

7. Talk with family about boundaries

You're not the only one with a camera. Grandparents, co-parents, babysitters, and friends may post photos of your child too, usually out of love and pride. A conversation up front about family boundaries keeps everyone on the same page.

You might ask family to:

  • leave full names and birth dates out of captions

  • skip location tags and recognizable landmarks in the background

  • use private groups or shared albums instead of public posts

  • check with you before posting anything

Sharenting FAQs

What is the meaning of sharenting?

Sharenting means posting about your child on social media, and the word blends "sharing" and "parenting." It covers anything from baby photos to regular updates as they grow up.

What are some common sharenting examples?

Common sharenting examples include ultrasound pictures, birthday posts, funny quotes, and health or milestone updates. Even a photo that seems harmless can reveal more than you intended, like a club sign or a team jersey in the background.

Why do parents share posts about their kids online so often?

Many parents share to stay connected and celebrate their kids. Sharenting keeps faraway family members in the loop and connects you with other parents who get it. During hard stretches, it can help you feel less alone.

What are the biggest risks of sharenting on social media?

The main risks of sharenting include a lasting digital footprint and exposed personal details. Photos can be saved, reshared, or collected by AI tools, which is why child safety groups encourage private accounts. 

That said, many posts don’t lead to harm, and small habits like turning off location tags help lower the risks further.

How does sharenting affect children’s privacy and safety?

Sharenting shapes your child’s public image before they’re old enough to agree to it. Names, schools, and tagged locations can also reveal their routines to strangers, and a handful of details may be used for identity theft. 

If you keep personal moments offline now, your child has more room to shape their own image as they grow.

At what age should kids have a say in social media posts?

There's no set age for when kids should have a say in what you post. Young kids may not fully understand what sharing means, so the decision starts with you. You can start asking as soon as they’re able to weigh in, and give their yes or no more weight as they grow. 

How can I practice responsible sharenting on social media?

You can practice responsible sharenting with a mindful pause before you post. From there, check your privacy settings, and leave out personal details including names, schools, and locations. Ask for your child’s input once they’re old enough, and let family members know your boundaries.

Justices to decide whether failure to plead an affirmative defense prevents moving for summary judgment on that same defense

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Younge v. Fulton Judicial Circuit District Attorney’s Office, Georgia, to be argued on Nov. 2, involves an issue that will quicken the pulse of civil procedure aficionados. No less than four different rules of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure are in play: affirmative defenses under Rule 8, amending a pleading under Rule 15, pretrial management under Rule 16, and summary judgment under Rule 56.

The case raising this cornucopia of pretrial procedural rules comes out of a relatively typical civil rights suit in which the plaintiff asserts that she was improperly dismissed from her job and is now suing for damages. Jasmine Younge held a high-level position in the Fulton County District Attorney’s Office for about two months until she was fired in July 2019 by District Attorney Paul Howard. Younge says she was dismissed from her job about two weeks after telling Howard that she was pregnant. Later that year, Younge sued the Fulton County District Attorney’s Office for a number of civil rights violations, including the claim that termination because of her pregnancy violated Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

The DA’s office answered the complaint, and pretrial proceedings ensued. Save for a few disputed matters, discovery closed in January 2022. About two months later, at a pretrial hearing on a discovery issue, the DA’s office informed the court and Younge that it intended to move for summary judgment, including on the basis of the personal-staff exemption defense, which it had not asserted in its answer. (Under Title VII, certain officials are exempted from the statutory definition of employee, including (1) members of an elected official’s “personal staff” and (2) “policymaking appointees.”) The DA’s office explained that it did not learn of the defense until its lawyer began drafting the summary judgment motion.

Soon after the March 2022 hearing, the DA’s office moved for summary judgment, citing its defenses based upon the statutory exemptions from Title VII in support of the motion. The DA never sought to amend its answer to add these defenses.

Younge objected to the motion, arguing as a procedural matter that it was too late for the DA’s office to assert new affirmative defenses. Her waiver argument regarding the personal-staff exemption was rejected several times in the lower courts, first by the federal magistrate judge who permitted the personal-staff defense to be asserted and recommended summary judgment in favor of the DA’s office predicated on that defense. The district court then agreed with this recommendation and entered judgment in favor of the DA. Next, the case was appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the 11th Circuit.

The 11th Circuit (as had the lower courts) applied its reasoning in the 1988 case of Hassan v. U.S. Postal Service, in which it had employed a prejudice analysis to determine whether to allow the defendant to move for summary judgment based on an unpled affirmative defense. Here, the district court had determined that there was no prejudice to allowing the personal-staff defense. Significantly, in addition to finding that the DA’s office should prevail on the defense, the lower courts noted that Younge could not identify any additional discovery that she would have pursued had the defense been asserted earlier.

As the 11th Circuit summarized, “since Hassan, [i]t had repeatedly reaffirmed that a district court may receive evidence of an unpleaded affirmative defense if the plaintiff was not ‘prejudiced’ by the defendant’s failure to plead the defense in its answer.” Furthermore, the circuit court explained that “the record contains copious discovery evidence concerning each of the six factors” relevant to “the personal staff exemption.” Accordingly, the 11th Circuit affirmed the district court, finding that “Younge had not suffered prejudice from the DA’s Office’s late assertion of the personal staff exemption” and that summary judgment had properly been entered in favor of the DA.

The justices agreed to hear the case in March to determine whether an affirmative defense may be asserted as a basis for summary judgment if not presented earlier in a pleading.

The parties’ positions

In seeking reversal of the judgment below, Younge argues that the court should take a textualist approach to reading the applicable rules. “Rule 8(c)(1) emphasizes” without exception “that a defendant must plead ‘any’ affirmative defenses,” she states. Since the DA did not do so, it should not have been allowed to seek summary judgment on the basis of such an unpled defense.

Younge further argues that allowing a summary judgment motion based upon an unpled affirmative defense permits a defendant to circumvent the requirements of Rule 15(a)(2) for amending a pleading and Rule 16(b)(4) for modifying a scheduling order. The former requires a court to analyze a number of factors, including “undue delay” on the part of the party seeking to amend, and is stricter than a mere prejudice standard. The latter requires “good cause,” considers due diligence, and, again, is more demanding than just showing prejudice. Younge would then have the court set aside the 11th Circuit’s prejudice standard and apply the “well-established standards and procedures of Rules 15(a)(2) and 16(b)(4).”

The DA, on the other hand, stands by the prejudice standard set out in Hassan and applied by the 11th Circuit. It asserts that this standard is correct given the interrelated purposes of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure: to resolve cases on the merits and to avoid their resolution on the basis of technical or formalistic application of the rules themselves.

According to the DA, the text, structure, and historical development of the FRCP support this purposive reading of the rules. Regarding the text, the DA invokes Rule 61, the harmless error rule, which provides that “[a]t every stage of the proceeding, the court must disregard all errors and defects that do not affect any party’s substantial rights.” “Structurally,” the DA expresses that “the Rules impose limits on amending pleadings to import new factual issues … but not on raising legal arguments like the one the DA’s Office raised here.” And as for the history, the DA points out that “Rule 12 used to provide that a defendant waives all unpleaded defenses but no longer does.”

Overall, the purposive approach to interpreting the FRCP may prove especially appealing to the court: As the DA argues, the FRCP drew on and were influenced by the rules in the courts of equity, which aimed to simplify pleading rules and adopted a flexible approach to promote the resolution of cases on the merits. In pretrial matters such as pleadings and discovery, context – the underlying facts, the claims and defenses asserted, and the parties’ conduct, among other things – matters. Given this, although the court may not write broadly, I think it’s unlikely to find that the 11th Circuit’s prejudice standard was out of bounds.
