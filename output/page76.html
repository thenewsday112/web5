The term cardio often elicits grumbles as people picture punishing treadmill sessions and trail runs. But add the word cozy before it, and cardio is suddenly…a lot more palatable. That’s the gist behind the “cozy cardio” trend, which took off on social media in 2024 and resurfaced in 2026. The concept is simple: perform low- to moderate-intensity workouts in a comfortable, inviting environment.

Cozy cardio could look like setting out a few candles in your living room, dimming the lights, and turning on your favorite Netflix show while you stroll on a walking pad. Or it could involve donning a comfy velvet sweatsuit and sipping a delicious beverage while going for an easy walk on your favorite trail or through your neighborhood.

Cozy cardio looks different for everyone, according to Hope Zuckerbrow, the TikToker credited with starting the trend. But no matter how you practice it, the overarching objective is the same: take the pressure off exercise and actually enjoy movement.

As a certified personal trainer, I wholeheartedly support that notion and think there are several key benefits that outdoor athletes could reap from embracing the trend.

Cozy Cardio Can Keep You Consistent When Life Gets in the Way–or Motivation Wanes

When it comes to exercise, many people have an all-or-nothing mindset—especially athletes who take their outdoor sport seriously. They have a set workout plan—for example, a five-mile run or an hour-long strength session—and when something thwarts it, they might throw in the towel and do nothing at all.

But staying consistent with your workout routine is key to reaping the mental and physical benefits of exercise. So a better approach is to still do some form of movement even if it looks different than what you’d originally envisioned. That’s where cozy cardio can come in.

Because the trend is all about lowering the stakes on exercise, it can feel really doable, even on those days when life gets in the way, or you’re not in the mood to do anything.

Plus, cozy cardio creates an environment that makes working out a pleasurable experience. And that’s important, because actually enjoying exercise is associated with actually sticking with a workout routine, according to research.

Gentle Movement Can Promote Recovery

Another benefit of cozy cardio is that it can serve as active recovery in the wake of long, hard workouts. Research suggests doing even six to ten minutes of active recovery after a workout may boost overall performance.

So engaging in a short cozy cardio session after a big hike, ride, or run could inject a little more energy into your next outdoor adventure.

Steady-State Cardio Can Bolster Your Aerobic Fitness

A 2026 study found that low-intensity exercises improve both aerobic fitness and cardiometabolic health, which refers to factors involving the heart and metabolism, including blood pressure and cholesterol.

If you choose a steady-state form of exercise for your cozy cardio session, such as walking or a bike ride, and keep your effort low, you’ll likely tick the box for Zone 1 or Zone 2 cardio—both of which, when done consistently, can help build your aerobic fitness, or your VO2 max. VO2 max describes how efficiently your body takes in oxygen and transports it to the rest of the body so that your muscles can use it during physical activity. The higher your VO2, the better your aerobic fitness.

Overall, one of the biggest benefits of improving your aerobic health is going longer and having more energy during your favorite outdoor sport.

Tokenized securities are advancing into mainstream finance — but their expansion is exposing a problem that blockchain technology alone cannot solve: how to maintain a unified market when the same financial asset can exist across multiple trading platforms and digital networks at once.

Banks, exchanges, and asset managers are racing to build blockchain-based infrastructure to modernize securities trading. Yet the promised benefits — faster settlement, continuous trading — hinge on a harder question: can these systems preserve liquidity, legal ownership, and reliable price formation as they scale?

The stakes are coming into sharper focus as traditional institutions move beyond experimental pilots. Tokenization may reduce operational friction, but without coordinated market infrastructure, it risks introducing new inefficiencies into markets that already depend on an intricate web of relationships between brokers, custodians, and clearinghouses.

Tokenized Stocks Could Create Parallel Markets

A tokenized share doesn’t necessarily behave like an ordinary share traded through a regulated stock exchange.

Different tokenization arrangements can grant investors very different ownership rights, legal claims, and relationships with the underlying securities.

Some structures represent direct interests in the financial instrument itself; others amount to claims against an intermediary that holds the asset. Crucially, a blockchain record alone does not establish that an investor holds the same rights as a registered shareholder—those rights depend entirely on the instrument’s legal structure and the ownership arrangements behind it.

Fragmentation adds another layer of complication

If tokenized versions of a company’s shares trade across several platforms, liquidity could splinter among venues operating under different rules. For directories and market participants tracking the broader crypto-financial landscape—such as Cryptwerk—the proliferation of trading venues underscores how fragmented digital asset ecosystems can become. Investors could encounter inconsistent prices, varying settlement arrangements, and differences in how corporate actions are processed from one venue to the next.

These risks grow sharper when tokenized products are issued or traded independently of the companies whose shares they represent.

For exchanges, the commercial challenge is significant. A platform offering continuous trading still has to attract enough buyers and sellers to support efficient transactions. Extended hours deliver little value if investors face wider spreads or thin liquidity outside established market sessions.

Faster Settlement Requires More Than Blockchain

One of tokenization’s most compelling advantages is the ability to coordinate the transfer of a security and its payment within a single programmable system.

Traditional settlement involves multiple institutions verifying transactions, updating records, and arranging the movement of assets and funds. Tokenization could streamline parts of that process by allowing related actions to execute together.

The Bank for International Settlements has studied this possibility through research on unified ledgers that combine tokenized financial assets with regulated forms of money. Such arrangements could support delivery-versus-payment transactions — reducing the risk that one party hands over an asset without receiving the corresponding payment.

But settlement efficiency depends on far more than a blockchain network’s raw speed.

Financial institutions still have to determine which assets can be used for payment, when a transfer becomes legally final, and how each transaction interacts with existing clearing and custody arrangements. A tokenized security might transfer in seconds while the underlying cash movement or legal reconciliation still runs on conventional infrastructure.

That creates a critical gap between technical settlement speed and the completion of a legally enforceable transaction. For institutional investors, that second ultimately matters.

Europe’s Tokenization Experiments Reveal Practical Barriers

Europe offers a cautionary example of how regulatory permission doesn’t automatically produce an active market.

In June 2025, the European Securities and Markets Authority reported that the EU’s Distributed Ledger Technology Pilot Regime had attracted only three authorized market infrastructures — with minimal live trading activity. The regulator pointed to obstacles including interoperability, access to central bank money, and restrictions on the types and volumes of assets eligible platforms could handle, and recommended adjustments intended to encourage broader participation.

These findings matter well beyond Europe.

The growing involvement of banks, asset managers, and stock exchanges is reshaping the industry’s priorities. The Cryptocurrency Post has explored this expansion of tokenization beyond crypto-native companies, including the role traditional financial institutions are playing in building regulated digital asset infrastructure. Their participation makes system compatibility far more important.

A tokenized bond issued through one regulated platform may not be transferable to another without additional technical arrangements, legal agreements, or intermediaries. A financial institution might be unable to use a tokenized asset as collateral unless its counterparties and settlement providers recognize the instrument.

Interoperability, in other words, involves far more than connecting blockchains. It requires common standards for asset ownership, transfer restrictions, compliance, and settlement finality.

Institutional Adoption Will Depend on Market Quality

The success of tokenized securities is unlikely to depend on how many assets end up represented on blockchain networks.

Institutional investors evaluate financial products on liquidity, execution costs, counterparty exposure, and legal certainty. Tokenized products have to meet all of those requirements while demonstrating a clear operational advantage over the alternatives already in use.

Consider a tokenized government bond. It could prove genuinely useful in collateral management — but only if it can move efficiently between approved counterparties and settlement systems. Its value would come from cutting delays and operational costs, not from simply existing on a blockchain. The same logic applies to equities: a tokenized stock market has to offer dependable pricing, enforceable shareholder rights, and access to real liquidity.

Institutions also face a structural choice: build their own independent blockchain platforms, or participate in shared infrastructure. Separate systems may offer more control, but they can drive up integration costs and complicate transactions between institutions. Shared platforms could ease some of that friction — though only with agreement on governance, technical standards, and operational responsibilities.

Ultimately, tokenization may become a routine component of financial infrastructure without spawning a separate market for every digital representation of an asset. The strongest applications are likely to improve existing securities processes while preserving the protections and liquidity investors already expect.

For Wall Street, the central question is no longer whether financial assets can be tokenized. It’s whether the resulting markets can operate as reliably and efficiently as the systems they’re meant to improve.

What Is EIP-7702? Ethereum's Smart Account Feature Explained, and How to Stay Safe

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On October 29, 2026, an Ethereum hot wallet belonging to Binance was emptied in a single transaction.

The tool behind the drain was EIP-7702, a feature that went live on Ethereum in May 2025.

As of October 30, 2026, no flaw in EIP-7702 itself has been reported.

It did exactly what a valid signature told it to do, and that is the part worth understanding.

Key Takeaways

●      EIP-7702 lets a regular Ethereum wallet run smart contract code, and it went live on May 7, 2025.

●      A token approval covers one token for one app, while an EIP-7702 delegation hands a contract your whole account.

●      Every delegation needs the wallet’s own signature, so the Binance hack looks like a signing-side failure, not an Ethereum bug.

●      Exchange users should watch official notices, withdrawal status, proof of reserves and the post-mortem.

●      Self-custody users only face EIP-7702 risk if they signed a delegation, which Etherscan’s Delegated Address field shows.

●      A delegation can be reset to the zero address, but if you don’t remember signing it, treat the wallet as compromised.

What Is EIP-7702?

EIP-7702 is an Ethereum upgrade that lets a regular crypto wallet, known as an externally owned account or EOA, borrow smart contract code and behave like a smart account.

It shipped with the Pectra upgrade, which went live on Ethereum mainnet on May 7, 2025.

The official specification, EIP-7702: Set Code for EOAs, adds a new transaction type that carries signed “authorizations.”

Each one names a contract and must be signed with the wallet’s own private key.

Our overview of the Ethereum Pectra upgrade covers the rest of that release.

How Does EIP-7702 Work?

It starts with a signature.

You sign an authorization that names a contract, the chain it covers and your account’s nonce, which is simply a running count of your transactions.

Any account can then submit that authorization to Ethereum, which is how a third party can pay the fee for you.

Once it lands, the network writes a small pointer on your account, which the specification calls a delegation indicator.

From then on, calls to your address run the code of the contract it points to, while your private key keeps full control of the account.

To undo it, you sign a new authorization that points to the zero address, and the account goes back to being a plain wallet.

How Does EIP-7702 Bring Account Abstraction to Wallets?

Account abstraction is the push to make Ethereum wallets programmable instead of limited to one signature and one action at a time.

EIP-7702 brings that idea to existing wallets, so nobody has to move funds to a new address.

The specification names three uses:

●      Batching: several actions, such as approve and swap, in one all-or-nothing transaction

●      Sponsorship: another account pays the network fee for you and can be repaid in a token other than ETH

●      Privilege de-escalation: a sub-key with narrow permissions, such as spending tokens but not ETH

Each of these depends on the contract your wallet points to, which is why the next section matters.

How Is an EIP-7702 Delegation Different From a Token Approval?

A token approval lets one app move one token, usually up to a set amount.

An EIP-7702 delegation hands the logic of your entire account to a contract, covering every asset inside it, until you reset it.

That gap in scope is why a bad delegation does far more damage than a bad approval.

Think of your wallet as a house.

A token approval is like handing someone the key to one cabinet to take out one item.

An EIP-7702 delegation is closer to hiring a house manager who runs every room.

A good manager, like a reputable wallet app’s smart account contract, only follows your instructions.

A malicious one can clear out the whole house in one trip.

** **

Your private key stays the master key, which also means anyone holding it can hire a new manager.

What Happened in the Binance EIP-7702 Hack?

On October 29, 2026, an attacker used signed EIP-7702 authorizations to point Binance’s Ethereum hot wallet at the attacker’s own contract.

Minutes later, one transaction emptied it.

Early on-chain estimates put losses across Binance’s wallets in the billions of dollars as of October 30, 2026.

Public on-chain data shows four steps:

●      Sign: Around 20:20 UTC, a batch of authorizations signed with the wallet’s key landed on Ethereum.

●      Point: The wallet’s nonce kept changing, so the batch reportedly covered many values, and the one that matched handed the wallet to the attacker’s contract.

●      Sweep: About three minutes later, one call moved out ETH, stablecoins and dozens of other tokens.

●      Reset:Almost four hours later, the delegation was revoked.
Step one is the key detail: a delegation needs the wallet’s own valid signature, so either the key itself leaked or a system that holds it signed when it should not have.

Was It a Leaked Private Key or a Tricked Signing System?

As of October 30, 2026, Binance had not published a root cause.

Two explanations are on the table, pointing to very different weak spots.

The first is a leaked private key, a familiar failure with a familiar fix: retire the key and lock down everyone who had access.

The second is more unsettling.

Many exchanges route hot wallet signing through automated services that check where funds are going and how much is moving.

An EIP-7702 authorization is not a normal transfer.

It is a short signed message naming a contract, with no recipient or amount for those rules to inspect.

If a signing service approved one without decoding it, the exchange’s own security layer signed away control of the wallet.
Early reports also described funds leaving on blockchains that have no EIP-7702 feature at all, which, if confirmed, would point to the signing side.

Is My Money Still Safe on an Exchange After the Binance Hack?

A hot wallet is only the slice of an exchange’s funds kept online for withdrawals.

Whether customers are made whole depends on the exchange’s reserves and what it formally commits to, such as a protection fund, so trust official updates over social media posts.

Watch for:

●      Announcements on Binance’s own website and verified accounts

●      Deposit and withdrawal status for each network

●      An updated proof of reserves report

●      A post-mortem that names the root cause and the fix

Scammers move fast after big hacks, so check any “refund” link against our guide to spotting crypto scams first.

Proof of reserves is an exchange’s published evidence that its holdings cover what it owes users, and our guide on how to assess whether an exchange’s funds are safe explains how to read one.

Should I Move My Crypto Off an Exchange After a Hack?

A hack at one exchange does not automatically put your funds at risk, and moving them is a personal call.

If you do move some, send a small test amount first and double-check the network and address before the full transfer.

For how often breached exchanges have repaid users, see our data on past exchange hacks.

Does EIP-7702 Put My Own Wallet at Risk?

Not unless you signed a delegation.

Holding ETH in a self-custody wallet does not switch EIP-7702 on, and there is nothing to “upgrade.”

Ethereum’s official site says anyone telling you to “upgrade” your ETH is trying to scam you.

The real risk is phishing.

A fake airdrop or website can ask you to sign an authorization that points your wallet at a malicious smart contract, disguised as a routine prompt.

Etherscan’s guide to EIP-7702 smart accounts, published in June 2025, said over 97% of all delegations were linked to scams targeting vulnerable users.

One more catch: an authorization signed with a chain ID of zero is valid on every EVM chain that supports EIP-7702, as the EIP-7702 guidelines on ethereum.org warn.

How Do I Check and Revoke an EIP-7702 Delegation?

Paste your address into Etherscan and find the Delegated Address field.

If block explorers are new to you, see our guide to how an Ethereum blockchain explorer works.

“Null” means no delegation.

If it shows a contract that isn’t your wallet app’s, reset it from inside the wallet.

●      Step 1: A known wallet contract, such as MetaMask’s EIP-7702 Delegator, simply means you turned on that wallet’s smart account.

●      Step 2: If you don’t recognize the contract, revoke it in your wallet app, which delegates to the zero address.

●      Step 3: Repeat the check on other EVM chains where you use the same address.

●      Step 4: If you don’t remember signing that delegation, assume your key or seed phraseis exposed and move everything to a new wallet.
Revoking won’t rescue a wallet whose key has leaked, because whoever holds the key can sign a fresh delegation a minute later.

Could an EIP-7702 Attack Happen Again?

EIP-7702 is a finalized, live part of Ethereum, so the feature is not going away.

The clearest fix sits with whoever controls signing: systems that decode every authorization and refuse any contract that isn’t pre-approved.

The specification itself says there is no safe way to let apps request delegations freely.

FAQ

Is EIP-7702 a bug in Ethereum?

No, it is an intended feature with Final status, and every delegation needs the wallet’s own signature.

What is the difference between EIP-7702 and ERC-4337?

Both move Ethereum toward account abstraction, but EIP-7702 upgrades an existing wallet while ERC-4337 relies on separate smart contract accounts.

Which Ethereum upgrade added EIP-7702?

EIP-7702 shipped in the Pectra upgrade, which activated on May 7, 2025.

How do I revoke an EIP-7702 delegation?

Sign a new delegation to the zero address from your wallet app, which resets the account to a plain wallet.

Does an EIP-7702 delegation work on other chains?

Only on the chain it names, unless it was signed with chain ID 0, which makes it valid on every EVM chain that supports it.

Was EIP-7702 hacked in the Binance incident?

As of October 30, 2026, no flaw in EIP-7702 had been reported, and the delegation carried the wallet’s own valid signature.

Do I need to do anything with my ETH because of EIP-7702?

No, your ETH needs no upgrade, and you only need to act if you find a delegation you don’t recognize.

Is my money on Binance safe after the EIP-7702 hack?

As of October 30, 2026, Binance had not published a root cause, so rely on its official announcements and proof of reserves rather than social media posts.

The Bottom Line on EIP-7702

EIP-7702 gives ordinary Ethereum wallets real smart account powers, and it also lets one signature hand over an entire account.

The Binance hack showed that power at full scale, with no Ethereum bug reported as of October 30, 2026.

Designing Cloud-Native Extensions on SAP BTP with CAPM: Patterns for Clean-Core Architectures

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Key Takeaways

●  Clean core extensibility is when we, as a design choice, decide to go that way per use case: synchronous OData for transactional use cases that require immediate response, asynchronous events for all other use cases.

●   At the beginning of each CAPM project, multi-tenancy issues should be addressed; retrofitting tenant isolation in a single-tenant already deployed extension is a much more disruptive affair than to include the feature from the word go.

●   Principal propagation does not feature in the design of which extensions preserve core system authorization and audit trails’ integrity versus which ones allow them to degrade silently. 

●  In CAPM extensions the great production risk is not in the framework at all but in the peripheral issues: destination config, XSUAA scope mapping, CDS to core field mapping which go out of sync across environments.

●  Clean core, which reduces upgrade risk in the ERP core but does not remove operational burden; it shifts versioning, monitoring, and authorization complexity into the BTP environment, which in turn requires the same operational care as the core itself.

Executive Summary

SAP’s clean-core strategy tells engineering teams to leave S/4HANA itself largely untouched. Custom logic should run beside it as applications on SAP Business Technology Platform, usually built with the Cloud Application Programming Model (CAPM). That change is not merely structural. Teams now have to deal with distributed-system problems—network requests instead of local function calls, tenant isolation, token-based authorization, and eventual consistency—in a domain long shaped by monolithic customization. This article examines recurring design choices in production CAPM extension projects, including how extensions compose services with the core, how tenants are separated, how SAP Event Mesh carries integration events, and how authorization relies on principal propagation. The discussion comes from hands-on experience designing and building an extension from start to finish, with architects and senior engineers in mind—people who need to make these tradeoffs correctly on the first attempt.

Introduction

For most of the past two decades, extending SAP meant changing the ERP system itself. Teams added ABAP reports, user exits, and Z-tables alongside the vendor’s standard code. The approach worked, but the trade-off was familiar: each custom object could fail during the next upgrade, and the line separating SAP’s code from the company’s own gradually disappeared.


Keep the ERP core to stock standards, and put custom logic into a separate cloud native runtime, SAP BTP, which can be stood up or taken down independent of the core’s release cycle [1]. CAPM is what SAP uses for that outer layer: a domain model-first, convention over configuration approach which compiles Core Data Services (CDS) models into OData services, with persistence, authorization and multitenancy mainly handled by generated code [2].


The problem is simple: “move the logic to BTP” is a slogan, not an architecture. Accepting it leaves a team to settle the details that improvised projects often mishandle: connecting the extension to the core, serving several business units from one codebase without exposing their data to one another, carrying a user’s identity across the network, and monitoring a service beyond the familiar SAP Basis toolchain. Without a deliberate design, teams tend to rely on synchronous calls, technical users rather than propagated authorization, and single-tenant deployments. The second consumer then arrives, and the design has to be rebuilt. These are the same distributed-systems errors organizations make during a first microservices project—only they reach the SAP ecosystem later.


Service design, multi-tenancy, event based integration, and authorization of principals we also look at what goes wrong in production. This article looks at the most important issues: 

Background

Standard configuration and key-user (in-app) extensibility is what we have in S/4HANA; for anything that requires custom code or a new data model we go to side by side extensions on BTP [1], [5]. The RESTful ABAP Programming Model (RAP) is the clean core compatible way to expose logic that must stay in ABAP, which we build on CDS views and which generates OData services with transactional (draft, lock, validation) handling [5], [9].


In the world of side-by-side half we have CAPM which is the model-driven framework. A CAPM project puts forth entities and services in CDS; the framework in turn puts out an OData v4 (or v2) service with default persistence and generic CRUD handlers which also has in it hooks for custom logic in Node.js or Java [2], [4]. Also it is typical for CAPM apps to run on Cloud Foundry or Kyma, which in turn is backed by SAP HANA Cloud, and we also see Fiori elements UIs generated from the same CDS annotations.


SAP Event Mesh is the managed version of the event-broker pattern which we have in BTP middleware that puts together event delivery between producers and subscribers so their availability and load are put out of each other’s business. It is the typical means for asynchronous integration between the core (which is publishing business events) and extensions that may react without polling.

XSUAA, the SAP Authorization and Trust Management service, is BTP’s OAuth2 auth server: it issues and issues JWTs which have in them scopes from role collections and is what CAPM apps use for auth and auth requests [6], [7].

Problem Analysis

Four problems recur across CAPM extension projects, independent of industry:


Where does the transaction boundary sit? A parallel extension is not in the same unit of work as the S/4HANA tables it depends on. What used to be a single local transaction now requires a synchronous call with its own error handling, or an asynchronous flow which includes eventual consistency. Teams that ignore this design step implement synchronous calls into every operation which in turn caps the extension’s availability to that of the core system.


How many tenants does the extension actually serve? In many cases, a single business unit’s extension is also required by a second unit or country within the first year. If tenant isolation was not a primary design issue from the start, then as we go in to retrofit it we are looking at re-provisioning infrastructure and re-testing authorization, which at this point the first tenant is already in production. 


Whose identity is on the wire? Calling S/4HANA through a broad-privilege technical user is easier than setting up identity federation, which explains its popularity. But it creates a serious authorization flaw: everyone shares one credential, so individual checks disappear and the audit trail points to the technical account rather than the person who made the change.


Who is watching the extension once it is live? A CAP service does not inherit the monitoring practices that come with the ABAP stack; a Basis team’s muscle memory for ST22 reports does not translate to Cloud Foundry logs, which in turn must be deliberately wired into an observability pipeline. 

Architecture and Design

In the case of S/4HANA, we maintain minimal custom logic (standard objects, ABAP CDS views, RAP exposed business objects), which in turn has a side-by-side CAPM extension in a BTP subaccount for UI, extended logic, and any data model that does not fit in the ERP schema.


Three decisions define this shape. 


Service composition: The extension connects to the core in two ways. Requests that need an immediate response use OData v2/v4 through the Destination and Connectivity services. Notifications go through SAP Event Mesh instead. In practice, asynchronous communication should be the default; use synchronous calls only when the caller cannot continue without the result, because they tie the extension’s SLA to a system outside its control.


Multitenancy: CAPM's @sap/cds-mtxs package implements SAP's standard pattern — one provider subaccount running the extension, an MTX sidecar handling tenant onboarding/upgrade, and one isolated database container per tenant subaccount [3], [10], [12]. This we see a shared application, isolated data model which is more economical than one deployment per tenant and we keep tenant data separate which is a point of note for the compliance reviewers.


Authorization boundary: XSUAA-issued JWTs authorize the extension's own endpoints; the Destination service, configured for principal propagation, carries the caller's identity through to the core so OData calls execute — and are authorization-checked and logged — as the actual end user [6], [7], [8].

The tradeoff: this trades in-core simplicity for operational surface area — a second deployable, security model, and monitoring domain, in exchange for upgrade independence. That trade is worth making for most net-new logic; it is not automatically worth making for a small, stable customization cheaper to maintain in place, a point returned to below.

Implementation

The following snippets illustrate the patterns above at the level of real project code.

Domain model keyed against a core identifier, not a replica of core data:

// db/schema.cds namespace com.example.extension; using { cuid, managed } from '@sap/cds/common';   entity RiskAssessments : cuid, managed {   salesOrder   : String(10) @title: 'S/4HANA Sales Order';   riskScore : Decimal(3,0);   reviewStatus : String(20) default 'OPEN';   reviewedBy   : String(256);   notes    : String(1000); }


The entity carries a plain salesOrder string rather than an association into a replicated copy of the order table — replicating core master data reintroduces exactly the coupling clean core is meant to remove. The extension resolves the current state on demand instead.


Synchronous read-through to the core via a remote OData service:

// srv/external/S4SalesOrder.cds using { API_SALES_ORDER_SRV as external } from './S4SalesOrder.csn'; service S4Service {   entity SalesOrders as projection on external.A_SalesOrder; } // srv/risk-service.js const cds = require('@sap/cds'); module.exports = cds.service.impl(async function () {   const { RiskAssessments } = this.entities;   this.on('READ', RiskAssessments, async (req, next) => { const results = await next(); const s4 = await cds.connect.to('S4Service'); for (const row of results) {   const order = await s4.run(         SELECT.one.from('SalesOrders').where({ SalesOrder: row.salesOrder })   );   row.orderStatus = order?.OverallSDProcessStatus; } return results;   }); });


cds.connect.to('S4Service') resolves, via configuration, to a destination that carries the inbound user's principal — propagation is configuration, not application code, so the same handler runs correctly whether the destination uses principal propagation or, in a lower environment, a technical user.


Reacting to a core event asynchronously, instead of polling:

// srv/event-listener.js const cds = require('@sap/cds'); module.exports = cds.service.impl(async function () {   const messaging = await cds.connect.to('messaging');   messaging.on('sap.s4.beh.salesorder.v1.SalesOrder.Changed.v1', async (msg) => { const { SalesOrder } = msg.data; await UPDATE(cds.entities.RiskAssessments)   .set({ reviewStatus: 'NEEDS_REVIEW' })   .where({ salesOrder: SalesOrder });   }); });


This handler updates the extension's own state and lets a downstream process act on it — decoupling that keeps the extension available even during a core maintenance window.


Authorization declared next to the model it protects:

// srv/risk-service.cds service RiskService @(requires: 'authenticated-user') {   entity RiskAssessments as projection on extension.RiskAssessments;   annotate RiskAssessments with @( restrict: [   { grant: ['READ'], to: 'RiskViewer' },   { grant: ['*'], to: 'RiskManager' } ]   ); }

RiskViewer/RiskManager map, via xs-security.json, to XSUAA scopes and role collections assigned to real users:

{   "scopes": [ { "name": "$XSAPPNAME.RiskViewer", "description": "Read risk assessments" }, { "name": "$XSAPPNAME.RiskManager", "description": "Manage risk assessments" }   ],   "role-templates": [ { "name": "RiskViewer", "scope-references": ["$XSAPPNAME.RiskViewer"] }, { "name": "RiskManager", "scope-references": ["$XSAPPNAME.RiskManager"] }   ] }


These role templates are what an administrator turns into actual role collections and assigns to users or groups in the BTP cockpit — CDS declares intent, this file is the contract USAA enforces against, and the role collection assignment is the step most likely to drift out of sync with the other two, as the Production Lessons Learned section below discusses.

Performance and Scalability

CAPM applications scale the way most stateless Cloud Foundry/Kyma services do: horizontally, with session state kept out of the application tier. The design questions that matter are elsewhere. Read-through calls to the core are the dominant latency cost, not the extension's own logic — a short-lived cache for infrequently changing master data reduces both, with a TTL reflecting how stale the UI may be for that field. One container per tenant which reduces cross-tenant contention but at the same time does require capacity planning against the largest tenant’s needs. Event consumption throughput is a function of the consumer which in turn means that for bursty events there must be sufficient number of consumer instances to handle the volume within the business’s freshness requirement. For bulk operations use OData’s $batch instead of individual calls in a loop which in turn results in one round trip as opposed to hundreds.

Security Considerations

Security in this architecture is a matter of the full request path maintaining the same guarantees which in turn each individual element of that path is responsible for and not of any single control. 


●  Token validation is automatic; scope design is not. At each point of entry we require a valid XSUAA JWT, also it is CAPM’s job to check signature, expiry, and audience which we do without custom code but still we have to write out xs-security.json scope definitions carefully because a very wide default scope renders CDS @restrict annotations useless [6].

● Principal propagation should be the default backend authentication mode, Not an issue that is addressed only as an after thought [7], [8]; any technical or user fallback should be put in place as a documented reviewed exception, not a silent default.

●  Authorization granularity matters more than authentication. OWASP’s API Security Top 10 puts out broken object level authorization and broken function level authorization as its top priority issues [14] which in turn map directly to CDS @restrict annotations that are too broad, or which are absent at all on a generated navigation property.

●  Secrets live in the platform, not the codebase. Destination credentials and API keys go into BTP’s credential store and injected service bindings which also means they should not be in the CDS model or a checked in config file. 

● Transport security needs attention at both ends. BTP by default enforces TLS 1.2+ on inbound traffic; for outbound connection via the Cloud Connector that is the team’s responsibility as far as certificate rotation goes, not the platform’s.

●  Zero-trust framing fits this architecture naturally. NIST SP 800-207 puts forth zero trust access in terms of per session and identity which is a different approach from that of network location [19] we present this model which by design has the application logging and the core no longer on the same trusted network segment.

Operational Excellence

A CAP service is only as operable as the observability wired around it, and BTP does not wire that in for free.


●  Structured, correlated logging. The Application Logging feature provides for search of logs across instances, but only if correlation IDs travel with each request through every downstream call which includes outbound OData calls to the core [24] without that a single reported issue from a user may not be traced across the full request path.

●  Distributed tracing matters more here than in a typical microservices system, because a meaningful share of "slow" requests are slow in the core, not the extension, and only a trace spanning both sides of the boundary proves which one [21].

●  Alerting should be scoped to actionable symptoms — We see that for elevated error rate, queue backlog, destination authentication failures not all anomalies the platform is technically able to emit; alert fatigue, which leads to practice defeat much faster than any one missed incident.

●   Multitenant upgrade rollout needs a staged path. MTX-powered schema upgrades go out to all enrolled tenants at the same time by default, which is why we require a canary tenant (or a small set) for an upgrade to go through before it is made available to all.

●  HA/DR is the team's own responsibility. For critical business extensions, we need to see a defined RTO/RPO and a proven out-of-band recovery method; living on a cloud platform does not, in and of itself, guarantee resilience.


Trade-offs and Limitations

     Clean core, side-by-side extension architecture is what we should go with for new custom logic implementation, but that is not a hard and fast rule. It introduces a network hit and an availability issue which core customizations did not have; in a use case which requires the same transactional consistency as native ABAP logic, forcing it side by side actually trades off upgrade risk for distributed transaction complexity which may not be a clear win. Also, it is not a free ride; each extension is a second thing to secure, patch, and staff. Multi-tenancy issues are a waste of effort for a tool which is very scoped and truly single-tenant, and event-driven integration trades off speed for resilience, which is the wrong trade-off when the business is looking for a sync answer. Small, stable, rarely used custom codes are often better left as minimal in core extensions, then re-platformed for strategic conformity -- clean core is a direction to move logic towards over time, not a reason to do a big bang migration of everything at once.

Production Lessons Learned

In many cases we saw two failure patterns which reported back often enough in real CAPM extension builds to be worth going into in detail; we thought at first they were application bugs in either of these and in fact they were not.


The "it works in every environment except the one that matters" destination bug. A CAP service may read from S/4HANA without trouble in development and quality assurance, then fail silently—or return a misleading error—when it reaches production. The application code is often fine. More commonly, production has a destination with the wrong authentication setup: basic authentication was configured instead of principal propagation, or a certificate that works in QA was never replaced with the production certificate. Development and QA tests still pass because each one uses its own correctly configured destination. The problem appears only in the environment that no one tests again before go-live. A lasting fix means managing destination configuration like versioned application code. Each change needs review, and every promotion should include a checklist confirming the authentication method and certificate validity for each landscape—not a cockpit setting configured once and then forgotten.


The silent authorization drift between the CDS model and the role collections that were actually assigned. A CDS @restrict is a statement of intent which the actual behavior a user sees is based on the role collection which an admin put together in the identity provider and which two which are edited in different tools by different people at different times. Early in an extension's life the mapping between CDS scopes and role collections is usually correct as one team designed both at the same time. Months down the line after a business request for a new field or operation which a developer then adds to the @restrict annotation which he extends the CDS scope for -- the corresponding role collection update is easy to miss what breaks is not immediate but the new feature just does not show up for anyone which is reported as a "bug in the new feature" instead of being identified as an authorization gap. The best fix is a single mapping document which is owned by someone and reviewed with the security team on a set cadence not just at go live so that a CDS change and its corresponding role collection change are always done together instead of independently.


An issue we see is that what began as a solution for one team grew to require a second and by that point the migration was much more difficult we had wished we had put tenant awareness in from the beginning. Also we note that Fiori elements really do speed up UI delivery when the backend CDS @UI annotations are used as designed teams which ignore this and go to manual UI coding see no benefit at all out of the framework which they may have thought would be the solution for them.

Best Practices

●  Default to async, event based integration for notifications; use sync OData for interactions that require an immediate response. 

●  Multitenancy model to be determined before writing the first line of extension code, which in turn may be for a single tenant launch.

●  Principal propagation to be the default back-end auth mode out of which technical user fall back will be a documented exception.

●   Write out CDS @restrict annotations for what the security team has approved in terms of role collection design, and at a set interval go over that mapping. 

●  For destination and connectivity config, version and review the same care that we put into application code. 

●   Put in place distributed tracing that spans the extension-to-core boundary from the very first production deployment, not only after the first latency issue.

●    Cache core master data reads with an explicit, business-justified TTL.

●   Treat HA/DR posture as an explicit requirement with a stated RTO/RPO, sized to the process's business criticality.

Future Outlook

SAP reports that it is presenting RAP (which is for logic that must stay in ABAP Cloud) and CAPM (which is for logic that belongs side by side) as embodiments of the same clean-core philosophy which we see in the use of CDS as the model language. As cross-system communication trends towards event-based models, extensions which are put together in a synchronous-first approach will see growth in the need to include asynchronous elements. Also, these trends do not change the base principles on which we operate; they, in fact, put into higher value the issues of service composition and multitenancy from the start.

Conclusion

In a side-by-side layer, a team owns end-to-end, not scattered through a vendor-maintained core. CAPM makes that layer a material improvement over hand-rolled middleware, but it does not do away with the underlying distributed systems issues: service composition, tenant isolation, identity propagation, and observability still have to be designed into the layer. Teams which approach those design issues with the same care as any other production distributed system do in fact end up with extensions that are truly upgrade independent; teams which see CAPM as a drag-and-drop solution tend to learn the hard way that these issues did not go away.
