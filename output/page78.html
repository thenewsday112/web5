How WeWork’s latest CEO changed the company—and how it changed him

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When WeWork CEO John Santora assumed leadership in June 2024, he noticed a few wardrobe choices that clashed with his old-school aesthetic.

“It was the middle of June, and [WeWork staffers were wearing] everything from jeans to pajamas and slippers and shorts and so forth,” he told Business Insider.

Santora, who spent 47 years at Chicago-based commercial real estate giant Cushman & Wakefield, decided the dress code needed some changes. “There are a lot more sport jackets around the office,” he said.

And Santora has learned to loosen up a bit. “On Fridays, I’ll actually sometimes wear sneakers,” he said. “I’m a suit-and-tie, buttoned-up real estate person, but I’m good now.”

Santora took the helm of WeWork just as it emerged from Chapter 11 bankruptcy, following years of financial turmoil and controversy under cofounder Adam Neumann. 

The once-high-flying co-working company had become a cautionary tale of unchecked growth, reaching a $47 billion valuation in 2019 before collapsing under the weight of costly leases, mounting losses, and a failed initial public offering. WeWork’s bankruptcy restructuring, which was completed shortly before Santora arrived, eliminated more than $4 billion in debt and reduced its future rent obligations by about $12 billion.

Santora has since sought to transform the company into a more disciplined, financially sustainable business while preserving its fundamental model, which he still sees as a valuable alternative to traditional corporate real estate.

One change was to give regional presidents responsibility for profitability, as opposed to just sales and operations. He has also tweaked the company’s design, nudging it away from the informal, loud aesthetics of its early days toward a quieter, more corporate style. 

But the biggest challenge has been restoring trust from employees, clients, and landlords. Santora has held meetings with workers to explain his plans and assure them that the company has sufficient capital to meet payroll and rent obligations. For clients and landlords, he has drawn on many of the relationships he built over decades at Cushman & Wakefield.

WeWork returned to profitability in 2025, reporting $2.3 billion in revenue, and Santora thinks it is poised to thrive amid current economic uncertainty.

“Not knowing how much space you need or how many employees you’re going to have—that is kind of in our sweet spot,” Santora said. At present, about 300 artificial intelligence companies occupy WeWork spaces worldwide, ranging from tiny startups to large brand-name businesses, he noted.

For Santora, rebuilding the company has meant balancing structure and discipline with the versatility that made the company appealing in the first place. That balancing act has taught him something he hadn’t fully appreciated: the importance of being flexible.

“I mean, I saw it, but now I live it. And I see how important it is,” he said.

Imagine asking an AI agent to book a business-class flight to Korea. It finds a bargain. The ticket fits your budget. There is just one problem: it has picked the wrong Korea.

Daniel Ishag, Bakkt’s chief commercial officer, recounts a version of this hypothetical from a panel discussion.

“How do I know the agent’s not going to book me a flight to North Korea instead of South Korea?” he asks.

The example is deliberately extreme. But it exposes a practical problem with giving software permission to spend. Following a budget does not mean understanding the job.

And once the money moves, somebody has to deal with the mistake.

A Budget is Only the Beginning

An agent might misunderstand a request. An attacker might feed it instructions disguised as useful information. Someone might steal access to its account. 

These failures can produce the same immediate result—a payment the owner never wanted—while raising different questions about responsibility.

BeInCrypto’s State of AI Agent Payments 2026 classified 14 of 19 reviewed products and workflows as able to act within permissions approved in advance. That finding describes documented capabilities, not proof that the controls cannot fail. 

Separately, two selected incidents examined in the report sent $281,200 to attackers before recoveries. Malicious instructions reached funded wallets without breaking the blockchain.

Earlier in May 2026, an AI Agent Mistakenly Triggered a $175,000 Transfer to the Wrong Address. Source: BeInCrypto Research

Phillip Lord, CEO of Bitcoin Japan Corporation, expects agents to introduce more risk. He is also blunt about the human alternative.

“But crypto is pretty bloody risky already, right?” he says. “Humans are not that good, right?”

People already click dangerous links and trust bad information. Delegating the task adds another decision: whose mistake will it be when the software acts on your behalf?

The Pressure to Keep Building

In Ishag’s telling, the competition extends beyond companies seeking customers. Countries are competing for the electricity, computing power, and chips needed to build AI.

“It’s sovereign against sovereign,” says Daniel Ishag from Bakkt. 

He worries that the pressure to move quickly can push security aside. 

That creates an uncomfortable setting for financial automation: the race rewards getting software to do more, while users need confidence that someone can stop it—or help them recover afterward.

Read the Promise, Then the Terms

Ehud Tal, co-founder and CEO of blockchain security company Sailo Technologies, presents reimbursement as part of his company’s answer.

“So we provide our customers for the first time ever a money-back guarantee.”

For a concrete example of how responsibility can be assigned, consider Stripe’s Link wallet. Its current agent terms say users are responsible, as between themselves and Stripe, for transactions initiated by connected agents—even when those transactions are unintended or result from bugs, hallucinations, or misunderstood instructions.

The terms make an exception for Stripe’s own technical failure or error.

That contractual position does not settle every consumer’s legal rights. It does show why permission to use a payment service should not be mistaken for a promise that the provider will cover an agent’s errors.

Approving a $200 spend tells a payment provider something about the amount. It may say much less about the product, recipient, or conditions you had in mind. 

After a purchase goes wrong, that difference can become the heart of the dispute.

There are efforts to make those disputes easier to resolve. Google’s Agent Payments Protocol describes signed records connecting a user’s instructions, approved purchase, and payment. Such records could help establish what was authorized. They do not themselves promise reimbursement.

Existing responsibilities also remain. The UK’s Financial Conduct Authority says regulated firms remain responsible for consumer outcomes when using AI, and warns that human review can become a box-ticking exercise.

For anyone funding an agent, the useful questions are therefore specific. Which purchases can it make? What record preserves your instructions? Can you stop further spending? And who handles a payment you challenge?

“We’ve got to build companies that people can trust,” says Phillip Lord from Bitcoin Japan Corporation.

For customers, that trust will be tested after the sale: when an agent makes a mistake, and somebody has to decide who gets their money back.

In the third diagram, I am one of the many points sending arrows into the SSOT. I am also one of the points receiving arrows from it. When I begin the day by reading a briefing prepared by an agent, I occupy structurally the same position as the agents that receive context from that same brain before beginning their work.

What makes something the original?

When we call something the original, the grounds usually fall into one of three categories: it existed first, it contains more, or everything else came from it.

I tried them one by one.

Of the four rows, the only one in which I come out ahead is the last. Yet having existed first is a weaker ground than it seems. Parents exist before their children, but are not their children’s originals. A first draft exists before a finished work, but we call the finished version the work.

A thousand bodies

Let us push the thought experiment one step further.

There are a thousand bodies sharing the same SSOT. Nine hundred and ninety-nine are machines, and one is biological. All thousand begin with the same memories, judge according to the same values, and return to the same brain at the end of the day to pool their experiences.

From the outside, this looks like a single being with a thousand bodies. To point to one of those bodies and say, “That is the real one,” we would have to identify something unique to it.

Memory will not do: all thousand have the same memories. Neither will values, judgment, or the capacity to act. We have already seen that in the table above.

Then why am I, the biological one, the original?

Greece's AI Strategy: How DAEDALUS and PHAROS Could Shape Its Future

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

DAEDALUS's inauguration in Lavrion on 19 October 2026 brings Greece's immediate AI policy question into sharper focus: how will institutions use the new computing base? EuroHPC and GRNET point to a delivered supercomputing installation, following earlier procurement and benchmark milestones. [1] This creates an opportunity for researchers, businesses and public organisations to attempt work that previously required different access arrangements. Its strategic significance will emerge through the projects that can use it reliably and the institutions able to retain the resulting expertise.

The most plausible Greek role is selective. A country does not need to lead every part of the AI system to build useful capabilities. It does need to choose applications where computing resources, relevant data and responsible organisations can be brought together. For Greece, PHAROS identifies health, culture and language, and sustainability as priority domains. [2] These provide a focus for assessing outcomes rather than treating every new AI activity as evidence of national progress.

Establishing what has been delivered

The DAEDALUS timeline deserves precision. EuroHPC launched the tender in June 2024. EuroHPC announced the signed contract with Hewlett Packard Enterprise on 28 March 2025. In June 2026, the system appeared in the TOP500 ranking, while EuroHPC still described wider European availability as forthcoming. The October inauguration is a further milestone. [3–5, 1] These events establish different things: procurement, contractual commitment, benchmark performance and public inauguration should remain distinct in the record.

At inauguration, EuroHPC reported sustained performance of 85.69 petaflops and peak performance of 115 petaflops. The system uses HPE infrastructure and more than 2,000 NVIDIA GH200 Grace Hopper Superchips. [1] Neither performance figure measures the quality of an AI model or the effectiveness of a public service. Benchmark capacity becomes useful to an organisation only when it has access, suitable software and people able to formulate and execute the work.

Hosting, access and responsibility

DAEDALUS is operated by GRNET within an international arrangement. The procurement documentation states that access is jointly managed by EuroHPC and the DAEDALUS consortium in proportion to investment. The acquisition cost was €36 million, with 35% funded by EuroHPC and 65% through the EU-financed Greece 2.0 recovery plan; Cyprus, Montenegro and North Macedonia also participate in the consortium. [1, 4]

This arrangement broadens the resources and relationships available to Greece. It also means that the machine's location does not give every Greek institution unrestricted or immediate access. Ministries, universities and firms need to understand the relevant allocation routes, eligibility conditions and support arrangements. A national strategy should therefore examine who can obtain useful computing time, how long applications take and whether users can continue their work after an initial project.

External technological dependence also remains material. Hardware supplied by HPE and NVIDIA brings substantial capabilities, while tying parts of the system's operation and renewal to those suppliers. [1] The policy response should concern support continuity, staff competence and credible options for future workloads. Owning or hosting infrastructure cannot by itself remove dependence on the organisations that design and maintain essential components.

PHAROS as the connection to use

The distinction between Invention, Industrialisation and Operationalisation helps locate the task. Invention includes the development of methods, models and specialised knowledge. Industrialisation supplies the infrastructure and repeatable technical processes needed to work at scale. Operationalisation concerns the sustained use of those capabilities in organisations. Governance connects the layers by determining access, data permissions, funding and accountability.

PHAROS describes its purpose as connecting academia, industry and the public sector through computing infrastructure, services and expertise. [2] Its importance lies in that organisational connection. A research group may need help adapting software; a small firm may need technical guidance; a public agency may need a clear procedure for using data and evaluating results. These requirements differ, even when users share the same machine.

For Greek-language applications, a plausible objective is better performance on particular public or professional tasks, supported by transparent evaluation. For environmental applications, it could be tools that improve a defined monitoring or planning process. In health, an initial priority could be carefully governed research workflows. These are proposed directions, not claims that PHAROS has already delivered those outcomes. Each requires an accountable institution and a plan for continuing the service after development funding ends.

How to judge the next phase

Four indicators would make progress observable. First, published access and utilisation records should show which categories of users obtain computing time and whether access meets their needs. Second, completed projects should report task-specific results, including failures and costs. Third, repeated use by public bodies and firms should reveal whether initial demonstrations become sustained activity. Fourth, staff retention and maintained software should show whether technical knowledge remains available within the organisations using it.

These indicators would also help direct resources. Heavy demand without sufficient support would suggest an implementation bottleneck. Available capacity with few viable projects would point towards weaknesses in project preparation, data access or institutional demand. The appropriate response would differ in each case. Aggregate computing performance cannot diagnose these problems.

Greece can develop a meaningful role as a location for selected AI applications and regional collaboration if it converts the new infrastructure into dependable institutional capability. DAEDALUS provides a stronger starting point, and PHAROS offers a route for organising its use. The decisive evidence will be organisations that can solve relevant problems, maintain their systems and exercise responsibility for the consequences.

After Josh Jacobs was arrested, three other women told police he had abused them, too

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

While police were investigating Josh Jacobs following a domestic violence accusation in May, multiple other women reported similar incidents of Jacobs abusing them.

NBC 26 in Green Bay received previously unreleased files in connection with the Jacobs case from the Hobart-Lawrence Police Department late Friday, and those files showed that officers heard from at least three other women who said they had been victims of violence at the hands of Jacobs.

One woman contacted police in June after hearing of Jacobs' May arrest, saying she had been the victim of a similar incident with Jacobs years earlier and was reporting it to police for the first time because she wanted people to believe the woman accusing him in May. That woman said that in January of 2022, Jacobs slapped her across the face, grabbed her by the hair, threw her onto a bed, pinned her down, threw her onto the floor, spit on her and grabbed her by the throat. She also said Jacobs took her phone as she attempted to leave his home.

Another woman also contacted police later in June and said that in April of 2021, Jacobs grabbed her by the arms, pushed her against a wall near a window and threw her belongings outside the home. She said she suffered bruising to her arms and pain and swelling in her rib area. She said she had reported it to the police in a different municipality, and the records released by Hobart-Lawrence Police to NBC 26 indicate that they confirmed with another police department that it did receive that report, but Jacobs apparently was not arrested because the woman had no visible signs of injury.

A third woman told Hobart/Lawrence Police in July that in May of 2022, Jacobs placed his hands on her head and neck, dragged her, scratched her chest, and took her phone. She said she suffered bruising to her arm and scratches to her chest. She said she did not report the incident to police or seek medical attention because Jacobs had told her she could be deported if she had any contact with the authorities.

Jacobs eventually pleaded no contest to misdemeanor battery and criminal damage to property in connection with the May arrest. The NFL placed him on paid leave for the first six games of the season, then allowed him to play starting in Week 7. Jacobs remains subject to a potential NFL suspension for violating the league's personal conduct policy, and the accusations brought by the three other women could be considered by the NFL in determining league discipline.

Night on a Spooky Planet

Explanation: What spooky planet is this? Planet Earth on a dark and stormy night, of course. The ghostly scene was photographed at Hverir, a geothermally active area found along northeastern Iceland’s volcanic landscape. Triggered by solar activity, geomagnetic storms have unleashed energetic particles trapped in Earth’s magnetic field. Penetrating the planet’s upper atmosphere at high latitudes, the particles create auroral displays in the starry night sky. Ghostly towers of steam and gas are venting from local fumaroles to dance against the eerie, greenish northern lights. For now, auroral activity on planet Earth remains in a high state as the Sun is only in early phases of decline from the peak of its solar activity cycle. And soon, other ghostly apparitions may dance in your neighborhood. Happy Halloween!
