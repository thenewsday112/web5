Creating AI value with enterprise context

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Across a business, any number of AI platforms, systems, and tools may be at work simultaneously—for example, AI-powered customer platforms, conversational assistants, and workflow and process automation tools. Each one is sophisticated, advanced, and quick to respond. But, when set to work, the outcomes and actions that emerge may be wrong, confused, or lacking a proper understanding of the business. Many organizations find themselves in this situation—and it could be one of key reasons they struggle to extract returns on their AI investments.

An MIT Technology Review Insights poll finds that while every organization surveyed is using AI today to some degree, fewer than one in five (16%) are extracting transformative value from their deployments. The largest percentage of respondents (40%) say they are seeing moderate gains in specific tasks or teams, while 21% report meaningful gains, leaving 23% that are seeing minimal returns or none at all.

Companies are mostly still piloting AI use cases (according to 38% of respondents) or using it to assist employees’ work with tools like co-pilots or chat assistants (35%). Just 12% of enterprises have AI agents in production and 3% say they are using AI to do most of the work in key processes.

“There’s a big gap between what the models are capable of and how the models are actually being useful inside organizations,” says Prukalpa Sankar, co-founder of Atlan. “For every real-life intent and purpose, artificial general intelligence is already here. It’s just not really useful in most companies today.”

The missing layer, Sankar says, is context, or how well AI understands a business. A context layer is the architectural infrastructure that sits between an organization’s business systems (for example, systems of record and data, and its AI platforms). It translates enterprise intelligence into governed, machine-usable context for AI agents or models and helps knowledge compound. “Without a context layer for AI, it’s almost like you’re hiring the smartest intern in the world, blindfolding them, and throwing them into your organization,” Sankar says.

6 ways to make homework less of a battle when your kid has ADHD

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Tonight’s homework is a single worksheet. It’s been 50 minutes. Your kid has sharpened every pencil in the house, visited the bathroom twice, and is now face-down on the floor insisting it’s impossible. You can feel your patience slipping by the second.

If homework battles turn your evenings into a standoff, you’re far from alone, especially if your child has ADHD. About 1 in 9 US kids have been diagnosed with the condition , and the American Academy of Pediatrics says it can profoundly affect a child’s academic achievement. For families managing ADHD, the after-school stretch is often the hardest part of the day.

There’s a reason those hours are so trying. By the time a child with ADHD gets home,  they’ve spent “six or seven hours working harder than their classmates to sit still and focus,” says Michael Morse, MD, a board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrist who directs the ADHD & Anxiety Center at Alvord Baker and is an associate professor of psychiatry at Georgetown University. That effort is used up, and for many kids the medication has worn off too, so “the after-school brain is often a tired brain.”

The resistance usually isn’t a matter of defiance. Executive function, the set of skills needed for getting started, planning and keeping track of steps, develops more slowly in kids with ADHD. That’s why a child can freeze in front of an assignment they’re fully capable of completing, and why the thing that helps is making the first step smaller rather than pushing harder.

Related 6 ways to make mornings smoother when your child has ADHD

Here are six ways Morse says parents can make homework less of a fight for everyone.

1. Don’t start homework the minute they arrive home

The worst time to start homework is the moment your child walks through the door. The school day has already drained the same systems homework demands, so Morse suggests framing the immediate after-school window as a time to refuel. “A snack with some protein comes first, because after-school hunger makes both focus and mood worse,” he says. Movement comes next, whether that’s riding a bike or walking the dog, which burns off the day’s tension and helps many kids with ADHD settle. Make the break predictable with a clear end, and pick a consistent homework time that comes after the break and well before your child is too fried to think.

Most importantly, hold off on screens. “The transition from screens to homework can be particularly painful,” Morse says, so it’s usually easier not to turn them on after school at all.

2. Shrink the first step

“A task feels too big when a child can’t see where it starts,” Morse says. Many kids with ADHD freeze in front of a big assignment because their brain can’t break it into steps on its own, so the task is to shrink the first step until it feels easy. 

Say a book report is due in two weeks. Sit down together and have your child break it into pieces: choose the book, read one chapter, jot notes on the main character. Then put each piece on a calendar so the deadline isn’t, in Morse’s words, “a cliff at the end.” For a single night’s work, 10 math problems can become two sets of five with a break in between. And when your child finishes a piece, say so out loud, since that small win makes the next one easier to start. (Younger kids need the pieces smaller and the check-ins more frequent.)

3. Coach the work instead of enforcing it

“Players usually appreciate coaches who run laps with them,” Morse says. He encourages parents to be “a coach and a model, not an enforcer.” In practice that can look like sitting at the kitchen table with your own work, while your child does theirs. A calm adult nearby helps many kids with ADHD get started and keep going. What a coach doesn’t do is finish the work so it comes back perfect, “because then the teacher never sees what the child can really do,” Morse says. The job is to help plan the night and praise the effort while leaving the outcome up to your child.

4. Offer two choices to sidestep the standoff

When homework turns into a power struggle, handing a little control back to your child can often defuse it. Offer two options you’re happy with either way, like “Math or reading first?” or “Before dinner or after, but before screens.” “A choice gives a child some control,” Morse says, which can take the fight out of the room before it starts. (If homework is still a daily battle no matter what you try, Morse notes a tutor can often take the coaching role with less friction than a parent.)

5. Know when to call it for the night

Some nights, the right move is to stop. “A parent should call it when the homework is no longer teaching anything or when substantial conflict emerges,” Morse says. If your child is in tears, exhausted or stuck on the same problem despite your help, more pushing won’t produce learning. “It mostly produces a worse night and a child who dreads the next one,” he says. When you stop, have your child’s back by sending the teacher a short note about how long they worked and where they got stuck. Most teachers would rather know.

“Your relationship with your child is more important than any assignment,” Morse says, and that relationship is what carries a kid through years of school. Kids with ADHD already hear a lot of criticism, and “if homework turns every evening into a battle, home stops feeling like the place where they’re accepted.”

6. Loop in the teacher when it’s bigger than effort

Some homework problems are too big to solve at the kitchen table. “I’d start advocating when the problem looks bigger than effort,” Morse says. Watch for homework that routinely takes far longer than the teacher intended, work your child understands but can’t get written down or turned in, or nights that keep ending in tears. Those point to a gap between what the assignment asks and what your child can do right now, and pushing harder at home won’t close it. 

Start by telling the teacher how long the work is actually taking. Some kids need more formal support through a 504 plan or an individualized education program, the school plans that put accommodations in writing. This matters especially for girls, Morse says, “whose late-night effort can keep their grades up and hide how much they’re struggling.”

As kids get older, Morse’s advice is to hand over the wheel gradually, with middle school as the moment to watch, when additional teachers and long-term projects arrive just as parents tend to step back. Teach your child to use a planner, check in briefly each evening, and let them take on more as they show they can. The long-term goal is teaching a kid which tasks will always need a system or a helper, because “knowing when to ask for help is one of the most valuable skills a teenager can carry into adult life.”

That need goes well beyond ADHD. Accountability partners and body doubles, “people whose quiet company helps you start and stay on task,” work for nearly everyone. “Not everyone knows to ask,” Morse says.

Stacked loads work best at mid-range, where TSS and steel pellets work together to increase hit probability. They are not long-range loads. TSS/steel blends like Apex TSS/Steel 2/7 ½ deliver more pellets and better penetration than straight steel at comparable price points, making them a practical upgrade for most waterfowl hunters.

  • Stacked loads excel at mid-range. TSS and steel work together there, not independently at separate distances.
  • TSS/steel blends deliver more pellets per payload than straight steel with equal or better penetration.
  • At long range, denser TSS outruns steel and the load strings out. Avoid stacked loads for extended shots.
  • Mixing a quarter-ounce of TSS into a 1¼-ounce payload keeps cost manageable without sacrificing pellet count.
  • Pattern board results from a Modified choke at 40 yards showed near 100 percent efficiency for TSS/steel blends.

Stacked loads are back. Last time around, in the 1990s, they were called “duplex” loads. They mixed two sizes of steel or two sizes of lead in turkey loads. Federal even briefly loaded a “Tri-Power” shell with three shot sizes. Duplex loads lasted a little while, then were dismissed as novelties. Now they’re back, and for a couple of reasons.

The first is that tungsten-iron and bismuth shot are expensive. The cost of the raw shot material is what makes premium non-toxics so high-priced. One way to lower the cost of premium non-toxics is to mix it with a pinch of steel. That’s how we got the original Hevi-Metal, which combined Hevi-Shot and steel, several years ago. That’s also the thinking behind Apex TSS/Steel, Federal Black Cloud TSS, Migra stacked loads, Hevi-Metal Xtreme, and others. At the same time, stacked all-steel loads, similar to the original Duplex loads of the 90s, are back. There seems to be a sound ballistic reason behind them, too. Here’s what you need to know if you’re considering stacked loads this season.

Many waterfowl hunters continue to make the switch to stacked loads. (Photo/Weatherby)

Premium Shot/Steel Blends

I’ve had a chance to shoot some of these, both at paper and at feathers. When used the right way, they can be very effective. I shot quite a bit of the old Hevi-Metal without complaint ten years ago, and while it worked, the new TSS/steel blends I have tried pattern much better. The high density of TSS means you can mix a quarter ounce of TSS shot into a 1 ¼-ounce payload and get a lot more pellets, yet the shells remain relatively affordable.

Take Apex TSS/Steel 2/7 ½, for example. There are 125 pellets in an ounce of steel. Add to that the 55 pellets you get with a quarter-ounce of TSS 7 ½s, and you have a load with 180 pellets. A straight 1 ¼ ounce load of steel contains 156 pellets. So the TSS/Steel load gives you more pellets, and the 7 ½s have at least the same penetration as the 2s.

On the pattern board, I got 150 hits in the 30-inch circle from a Modified choke at 40 yards. That’s the equivalent of a near 100 percent pattern with a comparable load of straight steel 2s. The hits from the 2s and 7 ½s were easy to tell apart. In my tests, all 55 TSS pellets landed inside the 30-inch circle, and most landed inside a 20-inch circle.

The laws of physics are immutable; bad things happen at longer ranges with blended loads. The more aerodynamic, denser TSS pellets outrun the lighter, bulkier steel, and the load strings out. These are not loads made for long-range shooting. And that brings us to the biggest misconception hunters have with these loads. Many believe “the steel is for short-range shots, and the TSS is for long shots.” Wrong. These loads excel at mid-range, where the TSS and steel work together to give you a much higher hit probability. At home, I shot the 2/7 ½s at ducks and a few geese that found my duck ponds with very good results.

These would be ideal loads for shooting at decoying ducks in flooded corn, which is some of the worst stuff for people and dogs to chase down crippled ducks. For long-range shooting, shoot all one size of bigger shot through a tight choke.

The author shot this goose with Kent Fasteel + BB/2 loads. (Photo/Phil Bourjaily)

Stacked Steel

Steel stacked loads, now offered by Kent and Remington, work differently. While premium/steel loads have bigger steel shot on the bottom and smaller tungsten or bismuth pellets on top, with steel you load the smaller shot first, then layer the bigger shot on top. Kent’s Jeff Barry told me that in their testing, the then-new Fasteel + ammo produced better patterns. The smaller pellets drafted behind the bigger pellets, he claimed, like NASCAR racers drafting behind semi-trucks. The smaller shot stayed in the pattern longer, getting a free ride behind the big pellets, which did the hard work of fighting air resistance. The result, he told me, was that the stacked steel load of, say, BBs and 2s actually put more pellets on target despite having lower pellet counts than straight 2s.

I did not say “yeah, right” out loud, but I wasn’t sure the blends would make a significant difference. It’s a good thing I kept my mouth shut because on the pattern board, the BB/2 Fasteel + ammo performed exactly as advertised. Due to the drafting effect Barry described, they put more pellets on target than straight 2s. Better yet, because some of those pellets were BBs, the pattern had more total energy than a pattern of all-2 shot would.

In the field, I found them to be a great load for days when the forecast called for mallards with a chance of geese. It’s not that I can’t shoot decoying geese with 2s; I have, plenty of times. But I can shoot them better when there are some BBs mixed in to anchor those big 10- to 13-pound giants, yet the load still has enough pattern density for ducks.

Lacking a high-speed camera, I can’t tell you at what distance the BBs outrun the 2s, but given the drafting effect, I would suspect this pattern stays together for a while, then ultimately strings out. Like the pricier tungsten-steel blends, this stuff is made for hitting birds at medium range harder, not for pass shooting. To use these effectively in the field, stay within the limitations of the smaller shot size in the shell. Do that, and you might find these shells stack the odds in your favor.

Solo Trekker Flouts Khumbu Rules, Evacuated After Cliff Fall

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

A 26-year-old Australian solo trekker who fell from a cliff near Lobuche in the Everest region has been evacuated by helicopter to Kathmandu. He is recovering in intensive care. By law, all foreign trekkers are supposed to have a guide in the Khumbu.

Joshua Patrick Cherubino sustained severe head injuries after falling at Kongma La Pass. He was first taken to Lobuche, then flown out on October 27, according to The Tourism Times.

Kongma La, at about 5,535m, is the highest of the trio of high passes on the classic Everest Three Passes circuit. It links the Chhukung side of the Imja Valley with Lobuche over rocky, non-glaciated moraine. The crossing is long and remote; the descent toward Lobuche is often loose underfoot and comes after hours already spent at altitude.

The case has again drawn attention to Nepal’s rules in the Khumbu. Since 2023, the Nepal Tourism Board has required that all foreign trekkers be accompanied by a licensed guide within national parks, including Sagarmatha.

In practice, the local Khumbu Pasang Lhamu Rural Municipality has continued to issue its own permits and has not strictly enforced the national guide requirement. Thus, independent trekking remains common on the main Everest routes. Officials quoted by The Tourism Times described unguided travel in the region as a violation of the mandatory rule and a source of preventable rescues.

Cherubino was traveling alone when he was found. No further details on the circumstances of the fall or his condition have been released beyond the hospital’s assessment that he is recovering.

In this installment of Home 2.0, we dive into reverse osmosis filtration for the kitchen, its benefits and hidden costs — plus which households should invest in the technology, and who can skip it.

I’ve lived in some places with questionable drinking water. One town issued a notice that the municipal water supply had experienced a sewage incident. Another had an arsenic problem. I’ve also lived on well water, with concerns of contamination from nearby agricultural fields.

My solution used to be buying five-gallon jugs of spring water. But that was costly and a hassle, so six years ago, I switched to an under-sink reverse osmosis (RO) filter for my drinking and cooking water.

Under-sink RO filters work like this: water passes through a series of filters, including an RO membrane, which removes dissolved substances — including lead, arsenic, nitrates, forever chemicals, chlorine, fluoride and sediment — down to 0.0001 microns. The cleaned water then comes out through a faucet, installed near the sink.

“Reverse osmosis provides the safest level of drinking water possible,” says international water filtration specialist Andrew D. Daily. “It strips out 100% of bacteria, cysts, dissolved solids, chlorine, rust, viruses and microplastics.”

But RO systems are also a considerable investment. So, we asked a range of health and plumbing experts for their thoughts on who should get an RO system, plus how to pick the right one. Ahead is what they said, how much RO systems cost up front and over time, and whether it’s worth the upgrade.

Upfront Costs of RO Systems

RO water systems range from a few hundred dollars to over $1,000. The good news is that if you have basic plumbing knowledge and are comfortable drilling a hole in your countertop, installing one can be a DIY task. Otherwise, pro installation usually runs upwards of $300, not including add-ons like a refrigerator/ice-maker connection, leak protection, an additional electric outlet and other plumbing modifications.

Operating Costs of RO Systems

The biggest downside to RO systems is that they waste water. For every gallon that comes out of the faucet, somewhere between one-half and four gallons get flushed down the drain through the filtration process. That means if you use 5 gallons of water a day for cooking and drinking, the RO system wastes between 2.5 and 20 gallons.

Over a year, that adds up to between 500 and 8,500 gallons, depending on your RO system. On the low end, that’s only a few pennies more on your yearly water utility bill, but on the high end, it could be close to $100.

  • Pro-tip: To reduce water waste, choose an EPA WaterSense-certified system.

Some RO water systems also require electricity. The amount of power they consume varies by model, but usually falls between 20 and 40 watts per hour. Since RO systems typically run for less than an hour a day, even if you live in a place with high electricity rates, they should add only a few dollars a year to your electricity bill.

Maintenance Costs of RO Systems

Maintaining RO systems is an easy DIY task, though some people choose to pay a pro to keep track of it for them. Expect to spend roughly the following for DIY maintenance:

  • Filters: $30-$100, every six to 12 months
  • RO membrane: $25-$60, every couple of years
  • Holding tank sanitizer: $20 every year
  • Checking tubing and fittings for leaks: $0, every year

Daily recommends buying only name-brand filters, even though they’re more expensive. “Name brands follow strict regulations,” he says. ”Generic China-based deals often do not provide the same protection.”

Longevity

A quality RO system can last 10 years or longer with proper maintenance. “Many parts, including filters, membranes, faucets and tanks can be replaced individually,” says plumbing expert Danielle Smith. “Once a system gets older, though, replacement may make more sense than repeated repairs.”

What Long-Term Users Say

“The biggest thing for me is convenience,” says environmental and wellness expert Helen Christoni, who has an AquaTru system. “We use water all day long, drinking it, cooking with it and filling water bottles. Once installed, it just became part of my kitchen and my family’s daily health.”

Herman Hartanto, founder of Tampa Fast Home Buyer, has a Waterdrop G3P600 in his kitchen ($429 retail). It’s a tankless model, a feature he appreciates since it takes up minimal cabinet space. He also likes the display with real-time reports on total dissolved solids (TDS) and filter health. His only complaint is the occasional noise it generates. “The pump that draws the water on demand makes quite loud noise, which I can feel clearly at night when everything is silent,” he says.

Personally, I’ve used two RO filter systems. My first one was an iSpring, which I chose because of its affordability. It worked well, but like Hartanto, I found the pump noisy (even though mine had a water-holding tank). My second one, which I’ve only recently installed, is a Waterdrop X12 Pro, which I chose to upgrade to because of its water-saving efficiency, ability to add calcium and magnesium back into the water, clear display and range of other premium features. So far, I’ve found it to be quiet, efficient, and free of quirks (though at $1,400 off-sale, the price is a bit of a shocker).

Alternatives to an Under-Sink RO Water Filter

Depending on what type of contaminants you have in your water, there are several alternatives to an under-sink RO water filter:

  • Under-sink ultrafiltration (UF) systems reduce microorganisms, retain dissolved minerals, and cost significantly less, but don’t remove a full range of dissolved chemicals, heavy metals and other contaminants.
  • Countertop and pitcher filters, like the Waterdrop CT01 and PT-72W Pitcher, improve taste and reduce chlorine, rust and sediment, but generally don’t remove many other contaminants.
  • Whole-house systems treat all of a home’s water, instead of just one faucet and are best for removing rust and sediment, softening water and making it taste better. Some, like Clearsource, also remove viruses and microplastics.
  • Countertop RO systems, like the AquaTru Carafe and the Waterdrop M6SL, are great for renters and students, as they offer the same filtration benefits of under-sink models, but without the need for permanent installation.

How to Choose an RO System

  • Have your water tested to identify which contaminants you need to address, then look for a system specifically certified to remove them.
  • Between models, compare water efficiency, space requirements, water pressure, display options, electricity and drainage requirements, flow rate, replacement filter costs, warranty and parts availability.
  • Decide whether you need helpful minerals, like calcium and magnesium, added back to the water after it’s filtered.
  • Consider upgrade features like a UV lamp, filter-life indicators, water-quality and flow-rate sensors and leak detection.
  • Decide between tank vs. tankless. Tankless filters usually cost more and require electricity (some tank models do as well), but they take up less space, often provide higher flow rates and reduce concerns about stagnant stored water.

“Find out what’s in your water before buying anything,” says Smith. “Don’t choose a system based only on how many stages it has or how many contaminants the box says it removes. Instead, start with your water and choose the system around what you actually need.”

Who Needs an Under-Sink RO Water Filter?

If you’re worried about contaminants like microplastics, arsenic, lead, forever chemicals, pesticides and pharmaceuticals, then an RO filter is a good choice.

“Water quality can vary tremendously depending on where you live, and even your home’s plumbing can affect what ultimately comes out of your faucet,” says Christoni. “Reverse osmosis addresses such a broad range of contaminants, and with an under-sink system, that purified water is available all day long without having to think about it.”

For Daily, the biggest reason to invest in a RO system is microplastics. “The amount of microplastics found in human brains, blood and organs have been increasing yearly,” he says. “Although there is not enough information to prove a detrimental effect on our health, many of my clients choose a cautious approach to water treatment.”

Otherwise, Daily says that RO filters are overkill in most U.S. cities, where tap water is generally considered safe. Even in Mexico, where water is often contaminated, he usually recommends sticking with under-sink ultrafiltration systems, since they’re a fraction of the cost, don’t waste water and don’t require power or space for a reservoir.

Is the Upgrade Worth the Cost?

If you’re currently buying bottled water, then yes, an RO system will soon pay for itself. But, otherwise, it really just depends on what you want to remove from your water. If you have arsenic, nitrates, salts, microplastics, fluoride, sulfates and other dissolved solids (TDS), RO systems are a great solution.

Otherwise, a less-expensive ultrafiltration system might be a better choice. Either way, having an under-sink filtration system is a good way to ensure your family has access to clean water, both on a daily basis and after flooding events and other natural disasters that contaminate city and well water.

So, are you ready for the upgrade?

I was cleaning my kitchen, pop music blaring from a speaker, when I glanced at the digital calendar on my counter. That’s when I saw it—the butterfly emoji that flutters every August 21 to memorialize the day I lost my pregnancy. 

I was gutted. But this time it wasn’t because of the miscarriage. It was because I forgot. For the first time in 10 years, the date my baby’s heart stopped beating had slipped my mind. 

It’s not hard for me to bring myself back to that day. At my 12-week appointment, the doctor ran a fetal doppler across my belly, searching for a heartbeat she would never find. I watched the crease between her eyebrows deepen as she moved the wand around. She tried to reassure me there could be a reason she couldn’t find the heartbeat. Still, it made sense to get an ultrasound.

I headed off to a new location. I didn’t call anyone, doing my best to will “it’s fine” into existence. I sat nervously in the waiting room, tapping my leg up and down as I listened for my name. 

When it was my turn, I laid back on the table, prepped for the squeeze of cool jelly on my stomach, then watched a screen on the wall as the ultrasound tech poked around to find my baby.

And there it was. The makings of my second born lit up on the screen. Those images are always a bit tough to discern, but I could clearly see a head, a body, and four limbs. But those limbs weren’t moving, and the tech stayed quiet. She pressed buttons, took snapshots, and kept her gaze trained squarely on her computer screen. When she finished, she said, “The doctor will be in to talk to you,” then scurried out the door. 

Seconds later someone else popped in and asked me to follow them to another room. I now realize I was taking up precious space near the ultrasound equipment. They’d need it for their next appointment, and the news I was about to hear might stun me in place for some time. 

The moment is crystal clear in my memory even ten years later. I remember the hard plastic chair I sat in. I remember looking around the room and realizing that half of it was used for storage. There were boxes of papers and remnants of mailers alongside random supplies waiting for their real homes. Next to me, a box of tissues sat on a small table. 

When the doctor walked in, he introduced himself. I don’t remember his name, but I’ll never forget his somber look when he said, “It’s not good news.” The floor falling out from beneath me. I called my husband in tears, barely able to say, “There’s no heartbeat,” then made a desperate call to my mom to share the same news.

I don’t remember how I got back to my grandmother’s house, where she was watching my 2-year-old son. I know I drove there, but the memory is hazy, overlaid with the sadness, terror and anxiety that were coursing through me.

When I arrived, I collapsed into my grandmother’s arms. “They couldn’t find a heartbeat,” I said, fighting back tears as my son watched us intently. She tightened her grip on me and let me dissolve.

I suffered a missed miscarriage—my body didn’t recognize that the baby growing inside had died. But based on its size and development, doctors were able to tell me that the heart likely stopped beating at 10.5 weeks. That’s how I came to mark August 21 as the day my baby died. 

In the years that followed, I anxiously awaited both that date and my due date as they crept closer. I knew they would be hard. Over the first couple of years, I’d plan for unproductive days, knowing I’d fall back into my grief. I’d spend the morning each August 21 remembering the day I found out, going over every detail as I let the tears rain down. I’d search for signs of butterflies, which became a hopeful symbol to me in the wake of my miscarriage. 

But ten years later, I was caught up in the details of a very busy life. I’m a working mom with two school-aged kids who spends much of my time chauffeuring them between playdates and sports practices. 

It’s likely a good sign that August 21 no longer looms over me like a dark cloud. In those early years of letting the grief wash over me in full force, I gave my mind, body and heart the space to release its tension and ache. But forgetting the date brought its own type of grief. It was a reminder of how far I’ve come, and how far my baby never did. 

Ten years later, I still grieve my miscarriage, but that grief has evolved. I will always wonder what that little life would have become, and why they weren’t given the chance. I will always take a moment to pause and remember the hope, joy and love I felt in the early weeks of that pregnancy, even if I need the calendar to remind me to do so.

And most importantly, I will always talk about it. With time, grief has lessened its stranglehold on my heart and emotions. What remains of my grief is more like a tool. It appears when I need to be a supporter. It boosts my voice so I can help others in the throes of their own despair. 

One in 4 women will experience pregnancy or infant loss in their lifetime. I’m a 1-in-4, and I hope sharing my story helps someone else feel less alone in their pain, no matter where she is on her grieving journey.

Hike of the Week: Follow a Phantom Railroad to a Ghost Town and Abandoned Rail Tunnel in Colorado’s High Country

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
  • Trail: Hagerman Tunnel via Colorado Midland Centennial Trail
  • Length: 8 miles
  • Elevation Gain: 800 feet
  • Why hike it now: Catch the last glimpses of fall and the first glimpses of winter

Growing up in Leadville, Colorado, I took a lot of the area’s history for granted. You’re surrounded by it when you venture into the mountains around town. At a certain point, the rusting mining equipment, deserted structures, and phantom railroads start blending into the scenery. But as an adult, I’ve started revisiting the old trails I used to hike with my parents, and I’m experiencing them all over again with a new appreciation for the stories they tell.

Recently, I hiked an old family favorite, and it became a new personal one. The hike to Hagerman Tunnel via the Colorado Midland Centennial Trail is an 8-mile out-and-back that starts just below treeline and climbs above it, following an old railroad grade and passing two alpine lakes and a ghost town before arriving at the abandoned railroad tunnel.

The area has a rich railroad history. When Colorado Midland Railway built the tunnel in 1887, it was the highest standard-gauge railroad tunnel of its kind in North America—a massive engineering feat for the era. You follow a grade as you hike through narrow canyons the railway company carved over 100 years ago. The old ghost town of Douglass City sits below the tunnel. It was once a rowdy, one-street labor camp for railroad workers.

The hike itself is pretty mellow, with just 800 feet of elevation gain. It climbs gently through lodgepole pines and blue spruce, snaking up above the treeline. Roughly three miles in, you’ll pass Hagerman Lake, a pristine body of water where anglers fish for cutthroat and rainbow trout. There are a few small dispersed camping sites at Hagerman Lake if you want to make this an overnight trip. From there, it’s just a little more than a half mile to the tunnel. On your left, you’ll pass another, smaller lake, beside which lie the ruins of Douglass City (really just a handful of rotting building foundations at this point). The Douglass City trail descends to the ghost town. It also acts as a shortcut if you want to get back to the car faster.

The trail follows a railroad grade carved over 100 years ago (Photo: Will Brendza)

When you finally arrive at Hagerman Tunnel, you’ve reached the end of the hike. Entry is discouraged, and signs warn hikers of the risks of cave-in or rockfall. The ground is nearly impassable, anyway, covered with thick sheets of ice and pools of dark water. However, you can stand at the tunnel’s mouth, peering into the darkness, and imagine the old mining locomotives that used to thunder through the mountain.

As you start descending from the tunnel, the trail faces east. It looks out toward the Mosquito Range. Below is Turquoise Lake and the town of Leadville. The views for such an easy and historic hike are well worth the effort of getting there. Extend your trail time by connecting with the Windsor Lake Trail on your way out to catch another stunning alpine lake and two extra miles of hiking.

The First-Time Experiences Kids Will Never Forget in Citrus County

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

There’s something magical about watching your child experience something for the very first time. The wide eyes. The nervous excitement. The inevitable “Can we do that again?” And the stories they tell anyone who will listen long after you’ve unpacked the suitcases. In Citrus County, Florida, those moments are practically everywhere.

Welcome to Crystal River, Homosassa, Inverness and Floral City—a refreshingly wild side of the Sunshine State where first-time adventures come naturally. Here, kids can swim alongside gentle manatees, search the Gulf for their own dinner, explore underwater worlds and discover that sometimes the best adventures don’t require a screen, a schedule or even a ticket.
It’s outdoorsy without requiring your family to be expert adventurers, educational without feeling like school and relaxed enough that you don’t need a vacation from your vacation.

An easy road trip from many Florida cities and an unexpected nature escape for families traveling from farther away, Citrus County is where your next family vacation can become a collection of unforgettable firsts.

1. Their first face-to-face encounter with a manatee

Imagine the look on your child’s face when a 1,000-pound manatee glides through the water nearby. It’s the kind of moment that makes even the too-cool-for-everything tween forget to play it cool.

Crystal River is known as the Manatee Capital of the World, and it’s one of the few places in North America where families can swim with these gentle giants in their natural habitat.
Book a certified tour with an outfitter like Explorida, where guides teach families the importance of respectful wildlife viewing, including the all-important “Manatee Manners.” Tours operate year-round, but winter is prime time for seeing larger numbers of manatees as they gather in the area’s warm springs.

Not ready to snorkel? Explorida also offers manatee-viewing eco cruises, so younger kids, grandparents and anyone who prefers staying dry can still experience the magic.

Why they’ll remember it: Because seeing a manatee in a book or aquarium is one thing. Sharing the water with one in the wild? That’s a story they’ll be telling for years.

2. Their first wildlife safari from a boardwalk

For little explorers (and grandparents) who aren’t ready to jump into the water, the next best thing might be watching nature unfold right beneath their feet.

At Three Sisters Springs, a boardwalk winds around the crystal-clear spring complex, offering an incredible vantage point for spotting manatees during the winter months. The surrounding wetlands are also home to birds and other wildlife, turning an ordinary walk into an impromptu nature safari.

Give your kids a challenge: How many manatees can they spot? Can they find a mother and calf? What other animals can they discover along the way?

It’s stroller-friendly, wonderfully unhurried and proof that you don’t have to get wet to make a splash.

Why they’ll remember it: The thrill of spotting their first wild manatee all on their own, and the pride of being the one who shouts, “I SEE ONE!”

3. Their first underwater treasure hunt (with dinner as the prize!)

Forget searching for seashells on the beach. In Citrus County, kids can head underwater on a real-life treasure hunt and bring home something delicious.

During summer scalloping season, families venture into the shallow Gulf waters off Crystal River and Homosassa, armed with snorkels, masks and mesh bags, to search the seagrass for bay scallops. Think Easter egg hunt, except underwater. And instead of chocolate, the reward might end up on your dinner plate.

Never tried scalloping? That’s part of the fun. Guided excursions with operators like Explorida help beginners learn the ropes, with equipment, guidance and plenty of encouragement along the way. And the moment your child spots that first scallop hiding in the grass? Pure victory.

Why they’ll remember it: There’s something incredibly satisfying about finding your own food. Just wait until they proudly announce at dinner, “I caught that!”

Tinybeans tip: Scalloping is seasonal, typically July through September. Check current dates, regulations and tour availability before planning your adventure.

4. Their first time captaining their own adventure

For kids who’ve never tried kayaking and paddleboarding, Citrus County offers a pretty spectacular introduction. Imagine pushing off from shore, dipping a paddle into clear spring water and realizing that, for the first time, you’re helping steer the adventure.

At Hunter Springs Park in Crystal River, families will find a freshwater swimming area, kayak launch, waterfront boardwalk, picnic spots and a playground. The spring water stays around 72°F year-round, and during cooler months, there’s even a chance of spotting a manatee.

Bring your own paddle craft or book with a local outfitter. Younger kids can ride along with an adult, while older children can take a more active role in navigating.

The best part? There’s no rush to get anywhere. Stop to watch a bird, look for fish beneath the surface or simply float and take it all in.

Why they’ll remember it: That first feeling of independence. The realization that they can help navigate a kayak, explore somewhere new and discover what’s around the next bend.

5. Their first walk beneath the surface of a spring

What if your kids could explore an underwater world without ever putting on a swimsuit? At Ellie Schiller Homosassa Springs Wildlife State Park, they can. The park’s famous Fish Bowl Underwater Observatory lets visitors descend beneath the surface of the spring to watch fish and, when present, manatees swim past the windows. For kids, it’s a little like stepping inside a nature documentary.

Back above water, shady, stroller-accessible boardwalks lead past native Florida wildlife, including black bears, alligators, red wolves and Florida panthers. Daily manatee programs offer another opportunity to learn about these remarkable animals and why protecting their habitat matters.

It’s an ideal first introduction to Florida’s wild side, especially for families with younger children or anyone who prefers wildlife encounters on dry land.

Why they’ll remember it: Because the first time you see the underwater world from a fish’s point of view, you never look at the water quite the same way again.

6. Their first big bike adventure through Old Florida

Remember the first time you rode your bike somewhere that wasn’t your driveway or neighborhood? The freedom. The excitement. The feeling that you could go anywhere?
That’s what makes the Withlacoochee State Trail such a great family adventure.

Stretching approximately 47 paved miles through forests, lakes and charming small towns, the trail is flat, scenic and easy to explore in family-sized sections. No one expects your six-year-old to conquer all 47 miles.

Start in downtown Inverness, where the trail passes through the Depot District, and let your kids help choose how far to ride. Along the way, keep an eye out for birds, turtles and other wildlife.
When everyone starts asking about snacks, make The Train Station part of your plan for pizza, pretzels and a well-earned break.

Why they’ll remember it: Their first real biking expedition, complete with the satisfaction of going farther than they thought they could. Bonus points if they proudly tell everyone how many miles they conquered.

7. Their first encounter with a real-life mermaid

You can tell your kids mermaids aren’t real. Or you can take them somewhere that might make them question everything.

At Mertailor’s Mermaid Aquarium Encounter in Lecanto, live mermaids perform underwater alongside fish, rays and other marine life, creating a magical experience that feels straight out of a storybook. But there’s more here than mermaid magic. The indoor aquarium features over 20 exhibits, interactive touch tanks, sharks, stingrays and freshwater animals, giving curious kids plenty to explore.

For children ages nine and up, the separate Discover Mermaid experience takes things a step further, with the chance to put on a mermaid tail and monofin and learn to swim like one.
It’s also a great rainy-day adventure or a welcome break from the Florida sunshine.

Why they’ll remember it: Because watching a mermaid swim with sharks is pretty unforgettable. But getting to be a mermaid for the first time? That’s next-level vacation bragging rights.

8. Their first taste of the laid-back Florida lifestyle

Not every unforgettable first requires an adrenaline rush. Sometimes it’s the first time your kids experience what it feels like to slow down.

Head to Homosassa for a waterfront meal at Crumps’ Landing, where palm trees, water views and laid-back Florida charm set the scene for a different kind of family dinner. No rushing to make a reservation across town. No squeezing in one more attraction. Just good food, waterfront views and time to talk about everything you’ve done together. Let the kids recap their favorite adventures while you watch the sky change colors over the water.

Why they’ll remember it: It may not be the meal they remember years from now, but they’ll remember the feeling: salty air, sunset skies and everyone together with nowhere else they need to be.

9. Their first wake-up call from Mother Nature

Remember when simply staying in a hotel felt like the most exciting thing in the world? Now imagine staying somewhere that puts you right in the middle of the action.

Paddletail Waterfront Lodge sits on King’s Bay in Crystal River, giving families easy access to the area’s most memorable experiences. With paddlecraft activities, manatee excursions, waterfront dining, a pool and complimentary hot breakfast, the lodge makes it easy to spend less time coordinating logistics and more time having fun. Rooms with refrigerators and microwaves, and select kitchenette options also make traveling with kids a little easier.

Wake up knowing that another adventure is waiting just outside.

Why they’ll remember it: Because when the place you stay is part of the experience, even the moments between activities become vacation memories.

10. Their first vacation where they get to decide what’s next

Here’s a first that might surprise you: What if you gave your kids an entire afternoon to decide what the family does? No packed itinerary. No racing from one attraction to another. No pressure to squeeze every last minute out of your vacation.

One of the best things about Citrus County is that it gives families room to explore at their own pace. Maybe your kids want another swim at Hunter Springs. Maybe they want to wander downtown Crystal River, go for another bike ride, find ice cream or sit by the water and watch for wildlife. Let them choose.

Because sometimes the most meaningful first isn’t swimming with a manatee or spotting a scallop. It’s the first time your child discovers that an adventure doesn’t need to be planned to be unforgettable.

Why they’ll remember it: Because they helped create the memory themselves. And those are often the moments that stick.

Their Firsts. Your Forever Memories.

Someday, your kids might forget what they packed, what they ate for breakfast or how long the drive took. But they’ll remember the first manatee they saw. The first scallop they found. The first time they paddled across a spring or rode their bike farther than ever before. And you’ll remember the look on their faces when they did.

That’s the magic of Citrus County. It’s not just a place to visit. It’s a place to try something new, discover something extraordinary and make the kinds of memories that grow more meaningful with time.

Earning a Michelin Star is to fine dining what thru-hiking one of America’s major long trails is to backpacking. It denotes commitment, higher-than-usual skill, and a knack for going further. Merit the honor of three Michelin stars (or navigate the nearly 8,000 miles of the Triple Crown of Hiking), and you become part of a small but elite club.

But what happens when a three-Michelin-starred chef takes their skills into the backcountry?

Chef Kyle Connaughton’s California-based restaurant SingleThread has consistently held three Michelin Stars every year since 2019. Each year, the chef proves his restaurant’s Japanese cuisine meets Michelin’s rather nebulous rubric, which factors in ingredient quality, flavor harmony, culinary mastery, and consistency while allowing a hint of the chef’s personality to shine through. Connaughton does this by taking his guests on a journey. Over a 10-course tasting menu, people savor the ingredients freshly harvested from the restaurant’s accompanying 24-acre farm, which is run by Connaughton’s wife and business partner, Katina.

“When guests come and dine, they’re experiencing what’s in season, what Katina’s excited about, what we’re harvesting,” the chef says of the hyperlocal produce and Kaiseki-inspired small plates they serve. “We tell the story of today.”

Kyle and Katina work together to run SingleThread Farm and Restaurant in California (Photo: YETI)

Spinning that tale is time-intensive, which means Connaughton has few hours to spend beyond the SingleThread kitchen. When he does have time, he heads outside. Avid hikers and backcountry snowboarders, Connaughton and Katina have tramped through the Sierra Nevada and Wasatch Ranges and heli-snowboarded in Silverton, Colorado. They take annual trips with large groups of friends to British Columbia and Hokkaido, Japan for cold days of cat laps and fresh snow. Even in the wild, however, the YETI-sponsored chef finds ways to wield his three Michelin-starred skillset—not for work, but for his own and his companions’ pleasure.

“In the restaurant, guests arrive expecting and ready to be impressed by the food,” he says. “But out in the backcountry, you can do very small things and just blow people’s minds.”

Backpacker sat down with Chef Connaughton to learn how regular home cooks can do a little gobsmacking of our own. Here, he shares tips, tools, and tricks of the trade for creating culinary magic in the great outdoors.

(Photo: Anastasiia Krivenok via Getty)

Anticipate and plan for appetites.

Connaughton acknowledges that yes, you can subsist on dehydrated beans and protein bars when you’re out on the trail. “They’ll offer calories,” he says, “but they’re not very rewarding.”

Instead, lean into omotenashi, an anticipatory approach to hospitality Connaughton learned while living and attending culinary school in Japan. The goal: Understand and plan for what the guest will want before they recognize the desire themselves. Applying this strategy in the wild is actually pretty easy—and doesn’t necessarily require much added effort. If your crew just hiked seven miles, it’s fair to assume they’ll be more excited by a spread of salami and hard cheeses than (more) trail mix.

“When you bring this hospitality into the backcountry, it’s even more meaningful because it’s not expected,” says Connaughton, who’s famous among his backcountry snowboarding friends for tucking a dozen burritos and a hot water bottle into his Yeti Roadie Cooler and stashing it beneath a bench in the snowcat. Then at 2 p.m., when everyone’s cold and wet after hours of shredding, he passes around still-steaming burritos. “It’s such a surprise and delight moment.”

Determine your constraints—then push against them.

Far before making menu decisions, Connaughton assesses what resources—and limitations—he’s likely to encounter. Think through the group’s mode of transportation, he says. Consider whether you’re hiking on two legs or have a vehicle to haul the cast iron to the campsite.

Assess the amount of packing space available and/or the weight that’s manageable to avoid becoming an annoyance–or, worse, a liability–to your fellow adventurers. Consider the heat source you’ll have access to. Will there be a full grill, a two-burner Coleman, an Iwatani stove (Connaughton’s preference), or just an open flame? Then, use all of these parameters to decide if you’re playing a grams game with your cooking vessel, if there’s room (and horsepower) for a more durable carbon steel pan, or if there’s a way to haul in a donabe, a Japanese clay pot, without it breaking. Connaughton recently used the latter to cook a whole duck over a campfire because he had the resources and was curious to test his abilities.

Ultimately, “if you know what the box is, there’s room for infinite creativity within that box,” Connaughton says. “Then, try to push right to the edge of what that box will allow.”

Do as much in advance as possible. The biggest secret to cooking a Michelin-level meal in the backcountry: Don’t. Chop the carrots and mince the garlic using the KitchenAid in your kitchen. Braise the beef or boil the chicken on your home stove. Close it all in no-spill containers or vacuum-sealed bags, and pop it into your pack. Connaughton often crafts an entire main course at home. Then, he freezes it and unveils the spread after a quick reheat deep in the woods. This tactic makes cooking (if you can even call it that) exponentially easier. But it also allows him to be a better host.

“I’m always planning a menu around how I can maximize the time with the guests,” he says, “so they’re not all having fun while I’m in the kitchen.”

Chef Connaughton inspects ingredients at his restaurant (Photo: YETI)

Don’t forget food safety.

For a chef keen for repeat guests, food safety is always top of mind. This is obviously also true for those handling protein and produce in the land of latrines and wag bags. Perishables in particular need special care.

“You’re always doing a calculation of what is my time from when I leave refrigeration,” Connaughton says, “to when I’m going to utilize that product.”

One of the chef’s favorite tricks is to make a dish (say a hot pot) at home, vacuum-seal the meal to reduce its bulk, and then lay it on its side in the freezer. He then takes the frozen package, puts it into a second vacuum-seal bag, fills that bag with ice cubes, vacuum-seals it, and freezes it all again. Before heading for the hills, he’ll slide the laptop-shaped mass into the back panel of his pack. The meal in the inner bag remains cold and food-safe. The exterior ice turns to water that he can later use to refill his water bottle. Once the crew makes camp and eats a “beautiful meal, rather than dehydrated food or something else really bland and boring,” Connaughton says. Then, he turns the exterior baggie into sealable trash storage.

“To be successful,” he adds, “you’re always thinking about the layers of purpose and repurpose all the way through.”

Add flair.

Will a final sprinkle of roasted sesame seeds or a drizzle of spicy, citrusy yuzu koshō impact the taste of your dish too significantly? Perhaps not. But garnishes like these are lightweight, easy to pack, and sure to please the crowd.

“Those little touches—something you wouldn’t pay attention to in the normal world—all of a sudden become so impactful,” Connaughton says. People notice when you’re far from home, “and you still take time to make it nice.”

Anticipate what kinds of food your guests want before they even know. (Photo: Anastasiia Krivenok via Getty)

Reduce and reuse so you can relax.

Crafting the meal is just one element of a multistep process. “Cleaning is part of it [too], right?” Connaughton says. “We’ve got to prep, cook, serve, and clean.”

Minimizing the number of dishes used will make the scourge of scrubbing faster. “Rather than having the pan for the meat, the pot for the sauce, and the skillet for the vegetables, plan whatever you’re cooking around how you can turn over that same equipment most efficiently and effectively,” Connaughton suggests.

When Connaughton chefs it up outdoors, he’s often a one-pan man. He first sears the meat and then sets it aside. Next, he cooks the veggies in that same pan and, when they’re done, places them with the protein. Lastly, he whips up a sauce, once again in the same vessel. By not washing in between steps, he allows the different flavors to build on each other. Bonus: “Adding the liquids [for sauce] last helps to deglaze and take some of the food bits off of the pan,” he says. “It comes out relatively clean for an easy final cleanup.”

Consider fellow adventurers guests at your backcountry table.

At the highest realms of fine-dining, terms like “customers” and “covers” are taboo.

“These are guests who are coming into the restaurant,” Connaughton says. “They’re guests in our home, the home of a chef and a farmer. We’re taking care of our guests. That’s our mentality.”

Take the same tack, he suggests, when breaking bread in the backcountry. There, you can lead guests on a journey, as Connaughton does in the kitchen at SingleThread, and you also get to join them on it.

“These are the people that I’m choosing to spend time with,” Connaughton says. “We’re all responsible for each other’s safety, and we’re all responsible for each other’s good experience. It’s the spirit of hospitality.”

One-Dish Red Curry Noodles

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 2 cups reduced-sodium vegetable broth
  • 1 can (13-1/2 ounces) coconut milk
  • 1 jar (4 ounces) red curry paste
  • 2 tablespoons reduced-sodium soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons minced fresh gingerroot
  • 2 teaspoons minced garlic
  • 1-1/2 teaspoons toasted sesame oil
  • 8 ounces dried Japanese udon noodles
  • 1 medium sweet red pepper, thinly sliced
  • 1 package (10 ounces) frozen broccoli stir-fry vegetable blend, thawed
  • 2 cups finely shredded cabbage
  • Sliced green onion, sesame seeds and lime wedges, for garnish
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. Preheat oven to 375°.
  2. In a 13x9-in. casserole dish, stir together vegetable stock, coconut milk, red curry paste, soy sauce, ginger, garlic and sesame oil. Add udon noodles; use a fork to carefully separate into a single layer. Top with red pepper, stir-fry vegetables and cabbage. Press down into the liquid to submerge.
  3. Cover with foil; bake 20 minutes. Uncover; stir. Return to the oven, uncovered; cook until noodles are tender, 15-20 minutes. Top with green onion and sesame seeds. Serve with lime wedges.

Nutrition Facts

1 serving: 300 calories, 12g fat (10g saturated fat), 0 cholesterol, 1131mg sodium, 38g carbohydrate (7g sugars, 4g fiber), 8g protein.

Saddle to Paddle: Bikerafting From Canada to Mexico

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Pete Streufert and his friend Carter (no last name) are cycling 4,025km and packrafting 645km across the U.S. from Canada to Mexico. Starting in Glacier National Park, they will cross four rivers and six U.S. states to finish in El Paso, Texas. 

Their route connects the rivers with long-distance cycling trails. This allows the pair to cross landscapes that would usually require separate trips by bike and watercraft. Streufert, a geospatial wildfire scientist and cartographer, came up with the route by combining existing GPS tracks and geographical data from Bikepacking.com, American Whitewater, and OpenStreetMap. He overlaid cycling corridors and rivers to identify where the two could connect.

Before starting the journey on September 17, he said they could find no record of anyone using this particular combination of rivers and cycling routes. So it is possible that they are the first to do so.

The start

They started what they neatly call the Saddle to Paddle Traverse on September 17, from Glacier National Park, Montana, on the Canadian border. They kicked off the first packrafting section with a 115km descent of the North Fork of the Flathead River, which forms part of the western boundary of Glacier National Park.

From there, they followed the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route through Montana toward Jackson Lake, Wyoming, for 1,107km. Here, they hit their second paddling phase on the Snake River.

Every time they reach a river, the pair inflates their rafts, load their bicycles aboard, and paddle downstream. Then at each takeout, they deflate the rafts, attach them to their bikes, and continue riding.

The route. Photo: Pete Streufert

 

After 130km along the Snake River, their route joined the SLoToJa cycling corridor. They pedaled through Idaho, Wyoming, and Utah. Here, a short connector track took them onto the newly developed Golden Gravel Trail. A further 860km of cycling brought them to Utah's Green River.

Class III and IV rapids

They then paddled 328km through Desolation and Gray Canyons, including a handful of Class III rapids, and their only Class IV rapid of the trip. 

Pulling their rafts out in Canyonlands National Park, they took the White Rim Trail to Moab. They are now taking a few days in Moab to “get all the mud off our bikes and escape the rain for a day or two before we keep heading south,” says Streufert.

From here, they will once again pick up the Golden Gravel Trail through the La Sal Mountains and across Colorado, then continue on the Great Divide Mountain Bike Route to New Mexico.

Paddling the Green River. Photo: Pete Streufert

 

A final paddling section on New Mexico's Rio Chama will take them to the last leg of the trip, a 950km ride to El Paso.

The pair are riding Rodeo Labs Flaanimal 6.0 Steel, fitted with 29-inch Vittoria Mezcal tires, and using Kokopelli packrafts. The pair have described this as a "self-supported" route, although of course they're replenishing their food along the way. They seem to combine camping with staying in towns.

The New York Times is adding more AI features to its Cooking app, and users aren’t thrilled

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sometimes, adding just one new ingredient to a stew can ruin the whole thing.[sidenote]Olives, yecch.[/sidenote] And that’s how at least some New York Times readers see its addition of AI features to its popular Cooking app.

Here’s Genevieve Ko, NYT Cooking senior editor and columnist, explaining the new features:

I naïvely assumed A.I. wouldn’t affect my work as a recipe developer and my role at NYT Cooking, where I oversee the editorial side of our recipes and related stories. But as artificial intelligence improved, our product and engineering colleagues saw its potential to help us create features Cooking users wanted.

We’ve just launched our latest tool, which gives cooks the ability to apply modifications to our recipes. We’re rolling it out gradually to monitor how it performs (whether it’s working fast enough, for instance) and to see how people are using it (as of now, we’re getting a lot of requests for ingredient substitutions). The tool is currently available to all Cooking subscribers on our iOS app, and we plan to make it available more broadly soon.

The Times had previously rolled out AI features aimed at converting volume-based and weight-based measurements (“What’s one cup of bok choy in grams?”) and scaling a recipe up or down to serve more or fewer people. The main new feature is focused on ingredient substitutions.

Say you’re cooking braised white beans and greens with parmesan, NYT Cooking app in hand. But you notice that you’re out of chard and only have spinach. Cooking veterans know that chard is a rough-and-tumble green that has some backbone[sidenote]There’s a reason one type of chard is known as “perpetual spinach.”[/sidenote], while spinach is a comparative wimp that will wilt the moment you look at it funny. If you need to sub in spinach for chard, how would the recipe change? Would you, for instance, add the spinach later than you would the chard to account for its pathetic stamina? That’s the sort of thing the new AI feature could account for.

Cooking staffers “spent countless hours evaluating sample after sample to get the chatbot answers to closely align with how we would answer as culinary experts,” Ko writes. “We applied the same rigor as we do to editing, grounded in Times standards.”

But because Ko knows that any AI-powered features are likely to be controversial with a Times audience, she spends quite a lot of space addressing potential objections, everything from “the impact of data centers” to “the concentration of power among A.I. companies” and “the risk of A.I. escaping our control.” These features are designed to enable “the tactile physical pleasure of cooking” and getting a “very real, very human creation” on your table.

Broadly speaking, commenters weren’t buying it.

“What a massive disappointment,” wrote Susan from New Jersey. “The internet is full of AI cooking content. I pay you because I prefer mine done by people.”

“This decision misses the mark by such a wide margin it’s baffling,” says Ian from Los Angeles. “And the disclosure just as baffling. It would not be inaccurate to summarize the piece as saying ‘I know doing this is bad and I don’t want to be associated with the bad thing so that’s why we are doing the bad thing.'”

“Nobody wants this, it’s bad for cooking, it’s bad for the NYT,” writes Bill from Pittsburgh. “Wasting a column trying to gaslight yourself and your audience into believing that it’s anything but is an embarrassment and a waste of everyone’s time.”

“PLEASE PLEASE PLEASE DO NOT DO THIS,” says Judah Mann of New York. “Oh, wait, you already did. Ok, I’m out.”

Or as Naomi from California puts it: “I am sad that AI has breached the one online resource I truly love.”

Because I enjoy the irony, I scraped the 171 comments left on the Times’ announcement and asked Codex (running GPT-6.1 Sol, at medium thinking) to do some sentiment analysis. (Twelve of the 171 were comments left by Times staffers; I’m excluding them here.) Roughly 77% of the reader comments were negative about the new AI features, it found, versus 14% positive and the remainder neutral or mixed. Among the biggest categories of complaint: This shouldn’t require AI. AI is destroying the environment. AI is not why I’m paying the Times $390 a year. The adjustments the AI produces are going to be bad.

Dozens of commenters asked for a way to turn off the feature. “No, not at this time,” Ko responded, “though this feature is completely optional to use.” Indeed: Don’t ask AI to substitute your chard and AI won’t substitute your chard.

One consistent thread was that cooking is supposed to present you with difficulties and challenges and the whole point is figuring them out by yourself. “Anyone using AI for cooking has no sense of taste and no desire to work out the intricacies of cooking and baking,” writes Michael King from Brooklyn. “To use AI for recipes is beyond the pale. If you are going to use AI, then just cut to the chase and defenestrate yourself.” Or BJ from North Carolina: “So, you’re telling me that I’m too stupid to figure it out on my own?”

Meanwhile, the few positive comments tended to focus on how the AI feature had, in fact, made dietary substitutions easier for them. “I love the new AI feature because it quickly (and so far) accurately converts recipes to gluten free,” Anne Wagner from Kansas City writes. “I don’t cook for stress relief,” says Andy from San Francisco. “I cook to put dinner on the table. Anything that makes the process a little faster or smoother is welcome.”

Four Practices to Help You Feel More Engaged at Work

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Work can be tough. With only 20% of workers feeling engaged globally, we need tools that can help boost engagement and well-being at work.

While some difficulty at work may always be expected, there are things we can do to take control of our work lives and bring more kindness, appreciation, connection, and trust into the workplace. For many years, researchers have been testing different practices that individual employees, managers, and teams can do to improve work life for everyone.

You can find some of these on Greater Good in Action, our library of over 100 practices found to improve well-being. Each practice includes:

  • Simple instructions on the “how” of the practice, which can generally be done in five to 30 minutes at work with minimal to moderate effort
  • Why you should try it (including why the benefits matter at work)
  • Why it works (what actually happens that leads to the benefits)
  • Evidence that it works (scientific research that points to its effectiveness)

Recently, we added four new practices that you might want to try in your workplace.

1. Appreciation Circle At Work

Let’s face it: We don’t always feel appreciated at work, even though appreciation seems to be a key need for most people. In this practice, you begin a regular team meeting with the simple act of appreciating the person to your left. You say something specific about one of their qualities or an action they took and the impact it had on you. Then that person appreciates the person to their left, and you go around the room (or virtual meeting) until everyone gets appreciated.

Years ago, I tested this practice in a meeting with a team I was working with. When we got to the third person being appreciated, she broke down into tears. She said, “I’ve been working here for nine years, and I didn’t know you guys appreciate me. . . . I didn’t even know you liked me.”

While this was hard to hear, it’s also a problem that is pretty easy to fix. Appreciation Circles, which can be repeated regularly, try to establish a culture and ritual of appreciation so that no one has to feel undervalued at work.

2. Three Questions to Engage Employees

This practice is designed for managers during their regular (and sometimes less than inspiring) one-on-one meetings with their direct reports. It recommends that you ask employees these three questions:

  • What are you most proud of in your work lately?
  • What’s an idea, perspective, or possibility you haven’t had a chance to share yet?
  • What’s a challenge you’re having or an obstacle that I can help you overcome?

As managers, sometimes we don’t realize the impact of our time with employees. Research suggests that our supportive behaviors can go a long way to helping employees feel more engaged. So how can managers be more supportive? 

One study found that when managers encouraged, assured, and valued employee efforts, employees felt more engaged. These are the themes that the three questions in this practice address.

3. Forging Trust at Work

The question of how you build trust has always been a fascinating one for me, because trust is so foundational to getting things done and is such an important part of a healthy workplace culture. Studies suggest that high-trust work cultures are often higher-performing, but how do you actually earn trust at work?

In their analysis of hundreds of studies on trust, researchers found that there are three key levers for building trust: demonstrating your ability, benevolence, and integrity. In other words, people trust you more when they believe you can get things done, have their best interests at heart, and follow through on your word.

This practice helps you identify ways to show others that you have these qualities by considering three questions and then acting on your answers:

  • What can I do to increase their belief about my abilities?
  • What can I do to increase their belief about me having their best interests at heart?
  • What can I do to increase their belief that I will follow through and do what I say I’m going to do?

4. Acts of Kindness at Work

This practice is probably one of the more fun and feel-good practices because it encourages you to offer some random acts of kindness to your coworkers and see if this creates a multiplier effect in your workplace.

For example, you could bring a coworker a cup of coffee, help them with a project, or offer a handwritten thank-you note.

Studies suggest that kind and helpful behaviors can be contagious to those receiving the kind acts, and that even bystanders see boosts in their well-being just from observing these acts.

We encourage you to try this low-risk and feel-good practice in your workplace soon—as well as the rest of them! Keep in touch and let us know how these practices land in your workplace.
