Judge lets Bay Area woman keep her ‘RVRTSEA’ license plate — for now

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

(CN) — A federal judge ruled Friday that a Bay Area woman can keep her personalized license plate reading, “RVRTSEA” — at least for now.

Iasmine Abdennabi has had the license plate, which is an abbreviation of the slogan, “river to the sea,” since 2024. The phrase is shorthand for: “from the river to the sea, Palestine will be free.”

The saying, often chanted during protests and rallies, has different interpretations. Some see it as a simple support for the embattled state and people of Palestine, currently under siege by Israel. Others see it as a call to arms, or worse — a call for the destruction of the state of Israel, which is bounded by Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea. The Anti-Defamation League, for example, calls the slogan an “antisemitic” rallying cry “denying the Jewish right to self-determination, including through the removal of Jews from their ancestral homeland.”

Many others disagree, especially in the U.S., where the slogan can be heard at any number of pro-Palestinian protests. In her complaint, filed on Monday, Abdennabi states that to her, the phrase is not an “instruction to commit genocide,” nor “a threat against Jews,” or call to violence. She says her intended message is “support for equal civil and political rights for all persons living between the Jordan River and Mediterranean Sea.”

The California Department of Motor Vehicles received a total of three complaints about Abdennabi’s license plate, including one from a man who wrote: “This advocates for the extermination of the state of Israel and all Jewish people. It supports terrorism and encourages violence.” Following department policy, the DMV ordered Abdennabi to turn over her plates in 2025, 15 months after it had issued them.

Abdennadi appealed, triggering an administrative hearing, at which a DMV representative explained the plates were removed “based on the connotation of violence or genocide.”

More than a million Californians have personalized license plates, far more than any other state in the country. License plates are rejected for a number of reasons: having swear words, dirty jokes, sexual connotations, hate speech, offensive messages, and references to violence, drugs and illegal activity, including references to gangs and marijuana. No plates with the number “420” are allowed, for example.

After her administrative appeal was denied, Abdennadi sued, arguing the DMV was infringing on her First Amendment rights to express her political beliefs. On Friday, a federal judge granted her a restraining order, allowing her to keep her “RVRTSEA” license plates, at least until a scheduled preliminary injunction hearing next month.

In his order, U.S. District Judge Richard Seeborg cast doubt on the constitutionality of the regulations that allow the DMV to reject certain vanity plates.

“On its face, this regulation appears to bar three separate things: fighting words, incitement, and speech referring to violence of various types,” Seeborg wrote. “One of those things is not like the others. Fighting words and incitement are long-recognized categories of low-value speech that do not merit First Amendment protection. By contrast, speech merely ‘referring to’ violence seems more akin to the language of offense, which the Supreme Court explicitly identified as protected First Amendment expression.”

He added if that was the case, then the regulations were “unconstitutional because they discriminate among private speech based on viewpoint.”

Abdennabi’s attorney, Michael Millen, praised the ruling.

“We are grateful to the court for recognizing that the government may not serve as the arbiter of free speech,” she said in an email. “Plaintiff is a firm believer in equal civil and political rights for all persons living between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea, and the DMV was wrong to revoke the license plate ‘RVRTSEA’ because an anonymous complainant and two other people felt it was ‘offensive.'”

Attorneys for the DMV did not respond to an email requesting comment on the ruling.

It’s not just dry messes that shop vacuums can handle. When unexpected wet messes happen, like a water main break, leak, or flood, a wet/dry shop vac is an indispensable tool that can quickly suck up the liquid into a canister for easy disposal. And, the variety of attachments that come with most shop vacs make them ready for flat surfaces, carpet, or even hard-to-reach crevices. The trick is knowing how to use a shop vac for water removal the right way. Here’s what you need to know.

Before You Begin

Water and electricity don’t mix. Although modern shop vacs are typically double-insulated, it’s never a bad idea to plug yours into a GFCI outlet for extra assurance and if you use an extension cord, make sure it’s wet-rated. 

Also, when using a shop vac for water, keep in mind that you will need to lift the shop vac’s canister to dump out the water when you are finished cleaning up a mess. It’s usually easier to dump out the water before it’s full.

How to Use a Shop Vac to Clean Up Water

A shop vacuum can handle a ton of jobs like sawdust collection, carpet cleaning, and gutter cleaning. Here’s how to use a wet vac and how to suck up water with a shop vac. 

Step 1: Remove the bag and filter from the vacuum.

If your model has a bag, remove it before using your shop vac for water. If you don’t, it will fill up with liquid and rip, making a nasty mess in the canister. These bags are typically very easy to remove and reinstall, so don’t throw yours out if it isn’t full.

Then remove the air filter. (If your shop vac has a wet filter, insert the model’s foam filter that’s designed for liquids.) If the air filter is installed when sucking up water, it will clog quickly and reduce airflow. The motor will then work harder than normally necessary, which could shorten the lifespan of your shop vac.

Step 2: Attach the best shop vac attachment for the situation.

Photo: Vacmaster

It’s easier to remove water from a surface if the end of the vacuum is compatible with the surface. For flat floors, look for the wide, T-shaped attachment. This attachment does the best job of removing water from hard floors of all types. Some of these floor attachments even have rubber squeegees that you can insert to increase their effectiveness.

Consider using the crevice tool for vehicle floors, tight spaces, depressions, or other spaces where the floor attachment won’t fit. You can also shop for a handheld wet vac meant specifically for small spaces. 

Step 3: Set the shop vac on a level surface and plug it into a GFCI outlet.

Place the shop vac near the spill, and then run the cord to the closest GFCI outlet to extra protect yourself from any possible shock during use. If the cord doesn’t reach a GFCI outlet, then run an extension cord that’s wet-rated. You’ll see a W on the cord’s jacket if it’s wet-rated. 

Step 4: Turn on the shop vac and start vacuuming the water.

Pushing the vacuum attachment back and forth across the floor rapidly will spread puddles and make them harder to clean up. Here’s how to start vacuuming water. 

  • Turn on the shop vac. 
  • Place the nozzle in the water. 
  • Hold the nozzle in the water until it sucks up most of the water in the area. 
  • Then, move to a new area and repeat.

For carpets and areas that require the crevice tool, adopt the same approach but on a smaller scale. Work in small areas, and give the vacuum a few seconds at each location before moving it to a new space.

Step 5: Empty the canister.

Photo: Glenda Taylor for BobVila.com

Your shop vac has a built-in safety device that stops the vacuum when it’s full. There is a ball or puck in the filter attachment that floats, and when the water reaches an unsafe height, this device blocks the suction. To keep vacuuming, you’ll have to empty the canister. 

  • First, remove the lid and motor assembly. 
  • Look at the canister full of water. If there are any large chunks of items or debris, remove them before draining. 
  • Next, carry the canister to a utility sink or somewhere you can safely drain the water. 
  • If you have a large shop vac that has a plug or cap at the bottom of the shop vac, open it to begin draining. Replace the cap once the canister is empty and head back to suck up more water. 
  • If your shop vac does not have a plug at the bottom, lift the canister and pour out the water. 

Step 6: Clean the shop vac when you’re done.

After you finish cleaning up the water, you will still need to clean and dry the shop vac to prevent mold and mildew. 

  • Remove the vac hose and rinse it with a garden hose. 
  • Then, use the garden hose to clean the dirt and grime out of the shop vac. 
  • Then clean the inside of the canister and the vacuum assembly with an all-purpose cleaner. 
  • Rinse everything once more before allowing it all to dry. For best results, turn the canister upside down while it dries. 
  • After everything is clean and dry, reattach the lid, filter, and vacuum bag.

When to Use a Wet Vac

Using a shop vac for something wet usually means it’s a surprise and an urgent situation. From plumbing emergencies to severe storms and accidental spills, a wet-rated vacuum is a handy tool that can help contain wet messes and speed-up the return to normal. Here are some situations when a wet vac is a smart tool that can help. 

  • Pipe burst or leaky pipe
  • Toilet clog overflow
  • Sewer drain or sink backup
  • Dishwasher or washing machine overflow
  • Minor basement flood
  • Broken aquarium
  • Sump pump failure
  • Soggy carpet from cleaning or spills
  • Water pooled in a crawl space
  • Rainwater in post holes for a deck or fence

How to Choose the Right Shop Vac

Photo: DeWalt

There are a variety of shop vacs that are designed to handle different types of messes. Since everyone’s situation is different, consider your needs so you can choose the best shop vac for you. Here are some popular types of shop vacs.

  • Large-capacity shop vacuums are great for big jobs and for those who frequently clean up after projects like professional remodelers and DIYers who are renovating entire homes. 
  • Battery-powered shop vacuums make it easy to transport and set up wherever a vacuum is needed. Since they run on batteries, they can run out of power during a big job unless you have an extra battery ready to go. 
  • Compact shop vacuums are designed to help clean up small messes in small spaces. If you don’t have frequent spills or messes, and if you don’t have a lot of storage space for another tool, a compact shop vac might be a good fit.

FAQ

Q. Should I rent or buy a shop vac?

If you have a surprise basement cleanup to tackle, it may make sense to rent a shop vac, since big box stores also rent shop vacs by the hour or day for less than the cost of a new one. However, it might make sense to purchase one if you expect that you’ll use it at least several times a year. 

Q. How long can you run a shop vac?

Experts suggest running a shop vac for 2 hours or less before giving the motor a break to cool down, which can help extend the life of the tool. It’s also important to keep in mind that tools have an expected lifetime. For example, Ridgid says their vacuums expect a life of about 500 to 700 hours. 

Q. Can you use a wet vac on other liquids than water?

Yes, a wet vac can suck up liquids beyond just water as long as the liquid is not toxic or hazardous.

Q. When should I call in a pro?

A shop vac is a great first response tool, and it’s useful for smaller messes. However, for substantial flooding or when water’s been present for long enough to cause structural damage or mold and mildew issues, you might need to call in the experts.

Crime Is Down: Why Leadership Matters

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

For years, Americans were told by the left that rising crime was either exaggerated, the result of poverty, caused by COVID, or simply the unavoidable cost of modern life. None of those assertions is true.

Victims, police officers, prosecutors, and families living in high-crime neighborhoods knew better. They understood that crime is not an abstraction. It is a stolen vehicle, a broken window, a family traumatized by violence, or a neighborhood slowly surrendered to fear.

That is why the preliminary results of the just-released 2025 National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS) deserve serious attention. The numbers point in one direction: crime fell substantially across nearly every major category measured by the survey.

The results are preliminary, and responsible observers will await final estimates and additional analysis. But the trend is unmistakable—and it coincides with a national agenda that has placed public safety, border security, law enforcement, and support for victims at the center of federal policy.

According to the preliminary estimates from the Bureau of Justice Statistics, the rate of serious violent victimization—violent crime excluding simple assault—fell 25%, from 9.9 victimizations per 1,000 people age 12 or older in 2024 to 7.4 per 1,000 in 2025. Aggravated assault declined even more sharply, dropping 31% from 4.5 to 3.1 per 1,000.

The property-crime figures are equally striking. Property victimization declined 19%, from 138.0 per 1,000 households to 111.2 per 1,000. Burglary or trespassing fell 25%. Motor vehicle theft declined 30%, representing approximately 349,000 fewer stolen vehicles. Other household theft declined 19%, while burglary and trespassing represented roughly 460,000 fewer victimizations.

These are not minor statistical adjustments. They represent fewer people harmed, fewer families disrupted, and fewer communities forced to absorb the economic and emotional costs of crime.

The NCVS is particularly important because it captures criminal victimization whether or not the victim reported the offense to police. Each year, the Bureau of Justice Statistics gathers information from a nationally representative sample of approximately 240,000 people age 12 or older in about 150,000 households.

It therefore complements the FBI’s Uniform Crime Reporting data, which are based primarily on information supplied by law enforcement agencies.

The two measures tell a consistent story. The FBI’s 2025 data showed the largest year-to-year decline in violent crime since the agency began tracking national crime trends in 1936. Murder and non-negligent manslaughter declined by an estimated 18.1%.

The NCVS now provides an important second measure, reflecting the experiences of victims themselves.

What explains the decline? No serious analyst should pretend that a single policy produces every change in national crime. Crime rates are influenced by many factors, including local policing strategies, prosecution decisions, economic conditions, demographics, and community involvement. Preliminary survey results also cannot establish a definitive cause-and-effect relationship.

But policy choices matter. A president who makes securing the border a priority, removes violent illegal aliens who are subject to deportation, directs the Justice Department to cooperate with the nation’s approximately 18,000 police departments, 3,400 sheriffs’ offices, and 2,300 elected district attorneys, funds law enforcement, and supports victims is sending a clear message: the government’s first responsibility is to protect the innocent.

That message matters. Criminals should expect enforcement, not indulgence. Police officers should know that federal officials recognize the risks they face. Prosecutors should have the resources and cooperation necessary to hold dangerous offenders accountable. Victims should not be treated as an afterthought in a system more concerned with the comfort of defendants than the security of communities.

“This report is proof that President Trump’s ’Make America Safe Again’ agenda is working,” Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward said, the No. 3 official in the Justice Department.

The statement captures the broader significance of the data, even if conclusions should await final estimates: public safety improves when government treats it as a core mission rather than a campaign slogan.

The lesson is straightforward. Effective enforcement, cooperation among federal, state, and local authorities, secure borders, adequately funded police departments, and meaningful support for victims are not outdated ideas. They are practical necessities.

Americans should celebrate these preliminary gains while remaining vigilant. The goal is not merely to report that crime has fallen. The goal is to ensure that it stays down—and that every community, regardless of size or location, receives the protection its residents deserve.
