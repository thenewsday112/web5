Why The Mechanic, not The Jackal, Is the Ultimate 1970s Movie Hitman

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In the annals of hitman movies, The Day of the Jackal holds a place of special distinction. 

The 1973 adaptation of Frederick Forsyth’s classic novel boasts a 91 percent critics’ rating on Rotten Tomatoes, and an enviable 88 percent audience rating. No less a luminary than Roger Ebert lauded it as a “a beautifully executed example of filmmaking … put together like a fine watch.” Others described it as a “clockwork amusement,” a “splendid film of thrilling, tense entertainment,” and a “polished and exciting thriller.”

Allow me to dissent, at the risk of controversy. 

If you’re looking for a superior period assassin movie, 1972’s The Mechanic is the discerning consumer’s choice. With an intolerably unjust 50 percent on Rotten Tomatoes, The Mechanic is overdue for a reassessment of its place in the hitman canon. 

Like most successful hitman stories, both Jackal and The Mechanic are competency porn. At the heart of their appeal is the viewer’s appreciation of watching someone excel at their craft. But that appeal is heightened by the nature of the craft—professional murder. 

The viewer wants to root for professionalism, but since the work itself is morally dubious, the assassins must be consummate artists, so incredibly skilled that you cannot help but admire them. This dynamic runs through the entire hitman genre, at the movies, from Le Samouraï to John Wick, and in books such as Barry Eisler’s A Clean Kill in Tokyo and W.J. Weatherby’s Goliath, a 1981 novel about an attempt to assassinate the first American pope. A 2024 prestige TV remake of The Day of the Jackal threads this needle so expertly that you find yourself cheering the assassin in one episode and utterly repelled by him in the next. 

From start to finish, the original Jackal is a deeply flawed movie, with some of its problems emanating from the source material and others all its own, following a high-priced assassin through elaborate preparations to assassinate French President Charles DeGaulle. The protagonist is a strange combination of magically successful and tragically inept—he cleverly throws off investigators by repainting his car after its description is exposed, then wrecks said car in a bit of unnecessarily reckless driving. He effortlessly seduces an unreceptive woman in one scene and carelessly neglects to kill witnesses to her murder in another—a mistake that leads to his downfall. 

The storytelling of The Mechanic is far more effective. The Jackal doesn’t appear until 10 minutes into his namesake movie, and he doesn’t perform a notable act of violence until the 45-minute mark. The Mechanic opens with its titular killer already at work—in a gripping but wordless 15-minute sequence that skillfully showcases his intelligence, methodical approach, and strategic patience, setting the stage for the violence to come. 

While the writing and directing are both superior to its glossier, international counterpart, star Charles Bronson is The Mechanic’s biggest advantage over Jackal. Seen here at the peak of his career, Bronson is intensely physical, visibly tough and strong, but also impressive in his ability to convey intelligence. Again and again, the camera focuses on his shrewd, calculating eyes as he approaches each job like an intricate puzzle. And that analytical instinct is not just limited to the job; we see it play out in social settings as well, with Bronson quietly observing and silently judging some truly bizarre interactions. 

The Jackal’s Edward Fox turns in a performance that is deeply shallow in comparison, projecting what feels like an unearned confidence, physical foppishness combined with an aristocratic British persona, infused a blasé assumption that he is simply up to the task.

In fairness to Fox, the script does him absolutely no favors. The Jackal is enigmatic by design, which some viewers consider an asset. His real name and history are a mystery. His personality barely shows in his extremely limited interactions with other people. We see him succeed at seduction, but we can’t imagine how. There’s no spark with his sexual partner. Maybe he smells good? We’ll never know. The Jackal is only the task, and aside from the task, he is nothing.

The Mechanic gives Bronson a protégé, through whom we learn about his history with the organization that employs him. The movie leaves plenty to the imagination, especially his motivation for the decisions that lead to his undoing, but the writers give you breadcrumbs, and Bronson’s performance is emotionally resonant and suggestive of an explanation. The enigma of The Jackal is meant to be accepted as impenetrable. The mystery of Bronson’s Arthur Bishop invites reflection. The viewer is given enough to encourage speculation, but not enough to draw definite conclusions. You’ll have something to talk about after watching this movie.   

Fictional assassins are often enigmatic by design—we know shockingly little about John Wick, to pick an obvious recent example. But the things we know matter. The undeniable talents of everyone involved aside, it’s hard to imagine John Wick reaching the heights of its commercial and storytelling success without the dog. The competency part of competency porn can only take you so far. You need a little heat to seal the deal. 

That’s also what makes the 2024 remake of The Day of the Jackal shine. When we’re introduced to the lead character’s non-professional life, I had a pretty skeptical reaction. Do I care about the assassin’s domestic problems? Turns out the answer is yes. 

The 1973 Jackal is almost an hour too long compared to the tight 95 minutes of The Mechanic, but the ten episodic hours of the 2024 Jackal are smoothly paced, allowing the assassin’s story and eventually his backstory to unfold gradually, preserving the sense of enigma while still ultimately giving viewers a three-dimensional person to love and hate. In comparison, the 1973 Jackal sometimes feels like an old-school marketing film about assassination, one that a guidance counselor might show high school students, either as a cautionary tale or an unusual “career day” feature. 

The workings of a machine are interesting, but the workings of a person are compelling. The Mechanic is an underrated success at marrying the technical fascinations of the hitman genre with the sweaty, bloody, flawed human at its core. While it’s still also a ’70s movie, with all the flaws and eccentricities that entails, it’s a mile better than its more celebrated counterpart, with enduring lessons for today’s storytellers.

Richard Chizmar on horror, alter egos, and ending his Boogeyman series

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

You can’t kill the boogeyman …

Richard Chizmar, as an unabashed Halloween fan, knows this as well as anyone—and yet that’s exactly what he intends to do in the concluding chapter of his Boogeyman trilogy, Killing the Boogeyman (Gallery Books; October 6, 2026). The book is the latest in a string of bestsellers including Chasing the Boogeyman, Becoming the Boogeyman, Memorials, and Widow’s Point: The Complete Haunting (co-written by his son, W.H. Chizmar).

His work also includes the Gwendy trilogy, two titles of which were written in collaboration with Stephen King. The founder and publisher of Cemetery Dance, Chizmar is a recipient of the Horror Writers of America’s Board of Trustees Award and has won two World Fantasy Awards and four International Horror Guild Awards.

Killing the Boogeyman is a showcase for the author’s genre-bending skills. In true meta fashion, Chizmar is the book’s beleaguered protagonist—and once again finds himself drawn into the web of childhood acquaintance, Joshua Gallagher, who he unmasked as their hometown serial killer in Chasing the Boogeyman.

Then, after achieving a level of fame with his book about the case, Chizmar became Gallagher’s unlikely confidant; some of their jailhouse conversations were sanctioned by the authorities while others were clandestine, conducted via a contraband cell phone and cementing the duo’s codependency. After surviving a series of copycat killers and facing public backlash for his role in sensationalizing the crimes, Chizmar made a vow to cut off contact with the Boogeyman and instead concentrate on rebuilding trust with his wife and children.

But when Gallagher escapes prison, Chizmar knows it’s only a matter of time before he comes calling. With his house under siege by the police, he can only wait and watch—first, when a dead body is found propped up against a tree outside Gallagher’s childhood home and then when his dear friend, reporter Carly Albright, vanishes without a trace.

Finally, a teen girl goes missing from town, the circumstances of her disappearance mirroring Gallagher’s original MO. Wanting to honor his word to his wife and community officials, Chizmar is nevertheless unable to refuse when Gallagher summons him for a third-act reunion. Will their final encounter be Chizmar’s redemption or will he become the latest victim in the Boogeyman’s master plan?

Now, Richard Chizmar talks about resurrecting the Boogeyman in a finale that puts his own character on the line …

*

John B. Valeri: Killing the Boogeyman is the third book in an unintended trilogy. What are the challenges of continuing a story that was initially conceived as a standalone? How does this book standalone while honoring its beginnings?

Richard Chizmar: The main challenge was just logistical issues such as maintaining lists of character names and dates and locations. Matters of continuity. The ongoing saga grew very organically so I definitely found myself making an effort to keep the storyline clear and precise and not too unwieldy. And thank God for those lists! Memory is a tricky thing, especially at my age.

Killing the Boogeyman picks up right where Becoming the Boogeyman ends and carries enough echoes of the first two books to stand all on its own. Thanks to my wonderful editor, Ed Schlesinger, this task was accomplished without any of those annoying “info dumps.”

JBV: Joshua Gallagher is the Boogeyman of these books (though not the only bad guy). What do you see as the quintessential characteristics of such an archetypal character? How does Gallagher both fit that mold and possess an identity that is unique to him?

RC: More than Gallagher’s overall appearance or defining characteristics, for me, it all comes down to that visceral reaction one has in Joshua Gallagher’s presence: a sense of the alien and sinister and unimaginable. Gooseflesh rises on your forearms, the tiny hairs on the back of your neck tingle and stand at attention. He even smells…different.

Now that Gallagher is imprisoned and owning up to his horrible crimes, he’s no longer hiding behind that mask of human normality. He’s a monster. The Boogeyman. 

JBV: “Richard Chizmar”—your fictional alter-ego—is the protagonist of these books. How do the circumstances of the story serve to reveal his greatest strengths and vulnerabilities? What, if anything, have you learned (or come to appreciate more) about yourself through writing?

RC: That was one of my favorite things about writing the Boogeyman books: giving readers a sense of what my everyday real-life looks like, how wonderful my family and friends are, the ups and downs of the writing and publishing worlds, even introducing them to our four dogs(!)…and then also trying to be as honest as possible when it came to how I portrayed myself in relation to the fictional storyline of the murders.

Would I be frightened in this moment or would my obsession take over? Would I be properly sympathetic and respectful to what was happening or would I allow my curiosity to affect my behavior? In most cases, it was a guessing game. I’ve never lived in a small town held hostage by a serial murderer. I’ve never had my life—or the lives of my loved ones—threatened by someone I was once close to.

In light of that, I tried to be accurate and honest in my reactions, even when it didn’t necessarily shine a positive light upon myself. After all, like all the rest of us, I’m only human.

JBV: Much of the story is limited to the Chizmar property. In what ways did that force you to be more innovative in terms of developing and maintaining suspense, both internally and externally? Also, tell us about the juxtaposition of home (our “safe place”) and the insidious nature of evil/horror as it threatens that sanctuary.

RC: Most of us—whether due to illness or bad weather or simple misfortune – have experienced the feeling of being trapped in your own home. Now magnify that imprisoned feeling with being surrounded by an unfriendly swarm of media, nosy lookie-loos, disgruntled townsfolk…and oh yeah, an escaped serial killer. My family and I are truly under siege in Killing the Boogeyman, and I am responsible.

That sense of understanding and guilt permeated my every thought and action throughout the story. I’m a mess! As a result, every detail out of place, every hint at foreboding danger, no matter how minor, took on an enhanced edge.

By the end of the story, I feel like I am losing my mind. Your home is supposed to be the one place you truly feel safe. Especially nowadays with security systems and all the other modern marvels of technology. Take away that sense of safety and the world suddenly feels like it has very sharp teeth.

JBV: Speaking of juxtapositions: You balance a strong sense of nostalgia with contemporary trappings (cell phones, computers, social media, surveillance systems, etc.). How does combining the two enhance the reading experience – and particularly for those who have lived through both ages?

RC: As hinted at above, all this technology is supposed to make life easier…and safer. Yet in many instances, it does the exact opposite. It complicates things and makes them more dangerous. I like playing around with that dynamic. So much of my work is about stripping away those everyday comforts and safeguards, and demonstrating that no matter who you are, where you live, or how much money you have in the bank, life can change on the drop of a dime.

The sense of nostalgia in my stories often performs the same task. It reminds the reader of a simpler—and safer—time … before lowering the boom.

JBV: These books straddle the line between suspense and horror. How do you weigh the considerations of appealing to a mainstream audience with staying true to your genre roots? Also, please tell us about your strongest creative influences and how they can be seen, or felt, in your work.

RC: I honestly give very little thought to the audience when sitting down to write. For me, it’s all about telling the story as honestly as I can…and then hoping and praying the readers will come along for the ride. The Boogeyman books are really just good, old-fashioned campfire stories. There’s a familiar saying: every small town has a haunted house. I think the same can be said for the boogeyman. Pretty much every town has one.

My strongest creative influences are classic storytellers like Stephen King, Robert McCammon, John D. MacDonald, and a much-missed writer by the name of Ed Gorman (readers should run out and grab Ed’s books right away). These folks write with a cinematic eye and a startling clarity, and I believe I’ve learned the most from reading their work.

Ray Bradbury is another influence, particularly his fondness for nostalgia. And of course, anyone who has read the Boogeyman books can spot the numerous nods to my all-time favorite horror movie, the original Halloween.

JBV: Your son, William (aka W.H. Chizmar), is emerging as a talent is his own right as your co-author for Widow’s Point: The Complete Haunting and a debut novel, Them, now available. Tell us about the experience of witnessing his evolution as a writer. In what ways do you think you’ve inspired him—and how has he affected you in return?

RC: It’s been amazing…and a little nerve-wracking at times! Writing is a tough business, fraught with unexpected potholes and twists in the road, so it’s not always easy to sit back and watch someone you love navigate those detours. But mostly it’s been wonderful and such a gift to witness.

It feels like just yesterday that he and I would spend an entire afternoon sitting next to each other on the family room sofa poring over one of those Barnes & Noble bargain-priced coffee-table books about the mysterious and unexplained. Billy has always been a storyteller, starting with drawing his own comics when he was six or seven and interrupting my bedtime stories to revise them to his liking.

My biggest influence is raising him in an environment surrounded by books. When he was growing up, family trips to the library and bookstore were greeted with as much enthusiasm as jaunts to the toy store. Watching his journey as a writer has reenergized my own passion and reminded me of the pure joy of creating new worlds and characters.

JBV: With the Boogeyman behind you (at least for now…because they always come back), what can readers expect next?

RC: I don’t like to talk about works in progress (superstitious and all that…), but hopefully I’ll manage to surprise readers with the next book. It’s very different than the Boogeyman trilogy and I’m having a wonderful time writing it.

Adam Plantinga on writing gritty, tough-guy protagonists in modern crime fiction

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I’ve always been fascinated by tough, formidable protagonists, whether they’re cops, private eyes or Harlan Coben’s standalones where, say, a suburban housewife turns into an intrepid investigator. Early Robert B. Parker’s Spenser is my guy. I favor gritty settings that have something to say about the plight of the inner city—George Pelecanos and Dennis Lehane are masters at this.

I’m drawn to hard-bitten types who make questionable or even regrettable choices. I enjoy Barry Eisler, Andrew Klavan, and Richard Price. They write a crackling tale while dropping hard truths about the human condition.

So I figured I’d try my hand at such a character. His name is Kurt Argento, a former Detroit SWAT cop. He’s a throwback to a simpler time, rough around the edges but a student of the policing game who won’t ever walk away from a fight. He’s no detective, but he’s relentless with good instincts.

Most importantly, for the purpose of this piece, in the words of Robert B. Parker, he’s “tough as the rails on a picket fence.” He policed Detroit, a tough city. He says tough things. He goes up against other tough guys. It’s all toughness all the time.

So what does the modern tough guy look like? For one, he knows the neighborhoods he walks through because so does the author. George Pelecanos worked as a dishwasher, a shoe salesman, and a bartender before he wrote convincingly about the blue collar worlds of his characters in Washington D.C. Michael Connelly was a crime beat writer before introducing the world to intense, driven L.A. homicide detective Harry Bosch. I feel comfortable writing Kurt Argento because I’m a twenty-five-year city cop and have seen and heard things on the job that I will sometimes just drop right into a novel.

These worlds the modern tough guy inhabits are often like chaotic obstacle courses. It’s a dirt-under-the-fingernails journey. Think Dan Simmons’ bleak, ice-strewn Buffalo where Joe Kurtz calls home or James Lee Burke’s Dave Robicheaux in a simmering New Orleans underworld. Argento’s debut was in The Ascent, where he finds himself in a maximum security prison when the computer system goes haywire, freeing the most dangerous inmates in the state. Mayhem ensues.

The modern tough guy knows violence. Jack Reacher can head-butt a bad guy into orbit. S.A. Cosby’s Bug Montage from Blacktop Wasteland will show you your own teeth. Barry Eisler’s ultra-lethal assassin John Rain once killed a man with a bench press bar.

But these days, modern crime writers strive to make their protagonists violent but more thoughtfully so. In my novels, Argento goes against some bad actors. Violence is the only language they understand. But although I’m writing fiction, I try to make the violence have weight to it, physically, mentally, and morally.

Every fight extracts a price. Kurt Argento isn’t a Walk-Into-a-Bar-and-Take-Out-Three-Bad-Guys-Without-Getting-a-Scratch-On-Him character. He’s a Walk-Into-a-Bar-and-Take-Out-Two-Bad-Guys-but-Sustain-a-Concussion-and-a-Moderate-Stab-Wound-kind of guy. I’m interested in writing an ultra-competent hero, but not Superman. It can be hard to make Superman interesting. The modern tough guy should have vulnerabilities. Sometimes he’ll fail.

All that being said, you can overdo it with all the toughness. Writer and editor Jim Thomsen says this about a certain such book: “It’s full of look-at-me prose that distracts from the story…interminable page after page showing men brooding and smoking and staring hauntedly into the burning American middle distance. It’s like its own parody of masculine literary tropes.”

I try hard to avoid that. Don’t get me wrong, I deal in tropes. Argento is a rough man alone in the world, an ex-cop with no real home and a dead wife, trying to find his way. That’s been done before. I just try to put my own stamp on it.

The modern tough guy may not emote much but there needs to be something going on below the surface. He tends to muse on things like courage, grief, and issues of crime and punishment.

And ideally the tough guy can’t just muscle his way around. He has to adapt to the problem in front of him and puzzle his way through. He may need friends to accomplish this. Walter Mosley’s Easy Rollins has Mouse. Elvis Cole has Joe Pike. But the backup needn’t always be another tough guy. Argento’s allies in my novels have included a criminology graduate student and, in my latest book Dark Country, a seventeen-year old Syrian refugee named Majed.

What else? The modern tough guy knows how to combat-breathe: five seconds in, hold for five, five seconds out. He’s aware a car engine block is appropriate ballistic cover, but a car door is not. He’s probably lousy with computers (a notable exception to this is Gregg Hurwitz’s sublime shadow operative Evan Smoak). He can be coldly practical when he has to; in The Ascent, Argento is saddled with inferior footwear, so he borrows the shoes off a dead prison warden. Better traction for the dangers that lay ahead.

But in the end, we come back to toughness. The modern tough guy goes hard. Even when he’s tired or hurt. Even when the odds are stacked against him. He’s mission-driven and presses forward because it’s always forward. He’s got grit and tenacity in spades. If you like these kinds of stories as I do, at some fundamental level, you’re looking for the same thing I am in the modern tough guy.

Someone who, when shit goes awry, can really bring it.

In the first installment in our Benighted series, Shino Jomoto shares with Climbing the weird and spooky tale from her night in the chimney on September 17, 2023—and what all climbers can learn from her epic. 

With over two decades of climbing experience, Jomoto specializes in epics, which she defines as getting sandbagged on adventure multi-pitch climbs and big walls that take far longer than anticipated. But her biggest epic of all occurred in the storied chimney route (5.10a) up Lost Arrow Spire in Yosemite. 

Earlier this week, a Yosemite Climbing Association (YCA) newsletter detailed the history of the Lost Arrow Spire, including the chimney route. First ascensionist John Salathé himself bailed near the top in 1946. The next year, he came back “for an all-out effort” that took over five days. “Pushing through the claustrophobic shadows of the Lost Arrow Chimney, Salathé and [Anton] Nelson endured searing heat, dehydration, and brutal physical exertion,” the YCA shared. “They spent four bivouacs dangling from crude hammocks tied to pitons driven into the wall.”

The Lost Arrow Chimney is also believed to contain a human skeleton. On March 19, 1961, 17-year-old Irving Smith died in a rappelling accident from the top. An American Alpine Club trip report shared that Smiths’ body remains in the chimney: “While a ranger and volunteer rescue party was preparing to attempt the difficult and dangerous removal, Smith’s parents and Park authorities determined that no further life should be risked, and that the body should remain at rest where it lay.” 

A comment on the Mountain Project page of Yosemite’s Lost Arrow Chimney (5.10a) foreshadowed what was to come: “Don’t let the moderate grade fool you. After pitch 7, you are halfway done.” The Lost Arrow Chimney is 10 pitches.

Poor fitness, dehydration, and sleep deprivation conspired, and by pitch eight we needed headlamps. Too tired to continue, we did the only sensible thing: slept inside the chimney.

I started climbing in 2005, mostly out of a hatred for my corporate job. That led to years living out of a station wagon, trying to figure out how to rock climb. Fast forward decades later, I’m in the business of going on epics—a lot.

Jim Herson said you could write an entire print edition on just my epics alone. I’ve had near-death epics like lightning and hypothermia on a big wall. But I’ll put it this way: I can climb El Cap faster than I can climb the Lost Arrow Chimney.

Why the Lost Arrow Chimney eats its young

Jomoto’s partner John Cannon moving his way up Lost Arrow Chimney. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

The reason why I wanted to share this is that nine out of ten climbers will have an epic on the Lost Arrow. You might be able to climb 5.13, but try to climb 10 pitches of old-school chimney—see if you can still call yourself a 5.10 climber. I think everyone goes in with the confidence of the grade. Then they just get gut-punched by the old-schoolness of the climb.

On my Lost Arrow Chimney epic, we weren’t in danger. We never called YOSAR (Yosemite Search and Rescue). You’re just humbled in a silly way because it’s such a silly grade.

Aside from the likes of Chuck Pratt or old-school climbers, I don’t know anyone who’s ever redone this route. When you do a climb, you usually redo it. You want a redpoint or you go back again. Nobody is dumb enough to redo this climb. We’ve done it three times, so it really goes to show either how incredibly stupid we are, or how memorable of an epic it was that we just needed to clear the record.

The lore that Jim Herson shared with me was he knew a guy who climbed the Lost Arrow Chimney and then never rock climbed again. The reality is he went to med school and gave up climbing. But that was his last-ever climb. It just embodies the spirit of the Lost Arrow Chimney.

Classic gumby moves

Climbing through the night on Jomoto’s first experience in Lost Arrow Chimney on October 5, 2019. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

The first time John and I did Lost Arrow Chimney in 26 hours on October 5, 2019 as a party of three, and we were actually in decent shape.

The time we slept in the chimney a few years later, we were a party of two. We thought we were fit. But we did all the classic gumby moves. We got up too late and got lost on the approach. To get back on route, we had to rappel. It’s obvious on a topo where you should go, but at four o’clock in the morning, you get lost. And we didn’t bring enough water.

On the climb, we started losing daylight. My boyfriend started dry heaving from dehydration and lack of fitness. He stopped mid-pitch in a chimney—he couldn’t go on.

There’s nothing you can really say in the dark. We’re not Rocky Talkie people. We have too much pride for that. So we just had to communicate with a rope. Finally, I was on belay. I went up to him. We were mid-chimney—this wasn’t even a belay station.

Jomoto’s partner John Cannon atop Lost Arrow Spire on his third successful mission. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

John was like, “I can’t. I can’t do this anymore.” And he’s a 10-times better climber than I am. So I was like, “All right, I’ll go.” He was like, “Are you sure?” I was like, “We can’t sleep here. We can sleep in a lot of places. This is not a place to sleep.”

It’s always scary when a very experienced person says, “I’m scared.” John rarely says he’s scared. It takes courage to say that. When you’re in the dark, there’s nothing you do. You can’t start yelling and placating, you know? You just realize in that moment like, it’s a serious climb. And you’re still having to reconcile that with 10a.

Our night in a chimney  

So I went questing and found this chimney that was adjacent to the climb. It wasn’t a chimney you go through on route, but everyone goes by it.

We were two pitches from the notch. Should we suffer for six hours, then rappel and call it a day? Or suffer through the whole night? You make those calculations. We decided we might as well stay put, rather than burden resources to call for YOSAR or any sort of search and rescue. I think that’s the number one thing: Can you self-rescue?

A photo of a topo in a guidebook, with the approximate bivy location encircled. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

We had brought a salad that we thought we were going to eat at the base. So we ended up at the bivy with a salad, but no water. It’s kind of hard to eat a salad without water. So all of my swear words were salad- or foliage-related. It doesn’t matter if you have a whole buffet, if you have no water, you don’t want to eat anything. So we were just staring at this salad in the chimney. It’s one of those things with the Maslow’s hierarchy—if you don’t have water, you can’t do anything.

We laid out both climbing ropes so that we were a little bit insulated from the ground. We did the bivy dance. It was flatter than most bivy spots I have slept on. We were really depressed, just waiting for daylight.

The bivy location where Jomoto and her partner spent the night. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

Back sleeping was impossible, so we were relegated to side sleeping for the next six hours. There was no way to sleep besides big spoon, little spoon. The walls were very cold. I put a giant sad face in chalk where I was. Another group went and did it later and said, “We knew where you bivvied ’cause there was a giant sad face.”

Daylight came.

But we were so thirsty, the epic wasn’t over. We could hear Yosemite Falls. Our brains went: Maybe we can just hike over there. Another lesson is you’re not rational when you’re having an epic. So we lost another half day going to the falls. We were sitting in a pool of water that we couldn’t drink. And we realized we couldn’t cut over. So we had to go back. A day and a half later, we got back to the car.

Yosemite Falls sounds deceptively close to climbers on the Lost Arrow Spire—but remains out of reach. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

The Lost Arrow Chimney Support Group

Finally, on June 2, 2024, we got our redemption. We did it in a day and tagged the summit where people do the Tyrolean. It still took us all day—and we didn’t even free it. We had to pull on gear. It makes you respect the history of the first ascensionists—the lineage of people who have tried it and who have bailed. There’s an insane amount of really, really old bail slings.

You’re in there and it’s a vortex. It’s spooky. I’m not a mystical person, but there are hexes that have been kind of swallowed by the rock. It’s a freaking spooky museum of gear. When you’re in there, you have an appreciation for who climbed this before you and the style in which they climbed it, because it is terrifying.

Jomoto’s partner in their bivy spot at first light after a long, cold night. (Photo: Shino Jomoto)

I feel like I’ve gotten my chimney badge in Yosemite. There should be a club for it: the Lost Arrow Chimney Support Group. If you can summit the whole climb and rappel in a day, I would have mad respect. More people climb the Nose than the Lost Arrow Chimney. Anyone who’s dumb enough and adventurous enough to sign up for that level of masochism will resonate more with me than if you’re C1ing a 30-pitch climb.

If you survive nine or ten pitches of chimneys with your partner, you will be bonded forever, for better or worse. They’ve done all these studies about reality TV shows, like for love matchmaking. You put people in intense situations and they confuse their endorphins as romance, but it’s really just adrenaline. Part of it is trauma bonding. Some couples are more willing to climb the Lost Arrow Chimney than go to couples therapy.

The beauty of getting benighted

“If you don’t have some delusional optimism, you will never get off the ground.” (Photo: Shino Jomoto )

The beauty of epics is there are way more fun memories than sending. Every sending story is so ephemeral. But when you’ve been sucker-punched so hard by something scary, or that requires lack of sleep, or incredible amounts of suffering, you remember those experiences for decades.

If you don’t have some delusional optimism, you will never get off the ground. That delusion will catch up to you at some point. But you have to have a little bit of fuel. As long as it’s not over your head or dangerous for you and others. Can you live well and safely enough to live another day? Go do it.

Delusion meets reality at some point, like when you’re having an epic. Benighted is a subcategory of epics—it’s in the taxonomy. There has to be some darkness involved, generally speaking, but you don’t need to have slept.

The easy way to know is when you end up at the parking lot, you see your best friends, and they’re like, “How was it?” The face doesn’t lie. You actually don’t need to say words. They see your face and they’re like, “That was an epic.”

John and I have an epic grading scale. It’s the reverse GPA.

  • 1.0: You’re back without headlamps—no epic
  • 2.0: You’re wearing headlamps on the approach
  • 3.0: You’re rappelling or descending off the climb with headlamps
  • 4.0: You’re still climbing and you need a headlamp to get off of the climb

Every time we finish, we rate them. The Lost Arrow Chimney was like a 5.0.

ADHD and the Menopause Experience

A woman who has gone 12 months without a menstrual period is in menopause. The median age for menopause is 51, though many women with ADHD are known to experience the onset earlier.

Declining estrogen levels during perimenopause and menopause are associated with reduced dopamine, leading to worsening ADHD symptoms and emotional dysregulation in many women – even those who had previously managed their ADHD symptoms well with medication, according to a separate study published in the Journal of Attention Disorders.

Off Hours: How Landscape Design Star Cleve West Survives His Day Job

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

What does a top garden designer do to relax? He gardens, of course—but not exactly where you’d expect. Cleve West, winner of seven Chelsea Flower Show RHS Gold Medals, spends his free time tending to a plot that couldn’t be more different from the conceptual spaces he creates for clients. Not especially private and the opposite of chic, his patch is in a West London community garden (in the UK it is called an allotment) and has been his personal project for a decade.

Cleve’s 2011 book, Our Plot, part memoir, part manual, part state-of-the-world discourse, takes us through the highs and lows of allotment life. Meanwhile, we’ve been invited for lunch:

Photography by Andrew Montgomery.

Above: “The oven is like a hearth bringing people together,” says Cleve. “It has made me love the plot even more.”

Cleve West is the Gary Cooper of the garden design world. Modest, sincere, assured, he is admired by all. Did I mention that he can cook? Besides growing vegetables, fruit, and flowers, Cleve does a surprising amount of entertaining in the allotment that he shares with his partner, Christine Eatwell.

Above: After much experimentation, Cleve followed Kiko Denzer’s instructions in the book Build Your Own Earth Oven.

They use a variety of garden-side cooking contraptions: a grill, a gas-fired oven, and a hand-built pizza oven. “Many hours are spent not growing vegetables,” admits Cleve, shown here with his grill, picked up in Normandy, and the impressive pizza oven, which has a brick base, mud and sawdust dome, and bottles placed in the middle to stop the heat from going down.

Above: “Allotments are not always that attractive from a Chelsea judging point of view,” says Cleve. His plot, which is on the large side at over 2,700 square feet, is designed around a series of raised beds.

“Some people think that the word ‘design’ is a little too pretentious and would rather their garden ‘evolve’ over the years,” says Cleve. “But how you arrange your plot, whether you draw it on paper or not, is design.” His intriguing wild tangle—I mean design—contains a rotation of vegetables including squash, beans, leaks, artichoke, onions, and gladioli by the shed.

Above: Corn and cabbage under netting (to keep out pigeons), and, in the foreground, gauzy purple verbena bonariensis.

Cleve’s client-based designs are characterized by sculptural forms and so, too, is his allotment. Wigwams made from coppiced hazel are finished off with heavy duty chains instead of insubstantial twine. They support beans and gourds, and last for about a season.

Above: This shed, with its old corrugated iron roof and reclaimed door, is armed with a lock, which is perhaps its main point. There is no formal security in allotments so essential items are kept under lock and key.

Recycle, improvise, and modify. This is the heart of the British community garden ethos. “If I had control over all the allotments, I would ban pre-fabricated sheds,” says Cleve. “I’d urge people to make their own in the name of local distinctiveness and individuality.”

Above: Cleve, shown here, prepares bhajis, Indian vegetable fritters, from a recipe handed down from his grandmother who was born in Calcutta. Both she and Cleve’s mother were in the movies, which may explain some of Cleve’s star quality.

Cooking at the plot intensifies the enjoyment of fresh food. Basic ingredients—seasoning, spices, oil, and vinegar—are kept in one of the sheds, but key elements, such as pizza dough, are made at home and carried in. True, cooking and entertaining without electricity and hot water involves extra work, but chopping and frying onions is far preferable out of doors than in.

Above: Grilling eggplant and green tomatoes for a chutney.

Feeling inspired? Here’s Cleve’s recipe for a simple relish made on a barbecue or makeshift grill. He promises that allotment cooking “doesn’t get much easier.” Pair with naan or pita bread for a quick lunch.

Blackened Eggplant Chutney

Serves 2-4

Ingredients:

  • 1 medium eggplant
  • A handful of green tomatoes
  • Some chopped fresh chili (up to half a teaspoonful)
  • 1 clove garlic
  • Small bunch of cilantro
  • Salt and pepper to taste
  • Good squeeze of lemon juice

Instructions:

Put the eggplant and green tomatoes on the barbecue or open fire and char them until they are black and “all squidgy” inside. Then cut along the middle and scoop out the contents of both eggplants and tomatoes. Mash, add the rest of the ingredients, mix, and eat!

Above: Bonfires are considered anti-social in summer, but in autumn and winter they are common practice as a way to burn garden waste. Cleve suggests using these fires as a feast opportunity: roast chestnuts and freshly-dug potatoes amid the embers. Nothing will ever taste so good.
