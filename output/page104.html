As South Carolinians prepare for Election Day on November 3, some are considering how their vote could affect the governor-appointed South Carolina Arts Commission (SCAC). In August, the agency established new guidelines prohibiting the funding of work that promotes "any ideology," echoing Trump’s rhetoric, and denied funding to an artist and two LGBTQ+ performing arts organizations earlier this month.

Comprised of nine governor-appointed volunteers, the SCAC has distributed over $132 million in grants to artists, schools, and cultural organizations across South Carolina since Governor Robert E. McNair established it in 1967. Over the past year, the agency has come under scrutiny for singling out LGBTQ+ arts groups in its grant decisions and implementing opaque funding restrictions. After an August 20 board meeting, the commission announced that grantees cannot direct funds to "promote any ideology."

"Without a clear definition of 'ideology' or 'promotion,' the new rule can be used to suppress almost any cultural activity that touches on society, history, identity, religion, science, or politics," the National Coalition Against Censorship (NCAC) wrote in a statement published yesterday, October 28, condemning the SCAC. The grant rule echoes President Donald Trump's targeting of federal grant funding for projects related to "gender ideology," a vague term used to push essentialist and anti-trans concepts of gender.

"SCAC's adoption of a new grant rule after proposals had been independently reviewed and vetted is a problem in itself," Elizabeth Larison, director of arts and culture advocacy at NCAC, added in a statement to Hyperallergic. "But the fact that this rule is vague enough to allow government appointees to veto any projects they don't personally like is a threat to artistic freedom and democratic principles in South Carolina."

In line with my belief that it’s important to always keep the resumé fresh, I signed up for first aid training six months ago and the first class is today. The Nine Circles exalts dying and death, but not for guests. Plus, most of us sit for nine hours a day and eat greasy meals at lunch, putting us at risk for croaking in our cubicles. A knowledge of first aid does not help me do my back-of-house job better, though if a colleague has a heart attack at work, I’ll be able to perform cpr while waiting for the paramedics to arrive. Even though we’ll be taking an exam during the final class, I’m treating these training sessions as a vacation from my desk. The best part is that Charles and Charlotte are also taking the course; he’s doing it because he cares about the welfare of other employees and she’s doing it because she’ll be on park grounds from time to time working on the new attractions.

Three months ago, Human Resources sorted everyone with a Chinese surname into the Cantonese training sessions without first checking whether we could read or write the language. HR’s mandate seems to be cart before horse. Although it was embarrassing to admit that I was illiterate except for menus and select street signs, I reported that my understanding of body part names and medical terminology was limited to English. (What’s the word for spleen in Cantonese? I still don’t know.) Human Resources Associate Gallant Sum instructed me to take the course on evenings and weekends at a community centre with our expat colleagues—the Nine Circles would still pay the fees, he assured me, but the company would not consider these thirty hours as overtime. But then it turned out that the executive team wanted some of the bilingual Hong Kong staff members to take the course in English to better serve international guests and vips, so Human Resources had to scramble to implement these classes on top of the final push to hire an additional hundred people to round out the team.

Charles and Charlotte are already in the meeting room when I arrive at 9:12 a.m., the official start time at the Nine Circles.

“Is this an actual first aid training session or a mass firing?” Charlotte asks, as she scans the room to see which colleagues are present.

“My tour with Pamela Poon, Raymond Shek, and Jade Chen Church went pretty poorly yesterday, so I can imagine why I’d be fired,” I say.

“I’m sure it wasn’t as bad as you think,” Charlotte says.

“Well, I didn’t make any serial killer jokes this time,” I say. Then I’m relieved to spot the Secretary to the Head of Project Delivery. “Helen Chan’s here, and I don’t think they would fire any of the secretaries. Everything would come to a stop if even one was terminated.”

“She’s pretty essential to the park opening on time,” Charles says. “Remember when she went on vacation?” When Helen took her annual leave to get married and go on her honeymoon, the paperwork for the air filtration system for the Afterlife lab went missing and Project Delivery started construction without understanding that the omission of this system violated building code. Charlotte spotted the error and reported it to the executive team and managerial staff. She noted that without air filtration it would be unsafe to display some of our most famous corpses. No one listened to her because she’s not head of her department. The Chaplins and I discovered, during an investigation that we conducted on our own time, that a Project Delivery manager had buried the paperwork to save money, but his gamble resulted in a million-dollar mistake. For reasons unknown, that manager still has a job at the Nine Circles, and somehow Helen got written up for this incident despite eight underlings reporting to the executive team that it was not her fault.

I have one of the jobs that is considered nonessential. I don’t fetch coffee or set up conference calls or schedule important meetings or handle corpses.

What I don’t say is that out of everyone in the training room, most of whom are junior staff from Administration, I have one of the jobs that is considered nonessential. I don’t fetch coffee or set up conference calls or schedule important meetings or handle corpses. No one up on aside from the Executive Director seems to understand that marketing is necessary to get paying guests through the turnstiles. Mirabella Lam, Educational Programmes Manager, believes that the high quality of her programming is enough to draw a crowd and insists that trying to attract attention is crass.

“But how will the public know about your projects if we don’t spread the word?” I once asked Mirabella during an all-team meeting. “We do good work,” she said, directing her comment toward the Executive Director. The ceo, Dalton Northcott, was not in attendance that day, or Mirabella would have addressed him instead. One can sum up Mirabella’s work philosophy as “managing up.” I later discovered I’d made a sworn enemy with my well-meaning query. Mirabella is employee ten, so even though she doesn’t outrank most of us in terms of the org chart, she wields her seniority as a sort of authority.

Many underlings have never met the ceo. With his travel schedule, he’s rarely at the Hong Kong offices. Early on, when the team was smaller, I met Dalton at the groundbreaking ceremony and asked him how to keep my houseplants alive—I’d read that he had a PhD in botany and an impressive orchid collection. His response was warm and engaging, yet I could sense he had boundaries in place that prevented the interaction from becoming overfamiliar. This gave me a false sense of safety—I believed the ceo was a leader I could trust.

The first aid teacher arrives and does not introduce himself. Instead, he says, “Where are we going to eat lunch?” Then he wipes his sweaty forehead with a faded pink towel.

There’s a ten-second silence that feels like an eternity. Many of the participants in this training session are under twenty-five and are too shy to make eye contact when they do their rounds with the mail or set up a projector for meetings. There is no way any of us are going to give up our lunch hour of rest (or work catch-up) to entertain our first aid teacher, but we have to figure out a diplomatic way to communicate this information.

“Most of us brought our lunches,” says Charlotte.

“There’s a food court downstairs in the mall, as well as a supermarket,” I say. We were supposed to have moved into the offices built on park grounds six months ago, but the sprinkler system keeps going off and no one can figure out how to fix it, so now they’re pulling out the entire thing and reinstalling it and most of us are still in our rented office space in the mall. On the way in every morning, I pass Celine, Chanel, and Louis Vuitton and see the salespeople lovingly dusting handbags that cost more than my monthly salary.

The teacher looks disappointed. Did he imagine we’d want to discuss the finer points of guts and gore while eating dumplings? When it’s clear no one is going to invite him to lunch, he opens his PowerPoint presentation. The first slide features a grainy black-and-white scan from a newspaper.

“What’s happening here?” he shouts. None of us can figure out what’s depicted in the image.

The blobs could be a cat playing the piano. “Come on!” he says. “What would you do if you came across this accident?”

“The photo is a bit on the blurry side,” says Charles, ever polite.

“Common sense! First thing at a scene, no illegal touch!” says the teacher.

I wonder if he’s had complaints against him while administering cpr. I don’t remember any instruction about illegal touch from my previous first aid training sessions in Canada. Icarus Sun from Administration, whose clothes always look shrunken in a cool streetwear way, is taking notes. I wonder if he’s writing Do not molest the injured person.

The room is airless, making me sluggish. I yawn and unzip my jacket. “Wah,” says the teacher, switching to Cantonese. His eyes are on my tits. “I thought you were going to take it all off.”

I zip my jacket back up. Charlotte and I look at each other. “What a perv,” she whispers. No one else in the room seems to have noticed what has happened, not even the Human Resources Associate who is observing the session to ensure everything goes smoothly, so I let it pass. “In a few weeks, I will introduce you to a beautiful lady for cpr,” the teacher says. “Her name is Annie.” Icarus is still taking notes. CPR on beautiful lady! “But what’s going on in the slide?” asks Charlotte.

“You will understand when you meet the beautiful lady!” the first aid teacher says with a wide smile on his face. As the minutes pass like snails trying to cross a freeway during rush hour, I think about all the unanswered emails in my inbox and slip into a dissociative state.

Quartz countertops have long been a favorite choice of homeowners and interior designers alike due to their strength, nonporous nature, modern look, and uniformity. However, recent discoveries and legal challenges regarding the material’s potential health hazards to fabricators have caused quite a stir and are now reshaping the industry. 

The main issue is with crystalline silica that’s a natural mineral found in quartz. When particles are inhaled, they can penetrate deep into a person’s lungs, causing damage that can lead to serious respiratory diseases like silicosis, lung cancer, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). And many workers have become ill from breathing in dust containing crystalline silica while fabricating quartz countertops. Australia has already banned many engineered-stone products, while California is pursuing policy rules that would prohibit fabricating engineered stone containing more than 1 percent crystalline silica.

As a result, more and more people are looking for safer countertop options to use in place of quartz. “We have found some homeowners shying away from quartz surfaces,” says Kaycee Conallen, owner of Kaycee Conallen Design Studio in Swarthmore, Pennsylvania. There are many alternatives to quartz countertops, and designers have helped us identify three top options for your new home or next remodel.

Note: There is no evidence indicating a health concern with already installed quartz countertops, so if you already have one, you don’t need to rip it out.

Photo: Michelle – stock.adobe.com | Recycled glass

Recycled and Composite Surfaces

One alternative to quartz countertops is eco-friendly, silica-free alternatives like recycled glass and paper composites. Both eliminate silica risks while providing unique designs ranging from bold, contemporary glass patterns to sleek, solid-color paper finishes.

Recycled glass countertops incorporate post-consumer or post-industrial glass fragments bound in cement or resin, offering distinct visual textures from fine-ground particles to large, jewel-like shards. Notable glass brands include IceStone, Vetrazzo, Curava, GEOS, and Compac Obsidiana Zero. Glass surfaces are effective at resisting cuts, scratches, and heat, though stain-resistance and maintenance depend on whether a resin or cement binder is used.

Paper-based composites work well for those gravitating towards a solid color and solid surface materials, such as PaperStone, Richlite, and Fenix brands. Surprisingly durable, these heavy-duty panels are made from layers of post-consumer recycled paper or paper from FSC-certified forests, which are compressed under heat and pressure with a silica-free and petroleum-free phenolic resin. 

While heat resistant to about 350 degrees Fahrenheit, minor scratches and stains may appear, but can be sanded out. “Fenix is a man-made resin and paper combination originally developed in Italy, and we love that it has a beautiful matte finish, feels soft to the touch, and is very durable,” explains Conallen. 

Sintered Stone

“Sintered stone is another strong option for its resistance to heat, scratches, and stains,” notes Blima Ehrentreu, founder and CEO of The Designers Group (TDG) in New York City and other locations. Using a high-tech process, natural minerals like porcelain and glass are combined under immense heat and pressure to replicate metamorphic rock with minimal to zero crystalline silica. 

The non-porous nature of sintered stone makes it resistant to staining, scratches, intense heat, and color-fading from UV exposure. Brands that make this type of surface include Lapitec, Dekton, Neolith, Florim, Sapienstone, and Caesarstone, which are actively eliminating or reducing silica content. In addition, many of these surfaces contain recycled content.

Photo: R_Yosha – stock.adobe.com | Soapstone

Soapstone

Conallen also loves the look of authentic materials like soapstone. “Each slab of stone carries unique characteristics, personality, and porosity,” she explains. Composed mostly of talc, this metamorphic rock is free of crystalline silica and has a distinctive, velvety feel. Soapstone offers design versatility, as it ranges in color from subtle grays and muted greens to charcoal or deep black, sometimes accented by marble-like veining. Not only does it offer a classic, understated elegance to modern spaces, but it is also a sustainable option given its low-energy extraction process, nontoxic nature, and recyclability.

From a functional standpoint, soapstone is very durable. It withstands heat, so you can set hot cookware directly onto the surface without trivets. It’s also nonporous so it doesn’t get damaged by acidic etching, spills, or bacterial growth. The only drawback is that it’s a softer material than most of the other options mentioned, so it can show minor scratches.

An on-chain pattern that has repeatedly preceded major Bitcoin advances is once again worth watching. Historically, when the share of Bitcoin held for more than 18 months across the network climbs and begins to fall, a strong rally has historically been observed. That shift signals long-term holders starting to move coins, often coinciding with the early phases of sustained upside.

According to a recent CryptoQuant analysis, the crypto market has already entered a bullish cycle. Yet conditions still look early. Interestingly, the classic long-term holder signal has not even started to appear. This suggests both meaningful upside potential and room for the advance to develop over time rather than unfolding in a short, sharp burst.

The broader sentiment picture supports this view. Most market players appear uninterested in crypto investment according to the recent report. Even among those paying attention, most are still on the sidelines rather than actively deploying capital. The market shows few signs of overheating. In that sense, the real rally may not have properly begun.

The data still favors a constructive stance among crypto investors. Markets remain in the early phase of the cycle, long-term holders show none of the distribution typically seen near tops, and retail interest stays subdued.
