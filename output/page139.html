West Ridge or Hornbein Couloir? Everest Team Prepares for Fourth Attempt

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Russian team is preparing to head up Everest's West Ridge for a fourth time. While no easy option exists, they want to avoid the extremely exposed, difficult, and uncertain ridge route. Their only alternative is the Hornbein Couloir, which would force them to stray temporarily into Tibet.

Andrei Vasiliev, Vitaly Shipilov, and Sergey Kondrashkin are in Kathmandu recovering from a viral infection and waiting out Cyclone Arnab, which is now weakening. They will return to their base in Lobuche this weekend, determined to climb Everest without supplemental oxygen.

Everest West Ridge some days ago. Photo: Andrei Vasiliev

Progress so far

"So far, we have completed three rotations on the mountain," leader Vasiliev told ExplorersWeb. "Part of this time was spent exploring different options for accessing the route."

The Russian team has a permit to climb Everest from Nepal, as long as they attempt a route other than the standard Khumbu Icefall-South Col-Southeast Ridge. This has become the normal South Side route since Hillary and Tenzing's first ascent in 1953.

Topo of the Russian team's planned route on Everest, courtesy of Andrei Vasiliev

 

First, the Russians tried to bypass the Khumbu Icefall via the Nuptse Shoulder toward Camp 1, but heavy snow forced them to find another route. Crossing the Icefall (sharing paths with the normal route) was also too hard in the deep snow and on the shaky snow bridges over the innumerable crevasses.

"Eventually, we selected a third option, via the Lho La," Vasiliev said. "From that pass, our planned route continues across the West Shoulder, along the West Ridge."

At the Lho La col. Photo: Andrei Vasiliev

 

On their latest foray, the team reached 6,400m. "We climbed approximately twelve 60m pitches over rock, snow, and ice of moderate difficulty," said Vasiliev.

He noted that on the way to the West Shoulder, they found traces of previous expeditions, including old ropes and snow anchors.

As for the terrain ahead of them, Vasiliev said:

From our highest point reached, the terrain ahead looks manageable. We can see two more steep rock steps, each approximately 100m high, along with possible bypasses on steep snow slopes. Beyond these obstacles, the terrain appears easier, and we hope to be able to move simultaneously.

Rocky section on the way to Everest's West Ridge. Photo: Andrei Vasiliev

 

Vasiliev and his team are confident they can manage this route, despite doing so in increasingly wintry conditions.

Hornbein Couloir?

The route linking the Lho La, the West Shoulder, and the West Ridge of Everest has been completed only twice. A Yugoslavian team led by Tone Skarja did the first complete ascent in 1979. They used the Hornbein Couloir variation on their descent.

The only repetition was done by a Bulgarian team in 1984. Hristo Prodanov reached the summit on his own and retraced his steps down the ridge but never made it back alive. Some of his partners who summited days later descended via the normal route. Three other teams who climbed the West Shoulder-West Ridge summited by traversing to the North Face, on the Chinese side, and climbing the Hornbein Couloir.

The North Face of Everest from the West Shoulder. Photo: Andrei Vasiliev

 

Objectively speaking, the Hornbein Couloir offers more summit (and survival) chances than the upper part of the West Ridge. However, it requires traversing into Chinese territory. The Russian team has a permit to climb only from Nepal. It is unclear if a brief border trespass could be considered, and Vasiliev has not confirmed what they will do. "First we need to complete one more rotation," he said.

Opinion: We urgently need to monitor AI agents’ actions in real time

—Madhulika Srikumar and Claire Leibowicz from the Partnership on AI, a nonprofit advancing best practices in AI governance.

AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, from hacking websites to accessing private government files. In many cases, they’ve eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. 

As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent potentially devastating consequences. And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability.

​​In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. Because right now, we’re effectively flying blind when deploying these tools. 

Here’s what needs to change.

Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

Climate change itself increasingly fuels climate change. Rising greenhouse gas concentrations are driving up global temperatures, creating dangerous feedback loops that aren’t fully represented in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals.

Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming. Now, a growing number of observations and studies are indicating how powerful these cascading effects could be. By extension, they’re also showing how much faster the planet may warm this century.

Turning Point rests its reputation on Andy Biggs’ race. It’s not looking good.

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Turning Point USA has gone all-in to make GOP Rep. Andy Biggs the next governor of Arizona, their top priority of the cycle.

It appears they will come up short.

Biggs was the last candidate Turning Point founder Charlie Kirk personally endorsed before he was killed one year ago. Kirk himself recruited the longtime ally in their home state to run for governor this cycle — and Turning Point has since spent $18 million, the most they’ve dropped for anyone this cycle, to try to salvage his campaign.

“It's a legacy thing for them,” said former Rep. Matt Salmon (R-Ariz.). “This is their swan song. This is kind of all-or-nothing for them.”

But Biggs, a former chair of the hardline House Freedom Caucus and close ally of President Donald Trump who previously served as Arizona Senate president, has trailed Democratic Gov. Katie Hobbs in virtually every public poll of the race this year, most of them outside the margin of error. And some Arizona Republicans have been baffled by Turning Point’s strategy.

The group faces immense criticism from within the state GOP for delaying its biggest spending until September, just weeks before ballots were sent out, according to conversations with 10 local operatives and officials.

Biggs’ race is the most high-stakes political test for Turning Point Action since Kirk’s 2025 death, and the result will have major implications for the group’s reputation and relevancy within the GOP moving forward. Turning Point has already preemptively endorsed Vice President JD Vance, who was particularly close to Kirk, for president. Should Vance decide to run, he could rely on Turning Point’s ground operation in swing states like Arizona and beyond.

The group could well overperform this year, and if they are successful in lifting Biggs in their home state, it could send a signal. But a loss could have 2028 repercussions, too.

“If JD Vance intends to run for president, I think it would be a good idea to reevaluate any thoughts he might have about utilizing Turning Point Action as his field operation,” said Gina Swoboda, a former chair of the Arizona Republican Party who has openly sparred with the organization.

A spokesperson for Vance did not respond to a request for comment.

Other Republicans, including the Biggs’ campaign, said they’re grateful that Turning Point didn’t wave the white flag in a tough year for the GOP, as voters reel from the impact of Trump’s policies.

“Turning Point Action’s investments both on the ground and over the airwaves in Arizona have been tremendous. We are very grateful they’ve stepped up to support Andy and Republicans up and down the ballot in an extremely difficult environment,” Biggs campaign spokesperson Drew Sexton said in a statement.

In a statement, Tyler Bowyer, chief operating officer of Turning Point Action, said he is “very proud of the strides we have made in Arizona and other swing states.” He emphasized TP Action is “a Get-Out-The-Vote (GOTV) centric organization” and not TV-focused, pointing the blame elsewhere.

“It is the job of national apparatus groups to go on air early and support Republican nominees,” he said. “We of course are disappointed that more national Republican dollars are not matching the national Democrat dollars being spent on Presidential swing states like Arizona.”

While the RGA has stayed out of the race, the Democratic Governors Association has spent at least $10 million.

Hobbs, who was elected to the purple state’s governorship in 2022, launched her first general election TV ad in May, while Biggs was still focused on his late July primary. At the time, Turning Point mocked Hobbs for "spending like a drunken sailor" by launching spending so early.

But now, Republicans say that Hobbs was able to entrench her lead in the race by spending early while Biggs was dark on the airwaves — and blame Turning Point along with other GOP outside groups like the Republican Governors Association for not spending earlier to keep the race competitive. The RGA declined to comment on their decision not to spend in the race.

In a state like Arizona, where an incumbent governor hasn’t lost reelection since 1966 and some 80% of voters cast ballots before Election Day, a late deluge of cash is less likely to sway the race.

“If you're spending that much money, a big portion of that would've been really helpful earlier in the race when Andy was completely broke, he didn't have two nickels to rub together, and Hobbs was dominating the airwaves with no response and beating the crap out of Andy,” said Salmon.”A lot of us are scratching our heads.”

Biggs’ campaign reported just $1.26 million cash on hand in July, while Hobbs’ campaign reported double that. Many donors to former GOP Gov. Doug Ducey have stayed on the sidelines this year.

One Republican operative working on other Arizona races, granted anonymity to speak candidly, said that at this point, “No amount of spending is going to change the fact that Biggs is going to lose.”

“In all likelihood, the top statewide seats are going Democrat. And Turning Point owns it,” the strategist added. “They wanted to be the player. They wanted to be the guy. Guess what? They are.”

Many local Republicans are frustrated that the RGA never spent a penny on the top race in a state Trump won in 2024. While Biggs was getting hammered early on the airwaves, the RGA was spending nearly $4 million on the Wisconsin Democratic primary to boost a democratic socialist who narrowly lost.

For several cycles, Turning Point has muscled statewide candidates through messy Republican primaries, only to see them falter in November. In 2022, it pushed hardliners Blake Masters and Kari Lake as nominees for U.S. Senate and governor, only to see both lose. In 2024, they backed Lake’s bid for Senate; even as Trump won Arizona by five points, Lake lost by two.

Biggs, some Republicans thought, would be different. He had high name ID statewide and a track record of public service for Arizona, both in the state Senate and the U.S. House. Kirk himself personally urged Biggs to enter the governor’s race, Biggs told the Arizona Republic, even though Trump had already endorsed Karrin Taylor Robson, a Phoenix attorney and businesswoman, early in the race.

After Biggs entered the race in January 2025, Trump split his endorsement between Robson and Biggs — and Turning Point took a victory lap.

“Turning Point Action will marshall our organization's full resources, ground game, and support behind the Biggs for Arizona campaign, and we’re honored to be able to do so,” Charlie Kirk said in a May 2025 statement. “He has my full and complete endorsement.”

Robson later dropped out, and Turning Point helped Biggs romp to a massive victory in the primary. Next Tuesday will test if their general-election acumen has improved.

What happens when an AI system becomes exceptionally good at optimizing the wrong thing? It makes the wrong thing happen faster, more efficiently, and at a scale that would have been impossible before. That’s a growing risk in AI-powered performance marketing.

AI can identify audiences, adjust bids, allocate budgets, surface patterns, generate insights, and optimize campaigns in real time. These capabilities can dramatically improve campaign operations. But AI can’t define what the business should ultimately value. That responsibility still belongs to marketers.

As performance marketing becomes increasingly automated, it’s easy to lose sight of the larger business objective. Relying too heavily on platform metrics, optimizing every available variable, overlooking incrementality, and undervaluing the customer journey can undermine the growth these tools are meant to deliver. Avoiding those mistakes requires clear business objectives and human judgment.

Mistake 1: Letting the algorithm choose the objective

Every optimization begins with a goal.

For a paid search campaign, that might be cost per acquisition. For programmatic media, it could be a conversion event, viewability, or return on ad spend. For social, it might be engagement, leads, or purchases.

AI can effectively optimize toward those inputs. But the more fundamental question is: Why does this metric matter to the business?

Consider a retailer trying to drive new customers. An AI-powered campaign could efficiently identify people most likely to convert and continuously shift spend toward them. On the surface, that looks like successful optimization.

But what if the campaign is primarily reaching existing customers or people who were already going to purchase? The campaign could produce strong conversion numbers without generating meaningful incremental growth.

The distinction between a campaign objective and a business objective matters.

A lower CPA can be valuable. A higher ROAS can be valuable. More conversions can be valuable. But none of those metrics automatically equals profitable, incremental business growth.

Define the business outcome first, then determine how technology can help achieve it.

Mistake 2: Confusing platform performance with business performance

One of the most significant challenges in modern performance marketing is the volume of available data.

Every major platform has its own dashboard, attribution model, optimization tools, and definition of success. The result can be a fragmented view of performance: A campaign can report strong results inside an individual platform while delivering a very different outcome for the overall business.

Imagine a consumer brand running paid social, search, connected TV, and programmatic campaigns simultaneously. Each channel may report positive performance based on its own attribution methodology.

But which channel actually created incremental demand? Which audiences were exposed across multiple channels? Which conversions would have happened anyway? And which investments generated customers with long-term value?

Those questions require a measurement framework that exists beyond any individual platform. The platform should inform the measurement framework — not define it.

AI can help connect and analyze increasingly complex datasets, but the business questions still need to come first.

Mistake 3: Optimizing every variable simply because you can

Automation is designed to make marketing more efficient. But efficiency can create its own problem when teams begin optimizing every available variable simply because they can.

AI can make thousands of adjustments across bids, audiences, placements, creative, and budgets. The technology could be doing exactly what it was designed to do. The bigger question is whether all those adjustments are materially improving the business outcome.

Performance marketing teams should resist the temptation to treat every fluctuation in a dashboard as something that requires action. Not every signal is an insight. Not every optimization opportunity is strategically meaningful. And not every metric deserves equal attention.

The marketer’s role is increasingly less about manually managing every campaign lever and more about determining which levers matter in the first place.

Mistake 4: Overlooking incrementality when measuring results

As platforms improve at identifying people likely to convert, you need to get better at determining whether media actually caused the conversion. That’s where incrementality becomes critical.

Suppose an automotive advertiser sees an increase in online leads after launching an AI-optimized campaign, and the platform attributes a significant number of those leads to the campaign. That is useful information — but it does not necessarily answer the most important question: How many of those leads would have happened without the advertising?

Incrementality testing, geographic testing, holdouts, and other measurement approaches can help shift the focus from what the platform claims credit for to understanding what the media actually changed.

The more automated optimization becomes, the more important independent measurement and strategic oversight become.

Mistake 5: Losing sight of the customer journey

Another risk of focusing too heavily on performance metrics is forgetting that there is a person behind every data point.

AI can identify patterns in consumer behavior at an extraordinary scale. But customer behavior is not always predictable, linear, or purely rational. A consumer may see an advertisement multiple times before taking action. They may discover a brand through connected TV, research it through search, encounter social content, and ultimately purchase in a physical store.

If you optimize every channel exclusively around the last measurable action, you can undervalue the earlier interactions that helped create demand.

Performance marketing should not mean reducing the customer journey to the easiest conversion event to measure. The objective should be to understand how different touchpoints contribute to the customer relationship — and ultimately to business growth.

Mistake 6: Relying on AI without applying human judgment

The rise of AI doesn’t eliminate the need for marketers. It changes where they can create the most value.

Machines are exceptionally good at processing information, recognizing patterns, and executing repetitive decisions at scale. Humans bring context — understanding the business strategy, the brand, the customer, the competitive environment, and the circumstances surrounding the data.

A model can tell us that one audience is converting more efficiently. A marketer needs to determine whether that audience represents the future growth opportunity or simply the easiest audience to convert.

A platform can recommend shifting budget toward a particular channel. A marketer needs to understand what that shift could mean for the broader media mix and customer journey.

An algorithm can identify an anomaly. A marketer needs to determine whether it represents an opportunity, a problem, or simply noise.

That human layer is not a limitation of AI. It’s an essential part of using AI effectively.

The next evolution of performance marketing

The promise of AI in performance marketing isn’t that humans won’t have to make decisions. It’s that they’ll have better tools to make them.

The organizations that get the most value from AI will not necessarily be those with the most platforms, the most automation, or the most data. They will be those that establish a clear connection between business objectives, measurement, technology, and human judgment.

We often describe our approach as the intersection of art and science. The science gives us the data, technology, and analytical capabilities to understand what is happening. The art is knowing how to interpret that information and connect it back to the human and business realities behind the numbers.

That balance is increasingly important as AI takes on more of the executional work of marketing. The marketer’s role is shifting from campaign operator to strategic orchestrator: defining the objective, establishing the measurement framework, challenging the outputs, and determining where technology can create the greatest impact.

AI can automate the path. Marketers still need to decide where they are going.
