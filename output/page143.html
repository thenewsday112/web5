The RFEF has spread the 24 Copa del Rey first-round ties postponed after the Urda rail crash across 29 October to 13 November. Valencia, Betis and Celta will now play their ties during the November international break.

A national tragedy has stopped Spanish football. The Royal Spanish Football Federation called off every Copa del Rey first-round tie scheduled for the evening of 28 October. All other matches in competitions it organises were postponed too. The decision was taken out of respect for the victims of the rail crash in Toledo province.

A Renfe high-speed train and an Iryo high-speed train collided near Urda station. The crash killed 68 people and injured 80, Público reported. An investigating judge at the court in Orgaz has ordered the first inquiries into what caused the collision.

The shutdown reached beyond the cup. The federation also postponed the nine first-round matches of the futsal Copa de España. The futsal draw planned for 16:00 on 29 October was cancelled.

Three top-flight clubs will play during the international break

Valencia, Betis, Alavés, Celta and Levante were the top-flight clubs due in cup action that evening. Each of them must now squeeze a rearranged tie into its schedule. That extra game has to fit into an already crowded run of league and European fixtures. The timing differs sharply from club to club.

Alavés go first. Regional side Anaitasuna host them at 19:00 on Tuesday 3 November. Levante travel to CD Tarancón two days later, on Thursday 5 November.

Three clubs face a longer wait. Celta close the round away to Salamanca, with kick-off at 21:00 on Friday 13 November. Valencia and Betis are in action earlier that same week. A tie that was meant to be settled in October will now be played while national teams are away.

Mallorca face cup and league games three days apart

Mallorca’s tie at CD Alcoyano kicks off at 21:00 on the same Thursday evening, after Levante’s game at Tarancón. COPE’s list of the rearranged ties records Mallorca as a Segunda División side, not a top-flight one.

Kalshi and Polymarket US filed federal suits against Missouri and Louisiana on the same day the CFTC sued Utah. The cases push the preemption fight into two states where licensed sportsbooks and casino partners stand to lose business to untaxed competitors.

Two more states now face prediction market operators in federal court. Kalshi’s case targets Missouri, while Polymarket US went after Louisiana. The CFTC filed its own suit against Utah that same day.

Kalshi is challenging a cease-and-desist demand from Missouri. The state told it to pull its sports event contracts or get a state sports betting license. Polymarket US filed as QCX LLC. It named Louisiana’s attorney general and the Louisiana Gaming Control Board as defendants.

Both companies make the same core argument. They say federal commodities law, the Commodity Exchange Act, overrides state gambling enforcement.

Missouri treats event contracts as unlicensed sports wagering

Missouri’s position is direct. The demand to Kalshi came from the office of Attorney General Catherine Hanaway, and Kalshi was not its only target.

The office says that offering sports event contracts in Missouri counts as unlicensed sports wagering. It also holds that federal law does not override state law here. In its view, sports event contracts are not swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act. Companies “cannot repackage sports bets as event contracts” to get around Missouri law, Hanaway said.

The stakes for the licensed market are concrete. Sports wagering became legal in Missouri through Amendment 2. Oversight of the licensed books sits with the Missouri Gaming Commission.

The commission’s July 2026 revenue detail report shows the scale of that business. A prediction market selling sports contracts without a license would pay none of that tax. It would compete for the same customers as the licensed books.

Louisiana’s casino-first licensing faces a federal challenge

Louisiana’s regulated market works differently. Its licenses go first to in-state casinos. Untaxed sports contracts there would compete with those casinos and their sportsbook partners.

The Louisiana Gaming Control Board had already acted on the issue. In December 2025, it sent an advisory on prediction markets and event-based contracts. The advisory went to all sports wagering licensees and platform provider permittees.

That approach targets the licensed side of the market. Polymarket US’s suit now tests whether the state can reach the prediction market operators directly.

The federal regulator has taken a firm public line. The CFTC said Congress made it the only federal regulator responsible for overseeing prediction markets. Its Utah suit seeks to stop the state from using its own laws to shut down CFTC-registered contract markets. It followed civil enforcement actions that Utah brought in state court.

Missouri’s dispute reaches well beyond Kalshi. Its cease-and-desist letters remain in play for Polymarket, Robinhood, Crypto.com, Novig and Underdog. The outcome could decide whether sports contracts are shut out of Missouri and Louisiana.

Morning all.

Let’s start with the news that Arsenal are set to appeal Max Dowman’s red card against Fleetwood on Tuesday evening. I think that’s probably the right thing to do, because had that gone to a VAR review, I think they’d have really struggled to make a compelling case that this was violent conduct.

Was it a small retaliation? Yes. Did it meet the threshold for violent conduct? Not for me. As laid out in the rules of the game, it is defined thus: Violent conduct is when a player uses or attempts to use excessive force or brutality against an opponent when not challenging for the ball, or against a team-mate, team official, match official, spectator or any other person, regardless of whether contact is made.

So, on that basis I think Arsenal are right to appeal. They might cite other incidents to make their case. Perhaps the fact Cole Palmer received just a yellow card in Chelsea’s last game against Sp*rs for what was a much more violent action against Mateus Fernandes. Imagine if we even used one of our own players, demonstrating how Bruno Guimaraes was allowed to forearm smash Jorginho in the side of the head, but didn’t get sent off because he didn’t use his arm like a weapon (or something).

Mikel Arteta is a man who, just last week, ‘joked’ he had a collection of images to show other managers outside their techincal area 36 times in ten minutes. If you don’t think he can’t dig up a few more examples of players getting away with violent conduct, you haven’t been paying attention. Whether it’s going to be sufficient to have the red card overturned is another thing, but at the very least we should see a reduction in what is, as it stands, a three match ban. I think the red was very, very, very harsh, but to miss three games because of that is absurd and unfair, so I think we’re right to appeal.

Once again though, there’s a conversation to be had about the punishments for red cards, and how it’s kind of ridiculous that every act of violent conduct = a three game ban. Some acts are more violent than others, some really do endanger the safety of an opponent, and yet they’re all treated the same. It’s never made sense to me, and I don’t really understand how there isn’t a disciplinary panel that assesses each sending off and applies the ban after the fact based on the seriousness of the incident.

Despite his frustration in the moment, I’m sure the manager’s view of Dowman’s behaviour might well have softened on watching the replays. In the heat of the moment part of it, I’m sure, is that we currently have Christos Tzolis and Bukayo Saka sidelined while Noni Madueke missed out on the midweek game to protect an ankle knock. So, we’re very light in the wide areas right now, and while the root cause of that might lie more at the door of the Sporting Director, he would have wanted Dowman available this weekend for the trip to Anfield.

I still think Arteta’s reaction was poor, but football is a game of opinions. I’ve seen some suggest if Max is gonna play with the big boys, he’s got to be treated as an equal. Perhaps that’s fair enough, but I’ve never seen Arteta react like that to a senior player being sent off, and we’ve had plenty of red cards during his tenure. You could argue that his age is why it’s different, that we all need to learn lessons when we’re younger in ways that differ from when we’re more grown up.

For me though, as talented as he is and as excited as we are about what he might become, it’s too easy to lose sight of the fact he is just 16, and that does make it different. It was so interesting that last week, after Bukayo Saka got injured against Bayern, there were myriad conversations about how Arteta was culpable to some extent because of how much he asked of the player since his breakthrough. And in the same breath, many of the same people were saying ‘It’s time for Dowman!’. I find it a little hard to square that circle. Do you actually care about the welfare of a young player or not?

I think Arteta has generally been rightly protective of Max. He knows that exposure at this age is something he and his staff have to consider for all kinds of reasons, and his playing time this season has been limited. In this instance though, regardless of the fact it’s just a few sharp words on the sideline, he’s made this a bigger issue than it should have been, with Dowman firmly in the spotlight, and I’ll be curious to see how he addresses it in his pre-Liverpool press conference tomorrow.

Bitcoin Price Faces $11B Option Expiry as Calls Outnumber Puts

Around $11 billion in Bitcoin options are scheduled to expire on Friday, October 30. The contracts represent a substantial concentration of open interest, making the settlement an important event for short-term volatility.

Derivatives data reported ahead of expiry showed more call options than puts. One estimate placed the put-to-call ratio near 0.53, indicating that outstanding call contracts significantly outnumbered puts. Calls generally benefit from higher prices, while puts can provide protection against declines.

This positioning offers a potentially bullish talking point, and options open interest reflects existing contracts. Some calls may be part of hedged strategies, but puts can serve purposes beyond outright bearish bets.

The $78,000 area has also emerged as a key reference point in expiry estimates. Often described as the maximum-pain level, it represents the theoretical settlement price at which aggregate option-holder payouts would be minimized.

But Bitcoin can settle well above or below the $78K price level, depending on market demand, liquidity, and trading activity. For us, the more constructive scenario would be Bitcoin holding support as contracts approach settlement. If dealers adjust their hedges and expiring positions disappear without triggering substantial selling, the market could find room for a directional recovery.

FOMC Uncertainty Clears, but December Remains a Wildcard

The FOMC decision is behind the market, but its implications extend beyond October. The Fed kept rates unchanged while describing economic activity as strong and inflation as elevated.

The two dissents favoring a rate hike also complicate the bullish interpretation. They signal that policymakers remain concerned about inflation, even as the committee maintains its current target range.

The battle for the future of a House Democratic majority is raging

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The battle for the future of a House Democratic majority is already underway.

In phone calls, group chats and strategy sessions, competing wings of the party are jockeying for position, seeking to build unity, influence and governing agendas in advance of what they hope will be an Election Day romp.

The Congressional Progressive Caucus gathered incumbent and incoming members to Washington last month to prepare an agenda and discuss how they will use their leverage to advance it. Moderate Democrats are also scheming in behind-the-scenes conversations about how they can act as a counterbalance to the left with their own affirmative vision for the majority.

The early plotting sets up an inevitable clash between the factions — both of which are already arguing any midterm gains would be proof that their wing of the party should be standard-bearers ahead of the 2028 presidential election.

“There's going to be some real tension, because we have to move,” progressive Rep. Delia Ramirez of Illinois said in an interview. “Preserving perfect relationships with colleagues who don't feel the urgency the way that some of us feel it because we live it every day isn't my top priority. Protecting people is.”

Both sides are courting potential incoming members with offers of mentorship and campaign resources as they attempt to shore up their ranks.

Ramirez is mentoring an incoming batch of ultraprogressives, including Darializa Avila Chevalier and Claire Valdez of New York and Melat Kiros of Colorado, whom she recently hosted in Chicago to talk about shared priorities. All campaigned on ending U.S. aid to Israel, abolishing Immigration and Customs Enforcement and passing “Medicare for All” universal health coverage, in addition to promoting economic populist policies like a minimum wage increase.

These presumptive members, many of whom identify as democratic socialists, have garnered outsize attention — some are calling them “The Bloc” — but the organizing effort has extended to a larger group of incoming progressives.

That outreach has included a September retreat run by the Congressional Progressive Caucus chair, Rep. Greg Casar of Texas, as well as other senior progressives such as Rep. Pramila Jayapal of Washington, a former CPC chair. The group strategized about how to maximize their influence to advance their wishlist and to settle on what Ramirez described as “non-negotiables.”

In a session on Medicare for All, for example, the progressives discussed pushing for a committee vote and Congressional Budget Office fiscal estimate on the policy as ways to build momentum around the issue ahead of a possible post-2028 Democratic trifecta.

Jayapal said the conversations centered on developing an “inside-outside strategy” that identifies points of leverage in the House — such as procedural votes and the drafting of a caucus rules package — to reflect “the voice of many of our new members who are coming in on fire.”

Moderates, meanwhile, are already wary of seeing routine House business weaponized for factional warfare, potentially threatening the reelection prospects of swing-district members. Members of overlapping centrist groups, including the New Democrat Coalition, the Blue Dog Caucus and Problem Solvers Caucus have discussed how to serve as a counterweight to the left.

Rep. Josh Gottheimer of New Jersey, a veteran leader of the centrist wing, said he was eyeing “concrete steps to stop socialists trying to jam the extremist parts of their agenda.”

“Just like with the progressives, there have been conversations among moderates about our policy priorities, with a clear proactive agenda,” Gottheimer said. “As expected, there will be policy red lines that are too extreme and out of step with our democratic values. Many of us are already talking to each about the importance of countering those extremist positions and focusing on issues more representative of the majority of Americans.”

“The good news: there are plenty of issues, frankly, that most Democrats agree on,” he added. “But there are places where you’ll see tension.”

Precisely how moderates plan to neutralize the party’s left flank remains a topic of live discussion among members and aides, who say a specific plan will depend on the outcome of the midterms. They are banking on a bumper crop of new pragmatic members from traditionally Republican territory to simply outnumber the “Bloc.”

“There is a lot of angst among some moderates about this DSA faction and squad expansion,” a senior moderate Democratic operative said in an interview. “The size of the majority will matter a lot.”

Each side is getting support from outside groups who are helping to develop policy plans and marshal grassroots support for their respective positions.

On the left, for instance, the Justice Democrats have convened meet-ups and are working to establish lines of communication between members. Centrist groups like Searchlight Institute and Third Way, meanwhile, are hosting their own meetings and cautioning Democrats against taking up certain hard-left positions.

“We see our role as, frankly, being kind of the hall monitor of the situation,” said Searchlight spokesperson Tré Easton. “Let's make sure that as Democrats are given the opportunity to govern, they remember why voters sent them to be there in the first place.”

Because fewer progressives are running in competitive races, the left has had more breathing room to begin organizing incoming members. But the centrist organs are hoping to quickly catch up and have already sketched out governing agendas that hew closely to the campaign messages coming from top party leaders.

The New Dems — the largest ideological bloc of House Democrats, with more than 110 members — published a 65-page dossier in May pushing broadly acceptable bills that target lowering costs.

Rep. Nikki Budzinski of Illinois, who is running unopposed to chair the group in the next Congress, said in an interview that the group’s size “will make us a force in determining the Democratic caucus's agenda” — with an eye on delivering on the campaign promises that incoming members made.

“We want to make sure that we're running and accomplishing the policy that they don't have to run from themselves back home,” she said. “That these are things that they can run toward.”

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries has already previewed an early slate of bills that align with the New Dems’ vision — rolling back Republican-led Medicaid cuts, restoring expired Affordable Care Act subsidies and ending President Donald Trump’s tariffs.

Jeffries has also taken steps to get his left and right flanks used to talking to each other and seeking compromise. This summer, he established five working groups, each led by a progressive and a moderate, to develop proposals to lower health care, housing, energy, child care and grocery costs.

He told reporters earlier this month that Democrats had made an “ironclad” promise to “focus on the affordability crisis and to drive down the high cost of living.”

“We intend to keep it, and it's what unites Democrats across the ideological spectrum,” he said.

Both moderates and progressives boast that they have Jeffries’ ear and have lobbied him in recent months to take up their priorities. But should Democrats fulfill internal expectations and win the majority, the speaker election looms as an initial flashpoint in the factional clash.

Although no alternative candidate has emerged, some incoming progressives have withheld commitments to supporting Jeffries as speaker, with many saying they want further “conversations” about the governing agenda — if not outright concessions.

For the centrists, support for Jeffries could be a litmus test: Rep. Greg Stanton of Arizona, who leads the New Democrats’ campaign arm, said in an interview that every single candidate the group endorsed supports Jeffries for speaker.

Kiros, for example, did not say in an interview if she would back Jeffries for speaker but she described a “wonderful” conversation with him and hopes to have future talks about her top priorities: Medicare for All, a data center moratorium and Israel arms embargo.

She and other progressives have offered an olive branch to moderates, acknowledging there will be ways to work across the caucus — and even across the aisle — to pass law. Members of both wings have repeatedly raised passing a minimum wage hike.

“A big part of what the Progressive Caucus has been committed to doing has been to reforming the image of the Democratic Party back into a party that puts working class people first,” Casar said. “It means doing important outreach work to other parts of the Democratic Caucus.”

But leaders on the left said that working with moderates should not be confused with ditching the values they campaigned on.

Voters, Ramirez said, “are tired of nice little words and statements and letters, and they want to see us fight like hell for them.”

Mia McCarthy contributed to this report.

Top House Democrats demand Trump preserve conversations with Grok

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The top Democrats on the House Judiciary and Oversight Committees want President Donald Trump to preserve his conversations with artificial intelligence chatbots as part of an inquiry into whether Elon Musk's AI assistant played a role in shaping White House policy — a preview of what’s to come if their party flips the chamber next week.

In a letter to Trump obtained by POLITICO, Reps. Jamie Raskin of Maryland and Robert Garcia of California are demanding that the president preserve “any prompts” submitted to AI chatbots, including Musk's Grok, OpenAI's ChatGPT and Anthropic's Claude while “carrying out your constitutional, statutory, or other official or ceremonial duties,” along with the AI responses.

Raskin and Garcia — who are expected to wield the Judiciary and Oversight gavels if Democrats win back the House, respectively — are also asking for a briefing on the matter by 5:00 p.m. on Nov. 11.

Their request was informed by reporting that said Trump sought Grok’s “advice on carrying out some of your presidential duties, including asking Grok to opine on how Venezuelans would respond to the capture of President Nicolás Maduro.” The lawmakers note that, just “weeks later,” Trump “ordered the U.S. military, without congressional authorization, to attack Venezuela and capture President Maduro.”

Garcia and Raskin called the example “deeply troubling” because Trump “consulted Grok — but not Congress — on your military deployment to Venezuela, circumventing Congress’s constitutional power to declare war and introducing instead into American foreign policy decision making a technology that is highly manipulable and demonstrably error-prone.”

They also said the use of AI chatbots by the White House “exposes the United States to acute national security risks” and that usage “exposes the country to malign influence from unknown and unaccountable third parties.”

A White House official said in a statement that "the White House preserves all required records in accordance with the law."

Musk did not immediately respond to a request for comment.
