Studies Show Diet Sodas May Not Be Stalling Weight Loss as Much as Previously Thought

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Artificially sweetened drinks are often demonized based on the theory that they may encourage a greater appetite, or supercharge our sugar cravings, but recent work appears to dispel the bad rap around diet sodas and gaining weight. Now, a two-year clinical trial from the University of Liverpool, England, has provided the greatest insight yet, into the long-term effects of diet drinks on weight loss.

The groundbreaking study, conducted over the course of 104 weeks and published in the British Journal of Nutrition, attempted to build on a similar report in 2023 that also found no proof for the theory that diet sodas can stall weight loss.

Do Diet Drinks Cause Weight Gain or Cravings? What the 2-Year SWITCH Trial Proves

While both studies pitted water against zero and low-calorie drinks that were free of sugar (also known as non-nutritive, or NNS beverages) the continued efforts to find the true value of diet drinks is critical, because sugar sweetened beverages often account for a substantial source of dietary sugar, excess calories, and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes. So, if science can show that NNS drinks do not lead to increased sugar intake or excess appetite, they could instead play a part in a balanced diet. To that end, the Liverpool University study has been described as the longest, most comprehensive trial in this area to date. “This study provides the long‑term clinical evidence that has been missing from international discussions,” explained Professor John Halford, who was lead author on the project.

2-Year Clinical Trial Comparison: Diet Drinks vs. Water

493 overweight or obese adults were entered into a structural weight management program and then split into those who only drank water, and those who consumed a choice of 20 commercially available diet drinks containing aspartame, sucralose, and acesulfame.  Each drink contained less than 2.1 calories per 100ml, with no overall daily calorie cap on drinks. The NNS group were allowed to drink water, but they were asked to drink at least two 330ml servings per day of a diet beverage.

Interestingly, neither group was not blocked from drinking sugary beverages, but they did received coaching to reduce them.

What were the results after 104 weeks?

  •   Weight loss difference between NNS and water groups deemed insignificant
  •   Both groups maintained sustained weight reduction for the full 2 years
  •   Both groups saw reductions in waist size along with improved body composition

The study also found that overall sugar intake decreased slightly greater in those drinking the NNS beverages, seemingly disproving the theory that artificially sweetened drinks encourage sugar cravings, and suggesting the opposite effect may occur.

“These latest findings show that concerns about sweeteners disrupting appetite or causing weight gain are not supported when tested in a rigorous, long-term randomized trial,” said Professor Harrold. “We hope this evidence informs future WHO guidance and public health policy.”

The Invisible Graph: How Companies Grow Revenue And Still Lose Value

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I got off the phone with a potential client whose company is doing great. The client, however, is not even thinking of selling the company as they see future growth over the next couple of years that they wish to materialize in order to get a larger exit.

The conversation prompted me to consider something I believe many entrepreneurs miss, and it’s this: Revenue can rise while enterprise value falls.

When revenue and value move in opposite directions

Founders and boards routinely track revenue and profitability over time. But another graph deserves equal attention: enterprise value. Its trajectory can turn downward while the operating numbers continue improving, with significant implications for M&A timing.

Consider a hypothetical SaaS company generating $15 million in annual recurring revenue, valued at 6x ARR. Its implied enterprise value is $90 million. Two years later, ARR reaches $20 million, but the multiple falls to 3.5x revenue. Enterprise value drops to $70 million. Revenue increased 33%, while value declined 22%.

These are only illustrative assumptions to demonstrate how multiple compression can overshadow operating growth. For a founder postponing a sale until the next revenue milestone, the practical implication is clear: Reaching that milestone may still produce a smaller exit.

Qualitative, absolute and relative factors

The revenue multiplier reflects several dimensions that evolve differently. Qualitative factors include management quality, founder dependence, competitive intensity, product differentiation and strategic relevance to buyers.

Absolute financial measures include revenue, EBITDA and cash flow.

Relative performance measures include growth, margins, net revenue retention and gross revenue retention. Although retention metrics are expressed as percentages, their importance lies in what they reveal about customer durability and expansion. Buyers assess these measures together and compare them with relevant peers. As companies mature, their absolute results can improve while their growth rates weaken.

Meanwhile, stronger profitability, better retention or deeper management capabilities may support a higher multiplier. The valuation outcome depends on the combined effect. Boards should therefore ask which characteristics will improve over the next year, which could deteriorate and how buyers might price those changes.

M&A preparation should begin while you are winning

A sale requires preparation, buyer engagement, negotiation and diligence, during which buyers continually reassess performance and forecasts.

Waiting until a slowdown becomes visible can weaken negotiating leverage and raise questions about management’s projections. Strategic buyer demand also follows its own timetable. A buyer may place substantial value on acquiring a particular technology, customer base or geographic presence today. That interest can diminish after an internal product launch, a competing acquisition or a budget change.

Waiting can still create considerable value when additional scale attracts more buyers, profitability improves or concentration falls. Boards can make that case tangible by reviewing estimated enterprise value alongside the operating plan, using scenarios in which revenue grows while the multiple holds, expands or contracts. They should also consider how debt, cash, dilution, liquidation preferences and transaction terms affect shareholder proceeds. Nobody can reliably identify the valuation peak in advance.

However, every decision to delay a sale embeds a view about future valuation. That view deserves its own graph.

From Monk To Startups: One Founder’s Unusual Path To Building Government Software

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: The following is the latest profile in a series of articles about startup founders from non-technical backgrounds who have launched successful venture-backed companies. Read the previous interviews in the series including with MagicSchool AI founder Adeel Khan here, Trunk Tools founder Sarah Buchner here, Tabs founder Ali Hussain here, Craft founder Ilya Levtov here, GC AI founder Cecilia Ziniti here, Incredible Health co-founder Iman Abuzeid here, and Hilt founder William “Will” Cielen here.

Lilly Chen was 16 and had been repeatedly hospitalized for Graves’ disease when her mother suggested they move to China and become monks. Frustrated with the care her daughter was receiving through Medicaid, she wanted to try something different.

Lilly Chen, co-founder and CEO of FSH Technologies. (Courtesy photo)

Chen left high school and spent about two years as a monk, ordained in the United States before spending several months at a monastery in China. She meditated, reread the same books, and gradually felt well enough to consider returning to school.

She went on to enter Colorado College without a high school diploma, briefly play video games professionally, and become an engineer at Facebook. When she quit that job, she didn’t have a startup idea. She just wanted to find work she believed in.

That mission eventually led her to FSH (pronounced “fish) Technologies,” a Philadelphia-based company that’s building software for some of the government’s least glamorous tasks, including administering school food services and managing public records.

After her brief stint at a large tech company, Chen realized she wanted to tackle the everyday problems that make people’s lives harder, even if solving them wouldn’t generate much excitement in Silicon Valley.

“What does it actually take to solve the really unsexy, boring stuff?” she remembers asking herself.

A different route to college

Before Chen was thinking about government software, she was trying to get well enough to return to school.

At the monastery in China, she meditated, ate food grown on-site or brought by nearby villagers, and lived without internet access. She continued taking thyroid medication and said her health became more manageable during that period.

She also read. A lot. With no nearby place to buy books, she reread the ones she had brought, finding different places around the monastery to sit with them.

Her attachment to those books prompted a conversation with a fellow monk who asked what she enjoyed about reading and where she might find more books. The discussion eventually turned to colleges, and why she wasn’t attending one.

Chen explained her health problems. He observed that she seemed well. She realized she was.

So Chen returned to the U.S., homeschooled herself and enrolled at Colorado College without having earned a high school diploma. She turned 20 during her freshman year and graduated in 2019 with a double major in mathematics and economics.

While she was in college, Chen briefly played Dota 2 professionally. She also played against OpenAI’s game-playing bots, an early encounter with AI technology she would later help support as an engineer.

After an initial job in Boston, she joined Facebook, where she built infrastructure used to train AI models.

Leaving without a startup idea

Then on Jan. 6, 2021, Chen was attending a remote company all-hands meeting while watching coverage of the attack on the U.S. Capitol on another screen. Her concerns about Facebook’s role in enabling the organization of violence made her question whether she could continue working there.

“I realized that I couldn’t work for Big Tech anymore,” she recalls.

She quit shortly afterward.

To figure out her next move, she returned to the sort of questions she had encountered as a monk. What made her happy? What mattered to her? What was she good at?

Programming remained her most concrete skill. She just needed to figure out where to apply it.

An introduction through Envision, a student-run accelerator focused on nontraditional founders, connected her with Contrary, a venture firm that has backed the likes of Ramp and Anduril Industries. The firm wrote Chen her first check in 2021, before she had even settled on the business that would become FSH.

Although she didn’t have a developed company pitch, Chen’s earnest explanation of what motivated her and her passion for solving a meaningful problem won investors over. That investment gave her room to experiment, she said.

FSH took shape into what it is today in 2024. The Contrary investor who first backed her, Jason Chen, has since joined the company as its chief operating officer.

The company has raised $25 million in total funding, including a recent $20 million Series A that Chen said came together in about a week. Participants in that raise included Lachy Groom, General Catalyst, Ramp, Scribble Ventures, Operator Partners, Basis Set Ventures. Contrary also led its seed financing.

Finding the problem beneath the paperwork

Ultimately, Chen landed on government software because in her view, it had the potential to solve many consequential problems that attracted relatively little excitement.

For example, permits, licenses, grant applications and other administrative processes can be slow for reasons that extend beyond an outdated interface, Chen noted. Information may exist in separate departments. Updating a website may require approvals from multiple stakeholders including policymakers, communications staff and an IT team.

Putting a chatbot on top of that system, Chen argues, leaves much of the underlying problem unresolved.

Her approach to solving some of the problems that exist within governments draws on an exchange she had years earlier at the monastery, where one of her chores was feeding koi.

The head monk would periodically ask her if she thought she was the fish or the pond?

Initially, Chen didn’t understand what he was asking. But he kept returning to the question, and she began thinking about it. Eventually, she picked a fish she thought resembled her: one that stayed out of fights, swam with the group and occasionally ventured off on its own.

The head monk’s response was, “You’re the pond, not the fish.”

Chen viewed the exchange as a lesson in looking beyond individual pieces to understand how a whole system works. Looking at government software, she believed that it meant examining why information is scattered or processes are slow before deciding what technology to build.

One early area for FSH has been K-12 food services, where administrators must perform tasks such as coordinating meal planning, nutritional information and reimbursement requirements. Another is records and archive management, including the paper documents that can still require residents to visit a government office to retrieve them.

FSH does use AI for tasks such as optical character recognition, translation and natural-language search. But, Chen said, the company treats it as a tool within its software, rather than relying on a chatbot or an AI agent to handle an entire process.

Government sales have required patience. The company generated less than $100,000 in revenue in its first year, Chen said, reflecting the time it takes to win contracts. At the time of this interview, FSH had roughly eight or nine employees, with additional offers outstanding.

Thinking slowly, moving quickly

Chen’s time as a monk has also influenced how she runs the company.

She admits she is skeptical of startup culture that treats sleeping in the office or working extremely long hours as evidence of commitment or a badge of pride. Rather, Chen views some of that behavior as performative, especially when it comes at the expense of the rest that she believes people need to work effectively.

Her preferred motto to the team is, “Slow is smooth and smooth is fast.”

“We think slowly about a lot of things, and then we do things quickly,” she said.

Chen believes it’s crucial to first understand the people, departments and procedures behind a government service before trying to improve it.

Looking back, Chen also credits her success to finding people willing to back someone whose experience didn’t resemble the usual résumé. That includes the college that admitted her without a high school diploma and an investor who wrote a check before she had settled on a company idea.

“You definitely end up finding your own way if you just stick to being weird,” she said.

The Maxus II represents the next generation of Browning gas guns, building on the success of the Gold and the original Maxus. The result is a very reliable, soft-kicking gun with several good ergonomic features. The addition of a soft rubber insert in the gun’s comb does a remarkable job of reducing the gun’s already mild recoil. The redesigned stock features a parallel section at the butt so that the stock can be shortened without the need to regrind or replace the original recoil pad. The stock comes with a shim kit to fine-tune fit, and the safety can be switched from right- to left-handed in a couple of minutes.

The gun also has an enlarged bolt handle and bolt release button found on many new semi-autos. Combined with Browning’s unique Speed Load, which grabs a shell thumbed halfway into the magazine and whisks it into the chamber, and a magazine plug that is easily removed and replaced with no tool other than a key, the Maxus II is one of the most user-friendly shotguns around. And it shoots flat to the point of aim and has an overbored barrel and long choke tubes to improve patterning. It’s a gun you can take from the goose pit to the dove field to the sporting clays course with total confidence.

What to Look for When Choosing a Semi-Automatic Shotgun

The author’s Benelli Montefeltro next to four drake mallards after a successful hunt. (Photo/Phil Bourjaily)

A semi-auto shotgun is absolutely the most versatile of all shotguns, but you should still choose your new gun with its main use in mind. Aside from the choices you make based on weight, barrel length, and so on, when you buy any shotgun, there are a couple of additional factors to consider when choosing asemi-auto:

  • Gas guns have noticeably less felt recoil
  • Inertia guns work well in bad weather
  • Inertia guns stay cleaner longer than gas guns
  • Typically, gas guns are more complicated to take apart and clean

Some 3 ½-inch 12-gauge semi-autos won’t cycle very light loads well. Many 3-inch guns will cycle light loads reliably. If you want to shoot targets and hunt with a gun, a 3-inch semi-auto may be a better choice.

FAQs

Q: Is a semi-auto shotgun worth it?

There is no more versatile shotgun made than a 12- or 20-gauge semi-automatic. It can shoot any load and acquit itself well in any type of shooting situation. They are light enough to carry, long and steady enough to shoot at targets, and gas guns especially reduce felt recoil. If you’re only going to own one shotgun and want to use it for a variety of purposes, it should be a semi-automatic. Even a top-of-the-line gun is worth it if you can use it for everything.

Q: How reliable are semi-auto shotguns?

Semi-auto reliability has improved a great deal. Most good semi-automatic shotguns can run for many hundreds of rounds without cleaning and without malfunction. Inertia guns are especially known for working well in wet, nasty conditions of waterfowl hunting. Semi-autos are also much less picky about ammunition than they used to be. You have to do your part to maintain the gun, and if you do, it will serve you very reliably.

Q: Do semi-auto shotguns jam?

Semi-autos used to have the nickname “jam-a-matics” in the 60s and 70s. The development of the inertia action, first sold by Italian makers Benelli and Breda in the 1960s, and improvements in gas-gun design have made semi-automatic shotguns extremely reliable. They can still jam, especially if they aren’t maintained by their owners. But many hunters and target shooters go through entire seasons without a problem with their semi-auto.

Q: What are the advantages of a semi-automatic shotgun?

A semi-auto offers more firepower than a break-action double without the need to manually cycle the action, as you do with a pump gun. Semi-autos can be made very light, with the use of alloy and plastic parts that don’t compromise performance. Gas-operated semi-autos have the huge advantage of reducing felt recoil. The moving parts of the gun essentially store, then release, recoil energy over a longer period, changing the feel of recoil from a kick to a shove.

Final Thoughts

Semi-automatic shotguns have achieved a level of versatility and reliability unknown to previous generations of shooters. There’s a gun for any purpose, plus plenty of all-purpose guns, as the list we’ve highlighted here shows. The Browning Maxus II, our best overall gun, is a good example of what a semi-auto can be: light, soft-recoiling, versatile, and adjustable to fit a range of shooters. On the target gun side, the Beretta A400 Xcel can go toe-to-toe with $10,000 O/Us in sporting clays competition, while a field gun like the Benelli SBE3 will shoot in any weather. There has never been a better time to buy a semi-automatic shotgun.

From garage-hammered pitons to swami-belt harnesses, climbers have been making their own equipment since the sport was in its infancy. Gear has come a long way since then. It’s also gotten a heck of a lot more expensive. While we can’t in good faith recommend you save money by making your own chocks and cams, there are still a number of things you can DIY for cheap.

Earlier this month, I went on a DIY kick and decided to hand-make a few pieces myself. Some were items I’d seen friends carry around at the crag. Other ideas came from online forums (and this magazine). With a single trip to the hardware store and a heap of old gear that’s been rotting in the trunk of my car, I was able to make five things—and saved $183 total. Here’s what I came up with, how much each item cost, and what I would do differently next time.

1. Ice tool leash

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $39.95 for an Edelrid Spinner Leash on Backcountry

DIY cost: $5
Savings: $35

Materials:

  • Double-length 1-inch tubular webbing
  • Tent pole shock cord (from an old tent, or $5 on Amazon)
  • 2 carabiners
  • Wire coat hanger
  • Lighter

Dropped tools are no joke. At Colorado’s ice parks, losing one will typically earn you a swim in a very frigid creek. On multi-pitch ice, you could end up stranded. The trick is to clip your tools to your harness via a bungee system that’s elastic enough to preserve freedom of motion, but not so springy as to send your dropped tool boomeranging pick-first into your thigh

Purpose-made leashes are pricey. A DIY version is free if you have old tent poles lying around your house and only $5 if you need to purchase new shock-cord. I started by clipping the bar tack off an old piece of double-length tubular webbing. I then tied one end of the shock cord to a piece of wire, and shoved it through the tube (this took forever). Once I could access the shock cord from either end, I tied an overhand knot in the center to create a girth-hitchable loop for my harness. I then scrunched the webbing down as much as possible over the two legs of the shock cord, tied overhangs for both carabiners, and melted the edges to finish them.

(Note: For another $10, you can purchase a swivel from most hardware stores, putting the result on par with $80 swiveling leashes.)

2. Nut tool tether

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $17 for a Personal Tool Tether on Gear Keeper

DIY cost: $5

Savings: $12

Materials:

  • Keychain spring coil or telephone cord ($3 at Goodwill)
  • Wire crimps ($2 at the hardware store)
  • Crimping tool or needle-nose pliers
  • Scissors

Some nut tools come with integrated leashes. These don’t add a ton of cost to the tool, but they are boring as all get out. Aftermarket versions from Gear Keeper, Amazon, and beyond aren’t much better. The answer: Make your own out of an old spring-coil keychain bracelet.

First, snip the bracelet in half and feed an electrical crimp onto either end. Next, fold one end of the coil around the inside edge of a nut tool, tuck the end into the nearest crimp, and squeeze the crimp shut with pliers or a crimper tool. Then do the same thing around a small accessory carabiner to complete the tether.

3. Stick clip

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $94.95 for a Metolius Basic Stick Clip at REI

DIY cost: $25

Savings: $70

Materials:

  • Painter’s pole ($20 at the hardware store)
  • Small spring clamp ($1 at the hardware store)
  • Two stainless steel hose clamps ($4 at the hardware store)

The cost of stick clips has always baffled me, especially since you can make one yourself with a tree branch, a small stick, and a bit of tape. However, I’ll concede that not everyone loves tramping around the crag looking for an appropriately sturdy stick. For those folks, try this trick.

You can buy a painter’s pole from just about any hardware store for relatively cheap. The ones at my local were $20 for 8 feet or $40 for 23. Once you’ve got that, use a couple of stainless steel hose clamps (basically big metal zip ties) to attach a small spring-loaded clamp. Voila!

4. Stiffy draw

(Photo: Corey Buhay)

Usual cost: $54.95 for a Panic Quickdraw on Kong

DIY cost: $15

Savings: $40

Materials:

  • Mad Rock Trigger Wire Carabiner ($11.95 at Rock and Resole)
  • 12 inches heavy-duty insulated housing wire ($3 at the hardware store)
  • Single-length 1-inch tubular webbing
  • Bent-gate carabiner
  • Duct tape
  • Scissors

The internet said I could make one of these by stuffing a piece of webbing into a section of transparent vinyl tubing and tying carabiners to the ends. I tried this and found the result upsettingly floppy. Vinyl tubing is also much more expensive than heavy-duty housing wire, which you can purchase by the foot at Home Depot. Tubing has the added benefit of being foldable.

So, after my first attempt failed, I went back to an old Matt Samet recipe and shoved a section of wire into the hollow core of a length of tubular webbing. I then tied the ends with a water knot to make a continuous loop, and duct-taped a Mad Rock Trigger Wire Carabiner and a bent-gate carabiner to either end. It’s not quite as long as other purpose-made stiffy draws, but it does the trick at a fraction of the price.

So, you want to be a school administrator?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As an educational leadership professor, I have coached many students aspiring to transition from classroom teaching to assistant principal and principal positions. Through this work, I have reviewed countless résumés, and one thing continues to surprise me: how many look almost identical to those used to apply for teaching positions, with little more than an administrative certification or graduate degree added.

Earning an administrative license establishes eligibility for school leadership positions, but it does not automatically make someone a competitive candidate. A résumé should reflect the transition from classroom teacher to aspiring school leader by demonstrating the ability to lead others, influence school practices, and make a meaningful impact.

The “So What?” Test

I recently came across a LinkedIn post by executive coach Melissa Schmidiger that made an important point about résumés: every bullet should pass the “so what?” test. Rather than simply describing responsibilities, applicants should demonstrate what they accomplished and what changed because of their work. This advice is particularly relevant to aspiring school leaders.

I frequently encounter statements such as:

  • “Responsible for facilitating professional development.”
  • “Participated in instructional leadership team meetings.”
  • “Served as grade-level chairperson.”
  • “Collaborated with administrators on schoolwide initiatives.”

There is nothing inherently wrong with these statements, but they tell me very little about the candidate’s actual leadership contributions. You facilitated professional development. So what? What did teachers learn? Did instructional practices change? How do you know?

These are precisely the types of questions aspiring administrators should expect when interviewing for leadership positions. A résumé that lists responsibilities tells me where you have been. A résumé that demonstrates impact helps me understand what you accomplished while you were there.

Sometimes It’s the Résumé. Sometimes It’s the Experience.

Through coaching aspiring school leaders, I have noticed two groups of candidates. The first has actually done quite a bit of leadership work. They have mentored colleagues, facilitated professional learning, helped develop schoolwide initiatives, led collaborative teams, and addressed meaningful problems of practice. Yet their résumés fail to capture the depth of these experiences. They describe their work in terms of responsibilities rather than contributions and results.

For these candidates, the solution is relatively straightforward. Instead of writing “Responsible for mentoring new teachers,” a candidate might explain how they developed a mentoring structure, supported colleagues with instructional planning, and used classroom observations or feedback to assess improvements. The experience is already there; the résumé simply needs to communicate it more effectively.

The second group presents a more complicated challenge. These individuals have earned their administrative credentials and completed the required internship, but they have limited experience leading beyond their own classrooms. Their résumés may be filled with descriptions of successful teaching, committee memberships, and graduate coursework. What is often missing is evidence that they have taken responsibility for leading adults, improving systems, or addressing challenges beyond their individual classrooms.

This is not necessarily a résumé-writing problem. It is an experience problem, and no amount of creative wording will compensate for leadership experiences that have not yet occurred.

Leadership Experience Doesn’t Begin With a Title

Being an excellent teacher is a tremendous asset when pursuing school leadership. Successful teachers understand instruction, students, families, and the daily realities of classrooms. However, the work of an assistant principal or principal requires additional competencies, including supporting adult learning, navigating difficult conversations, analyzing competing priorities, allocating resources, building trust, and making decisions that affect an entire school community.

A teacher may have tremendous success with students but still need opportunities to develop these broader leadership capacities. Fortunately, aspiring administrators do not need to wait for an official title to begin developing them.

Leadership opportunities can take many forms: facilitating professional learning communities, coordinating interventions, mentoring colleagues, leading curriculum development, examining attendance trends, or helping a school address an instructional challenge. What matters is not simply joining another committee or adding another responsibility. Candidates should be able to explain the problem they addressed, their specific contribution, and the results of their efforts.

Leadership experience is not necessarily about how many activities appear on a résumé. It is about the substance of those experiences and what they reveal about a candidate’s ability to work with and through others.

Your Internship Should Help Tell That Story

Educational leadership preparation programs also have a responsibility to help candidates develop these experiences. An administrative internship should be more than a collection of completed hours or a checklist of activities. While observing administrative work and participating in school operations are valuable, aspiring leaders also need opportunities to exercise judgment, solve problems, work with colleagues, and reflect on the consequences of their decisions.

If your internship involved attending meetings, observing administrators, and assisting with school operations, those may have been worthwhile experiences. But what did you learn about leadership? What opportunities did you have to influence decisions or contribute to improvements?

Consider an intern who identifies a pattern of chronic absenteeism, works with school staff to examine underlying causes, helps develop an intervention, and monitors its implementation. That experience tells a much richer leadership story than simply writing “Assisted with attendance initiatives.”

Even when an initiative does not produce the intended results, candidates should be able to explain the leadership process, the evidence considered, the challenges encountered, and what they learned. Impact is not always a percentage increase or a dramatic turnaround. Sometimes it is a stronger process, improved collaboration, a better-informed decision, or a meaningful change in practice.

Before You Submit That Application

I encourage aspiring administrators to review their résumés through a different lens. Ask yourself:

  1. Does my résumé describe primarily what I did as a teacher, or does it demonstrate my development as a leader?
  2. Can I identify specific examples of how I influenced the work of other adults?
  3. Have I helped address a problem that extended beyond my own classroom?
  4. What changed because of my contributions, and what evidence supports that conclusion?
  5. If I cannot answer these questions, what leadership experiences do I need to pursue next?

These questions should not be reserved for the weeks leading up to an interview. They should inform how aspiring administrators approach their graduate coursework, internships, professional responsibilities, and opportunities for growth. Waiting until application season to think about leadership experience may mean discovering too late that there are significant gaps in your preparation.

A License Is a Starting Point, Not the Finish Line

My intention is not to discourage teachers from pursuing school leadership. Schools need thoughtful, capable educators who are willing to take on the challenges of leading others. But preparation for that transition should be intentional, and candidates need to understand that demonstrating effectiveness in the classroom is not the same as demonstrating readiness to lead a school.

For some aspiring administrators, the next step is to revise their résumés so that their leadership accomplishments are more visible. For others, the next step is to seek out the experiences that will eventually make those accomplishments possible.

Your administrative license tells me you have met the requirements to apply. Your résumé should tell me what you have done to prepare yourself to lead.

And if you cannot yet tell that story, perhaps the next step is not another revision of your résumé, but another opportunity to lead.
