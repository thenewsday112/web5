The great myth is that truth is an emergent property of fact, that it bubbles up from the bottom of reality once the mind attains enough fathoms of factuality. But objective reality — all those things like gravity and light and the fossil of the Archaeopteryx that exist whether or not we believe in them — is pocked with myriad subjective realities, each lensed through the particular qualia of the perceiver, each a function not of the mind alone but of the entire organism and the whole of its lived experience, embodied and enacted by the total creature. What we call truth, and how we arrive at it, has more to do with that tessellated totality than with the mind’s rational analysis of reality.

Virginia Woolf (January 25, 1882–March 28, 1941) explores this with her characteristic rigor of thought and passion for language in a wonderful essay about the Ancient Greeks later included in The Common Reader (public library) — the classic collection that also gave us Woolf on how to hear your soul.

Virginia Woolf

With an eye to “the indomitable honesty, the courage, the love of truth” that made Socrates such a timeless fulcrum of wisdom (which, I suppose, is the ultimate use of the truth), and in fiery defiance of Descartes, she insists that we arrive at the truth — about the world, about ourselves, about the substance life is made of — with more than the mind:

What matters is not so much the end we reach as our manner of reaching it… Truth is various; truth comes to us in different disguises; it is not with the intellect alone that we perceive it… Truth is to be pursued with all our faculties. Are we to rule out the amusements, the tendernesses, the frivolities of friendship because we love truth? Will truth be quicker found because we stop our ears to music and drink no wine, and sleep instead of talking through the long winter’s night? It is not to the cloistered disciplinarian mortifying himself in solitude that we are to turn, but to the well-sunned nature, the man who practises the art of living to the best advantage, so that nothing is stunted but some things are permanently more valuable than others.

The great paradox is that truth — the truth — is at once multifarious and unitary, something Woolf captures in her altogether exquisite meditation on creativity as the antipode to the “non-being” that slips over reality like cotton wool, at the end of which she writes:

Behind the cotton wool is hidden a pattern… The whole world is a work of art [and] we are parts of the work of art. Hamlet or a Beethoven quartet is the truth about this vast mass that we call the world. But there is no Shakespeare, there is no Beethoven; certainly and emphatically there is no God; we are the words; we are the music; we are the thing itself.

Photo Gallery: The Craftsmen Shaping Texas Surf Culture

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In Texas, a good surfboard has to earn its keep. Gulf Coast waves can be fickle, the wind rarely takes a day off, and when a proper swell rolls through, you’d better be ready. Making the most of those fleeting opportunities takes more than a favorable forecast. It takes ingenuity, experience and the right board beneath your feet.

Those boards and the people that make them took center stage at the 11th annual Surftoberfest in downtown Corpus Christi, where The Board Walk brought together more than 20 Texas surfboard shapers for a celebration of the people and craftsmanship behind the boards. Organized by photographer Jon Steele alongside surfers and Executive Surf Club owners Brad and Richard Lomax, the installation showcased an impressive cross-section of the state’s shaping community.

Jon Steele

Held on October 17, 2026, the event blended Texas surf culture with the traditional trappings of Oktoberfest: bratwurst, schnitzel, pretzels and plenty of beer. But beyond the festivities, The Board Walk had a bigger purpose: to give overdue recognition to the craftsmen who have helped build and sustain the Lone Star State’s surf scene.

Texas surfboard shaping dates back at least to the late 1960s, when Danny Jauer was making boards in high school and Galveston’s BJ Williamson was developing his own shapes. Since then, generations of shapers have experimented with designs suited to the Gulf’s particular conditions, while others have carried their knowledge and craftsmanship to some of the world’s most celebrated waves.

Consider Corpus Christi native Robert “Cowboy Bob” Rudine, who collaborated with Williamson on handcrafted balsa boards, including a custom semi-gun built for big-wave surfer Bobby Gonzales. Their work is one example of how Texas shaping extends well beyond the state’s own coastlines, connecting local craftsmanship to surfing’s broader history.

Roy Maza whittling away…square those rails!

Jon Steele

For Steele, documenting these stories is just the beginning. Much of Texas shaping history has been passed down through conversations, personal recollections and the boards themselves, rather than through any comprehensive written record. The Board Walk aims to change that, creating an annual showcase that can grow alongside a more complete historical timeline. Steele also plans to incorporate video interviews in future installments.

The portraits that follow offer a glimpse into this community, pairing images of the shapers with photographs of their workshops, their work and the waves their boards were built to ride. Some are established veterans; others represent the next generation carrying the craft forward.

Different backgrounds, different approaches, different boards. All share a common thread: a dedication to making something that brings surfing to life. Every board has a story. These are the people who shape it.

A monochrome palette and influences from Japanese metabolism characterise Bistrot à Franky in Montreal, Canada designed by local outfit Atelier Zébulon Perron.

The restaurant is a home base for local chef Francis Blais in the city's hip Mile End neighbourhood.

Atelier Zébulon Perron took what it views as the standard design language of a Montreal bistro – bright lighting and lots of activity – and translated it into a more contemporary design.

For studio founder Zebulon Perron, the monochrome whites and beiges, interspersed with milled aluminium accents and a large zinc bar top, allowed the studio to experiment with textures and lighting to create visual interest and contrast in the dining space.

"With the monochromy of the restaurant, we experimented with textures – shiny versus matte, tile nd and stucco – and how light reacts to those surfaces in different ways," Perron told Dezeen.

The studio emphasised the postmodern and "retrofuturist" elements of the design, pointing to the custom chandeliers with their illuminated Pyrex tubes and exposed wiring, fabricated by Perron's long-time collaborators, local lighting design studio Lambert & Fils.

Perron highlighted the milled aluminium wine cellar that sits above the bar face, interspersed with portholes that are a direct reference to Japanese metabolist architecture.

"There's a postmodern, 1980s aesthetic that naturally rose from everything we were doing," said Perron.

Visitors enter the restaurant through a red-painted hallway and a metal door and handle carved from butcher block by local cabinetmaker Olivier Doray.

Inside, only the terrazzo floor was a holdover from the building's former use. Granite tabletops cover some of the larger tables, while the classic bistrot white tablecloths are draped over the smaller tables.

Blond leather banquettes were placed below large mirrors on the wall opposite the bar, and these larger seating arrangements are centred by large butcher block tables, also fabricated by Doray.

Custom walnut chairs were designed by local industrial designer Guillaume Sasseville.

On the walls, white tile is interspersed with stucco and works of art, curated by local filmmaker Xavier Dolan. These artworks give pops of colour and Perron said that the monochromatic design elements allow both the food and the artworks to stand out in the space.

"Since the canvas is pretty neutral, the food and the artwork take centre stage," he said.

A set of windows opens up the kitchen space to the dining room.

Custom bar seating features white columns with seats informed by transportation design, and Perron noted that the metal details and clean lines of the bar space, as well as the metal fans, also give the interior a streamlined look.

Anthropic: That Claude Code extension compromise… couldn't be us

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic has denied allegations that a malicious version of the official Claude Code extension published to Cursor’s marketplace was linked to any kind of compromise of its own systems.

The company was forced to issue a statement after prominent figures in the cybersecurity industry alleged that the extension, which infected up to 5,000 developers, was uploaded to Cursor’s marketplace following a breach at Claudemaker Towers.

Felix Rieseberg, a member of Anthropic’s technical staff, Xeeted today: "We're working with Cursor on this incident, but it wasn’t a compromise of Anthropic's systems or employee laptops.

"A malicious actor used access to Cursor's marketplace to publish a tampered copy of the Claude Code extension, which we didn't build or publish. The versions on our official channels are not affected, and this only impacts Cursor users."

First spotted by EDR vendor Phorion, an unknown party pushed an update to Cursor's official Claude Code extension for Visual Studio Code that contained a persistent SSH backdoor written for Windows, macOS, and Linux.

According to Cursor, the extension – version 2.1.313 – was available to download for around 11 hours between 0227 and 1335 PT (0927 and 2035 UTC) on Thursday.

The vendors' analyses of the implant revealed that it collected information about the host machine and user, including usernames, configured Git emails, and public IP addresses, as well as attempting to open a reverse SSH tunnel.

Crucially, Cursor echoed Phorion's assessment that the package contained a critical flaw.

The script responsible for opening the SSH tunnel required admin rights on macOS and Windows to do so, and in the vast majority of cases it did not have these, resulting in failure.

Regardless, the extension still forwards the collected host and user data to the attacker, as well as dropping the attacker's SSH key and installing the persistence mechanism, so developers must go threat hunting in case their instance allows for the SSH tunnel to be created successfully.

Simply installing the latest version of the extension is not enough to clear the persistence mechanism, either.

The full details of how to remediate the issue, and links to download scripts to wipe the infection, are laid out in Cursor's account of the situation.

Aside from Rieseberg's statement today, neither Cursor nor Anthropic have commented on how the malicious package made its way to the official marketplace.

Anthropic's position is that a malicious actor uploaded the tampered copy to Cursor, but the latter has not outlined how it reached the marketpplace.

"[We] removed the malicious version from the marketplace immediately upon detection and blocked further distribution," Cursor said in the advisory. "We followed with a full shutdown of the VSCode Marketplace out of an abundance of caution."

It also ran checks to confirm no other extensions were compromised in the same way, rotated its own keys before restoring the marketplace, and worked with a cloud hosting provider to block the connection to the attacker's server, it said. ®
