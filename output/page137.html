Testing Results

Ease of Use

I found the Goal Zero Yeti 1500 easy to use right out of the box. The display is simple to navigate and shows how much battery is left, how much power you’re using, and how quickly it’s charging. Power and Performance

What stood out to me during testing was how much the Yeti 1500 can handle at once. I can keep multiple devices plugged in without having to constantly unplug one thing to charge another, and the display makes it easy to see how much power I’m using. With 2,000 watts of continuous output, it has enough capacity for more than just phones and smaller electronics. Battery and Charging

Battery life is one of the most important features of a power station, especially if you’re taking it camping or relying on it during an outage. The Goal Zero is worth every penny. Full stop. At $1,500, it’s definitely an investment, but if you spend a lot of time in the outdoors or want something reliable to have on hand when the power goes out, I think the performance and peace of mind justify the price.

The best way to survive a winter storm is to not get caught in one. True as that may be, however, it’s not a very useful tip when the snow has already started falling. As weather grows more unpredictable, getting stuck on the trail, even when you’ve dutifully checked the forecasts, could happen to any of us. The key to surviving is always being prepared for the worst by packing the 10 essentials and reading articles like this, even if you’re just planning to go on a day hike.

“If you ask me what’s the highest risk, it’s those short hikes because they’re the ones most people aren’t prepared for the longer term,” says Dan Wowak of Coalcracker Bushcraft, a wilderness school and YouTube channel based in Pennsylvania. “A two-mile hike can turn into a devastating emergency situation. You roll an ankle, a storm comes in, it gets dark, you have no equipment, and you don’t know what to do.”

We talked to Wowak and two other experienced hikers about what to do when bad weather rolls in with no warning. The first step: They all suggest packing the right things for winter, including an emergency shelter, communication device, extra layers, navigation, and a fire starter.

1. Stop and Evaluate

You might think that if you’re only a mile from the trailhead, you’re home free. But as snow starts to cover the trail and any tracks you may have left, you might as well be days away. “When you’re out and the weather is turning, you might be inclined to go faster,” says Danny Giovale, the founder of Kahtoola. “I would strongly suggest stopping for a sec.”

Knowing when to stop and wait out the storm will save you from precarious situations. Giovale and Wowak suggest considering how far you have left to go and the time of day. If the storm is moving in quickly and it’s getting dark, it’s best to stop and prioritize survival. Nighttime, Wowak says, is one of the worst times to travel because both visibility and temperatures are at their lowest. So if dusk approaches and you’re not prepared with the equipment and knowledge to travel in the dark, settle in to spend the night out there.

2. Set Up—or Find—Shelter

If you packed an emergency bivy or a tent, it’s crucial to set that up immediately to protect yourself from the elements, like snow, rain, and wind. Blocking the weather is going to prolong your survival timeline. “If you’re not dry, you’re in trouble really fast,” says Jon King, who hikes daily and sends the trail report for 10,000-footer San Jacinto Peak in California.

If you don’t have shelter, Giovale suggests looking for a cave or building your own in the snow. This, of course, requires prior knowledge, but the idea is to create a big pile of snow. Let it set up into a dome and then dig out a space for your body. Don’t forget to poke an air vent.

Then there are trails with man-made shelters, like on San Jacinto Peak, says King. Part of your preparation is knowing where these huts and structures are located in case you ever need them.

3. Keep Warm

As your hands grow cold, they become less functional. As your body loses heat, you become at risk for developing hypothermia and making rash decisions. In addition to a shelter, Giovale always brings a lightweight shell jacket with a hood. It’s “one of the ounce-per-ounce most impactful pieces of gear,” he says. Staying as dry as possible with extra layers will keep you warm. Keep exercising and moving to stimulate blood flow to your fingers and toes.

Wowak also recommends building a fire. “Getting a campfire started is a very relaxing and primal thing that makes people feel safe in just about any situation,” he says. Some caveats apply. It’s harder to do in a torrential downpour than in a snowstorm. It’s also not always advised in fire-prone areas or during a fire ban unless it’s a life-or-death situation.

Should you spend the night outside, make sure to insulate your body from the ground so you don’t lose body heat, he adds. A foam mat will work, but in lieu of that, so will pine boughs. In a pinch, you can sit on your backpack.

4. Stay Hydrated 

Ration your water reserves to stay hydrated, or use the campfire to melt snow and warm up a drink. Drinking cold water can drain your energy, Wowak says, so warm water is better.

Unless you brought snacks, you might start to feel hunger pangs. Hydration can help there, too. “,Even if we don’t have a lot of food, if we were only planning on a day hike and a storm comes in, and we’re stuck there, being hydrated will trick your body and you won’t feel as hungry because you’re full of fluid,” Wowak says.

5. Stay Positive

The worst thing you can do in any kind of survival scenario is dwell on the situation, Wowak says. Keep your mind engaged with survival tasks like making your camp bed, boiling water, organizing your gear, moving your toes and fingers, etcetera.

Giovale says to trust your prior training to get you through the storm.

“My experience with being in serious situations outside is that whatever you have learned or been exposed to, you’ll probably remember,” he says.

The Biggest Archery Mistakes You’re Making This Bow Season

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Over the past 20 years, I’ve shot, competed with and hunted with everything from selfbows to compounds. If there’s a mistake you can make while shooting a bow, I’ve done it. Luckily, I’ve also had the chance to learn from world-class coaches, Olympians, and the best archers in the world. So I’ve learned how to fix the worst errors and spot trouble in other bowhunters.

Here are five common archery mistakes that new and experienced bowhunters make that ultimately cost opportunities in the field. 

Grip 

The easiest archery flaw to fix is also one of the most common ones. When you grip your bow properly, it becomes easier to tune, and your accuracy will improve because you won’t induce inconsistent torque into the bow. To grip your bow, place your hand as high as it will go in the grip, turn your knuckles so they’re at a 45-degree angle to the riser and make sure the bow touches your hand only between your thumb pad and lifeline. 

Read Next: How to Grip a Compound Bow for Better Hunting Accuracy

Relying on Autopilot 

Have you ever used the wrong pin, forgotten to look through your peep or fired as soon as your pin was close to a deer’s kill zone? Those mistakes happen because you’re relying on your subconscious to shoot the bow.

In the backyard, you can let your subconscious run the shot. Heck, you could shoot half asleep and do all right. But shooting under pressure is a different story. You can’t trust your subconscious in those situations because it will take shortcuts to skip to the ultimate goal — firing the arrow. It’s a lot like AI: It finds the most efficient solution, and wiping you out might be mere collateral damage. 

Instead of relying on autopilot, you need a firm shot process that you fully control. Make a list of the key steps of your shot and check them off one by one. This keeps you in the present and focused on the process, not the result.

Assuming Your Bow Is in Tune 

With time and repetitions, your strings can move, which affects your bow’s tune and sight tape. Don’t be one of those hunters who misses an easy shot because their sight was off or their drop-away rest timing changed. Every month or so, check your sight and rest timing, shoot your bow through paper and make sure your broadheads are in tune. 

String Pressure 

When you first learned to shoot a bow, someone probably told you that you need a consistent anchor point. But that doesn’t mean you need to anchor the string into your face. Pressing your face or your nose against the string causes oscillation, which leads to left-and-right inconsistencies. The string can touch your face, but only lightly. 

Fighting the Bow 

If you find yourself trying really hard to hold your bow steady, you’re approaching archery the wrong way. A steady aim is the result of your form and bow setup. There’s no muscling your way to it. You also can’t make your pin stop moving. The goal is for your pin to have a slow, consistent float around the spot. Once you achieve that, your next job is to focus on shot execution. 

A Final Tip

Scott Einsmann

 See It

When you make a change to your archery form or your bow, it’s normal for your accuracy to suffer at first. Trust the process during that learning period, and shoot at a big target such as the Blackout Longshot 36. The goal during the period is to get used to your change and not care about accuracy until your shot feels good again.

Scandinavian design is celebrated for light-filled spaces, pale woods, clean lines, and a sense of calm. Translating that aesthetic to a cruise ship, however, presents a spatial challenge—particularly when compared with the backdrop of the oversized, ornate interiors typical of mass-market cruise liners.

Sunlight pours through the lounge’s expansive windows, illuminating pale wood, textured upholsterery, and warm leather furnishings that lend the space a residential feel. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Viking has made Scandinavian modernism central to its fleet’s design identity, creating onboard spaces that feel less like conventional cruise ships and more like considered residential interiors. Across the vessels, the design language reflects a contemporary interpretation of Scandinavian aesthetics, sometimes called “New Nordic,” through natural textures, tactile materials, subtle color, and sculptural, carefully proportioned furnishings.

A sculptural wood lattice ceiling beneath expansive glazing brings natural light and organic forms into the Wintergarden lounge. Photography courtesy of Viking.

“Although ‘new Nordic’ is not terminology we use formally, traditional Scandinavian principles have evolved and are being interpreted for how people live and travel today,” says Richard Riveire, founding principal of Rottet Studio and the designer of Viking’s river, ocean, and expedition ships. “There’s still a strong sense of restraint, but it’s balanced by a feeling of being lived in. These days, Scandinavian design is less about minimalism and more about creating spaces that feel welcoming, thoughtful, and enduring.”

A deep blue built-in bookcase displays books, maps, shells, coral, and collected objects, connecting the residential atmosphere of Viking’s interiors with the theme of exploration. Photography courtesy of Viking

Drawing On Nordic Principles

Instead of treating Scandinavian design as a static visual style, Riveire and his team anchored the fleet’s interiors around five underlying principles: residential modernism, nature, craft, heritage, and exploration. This framework establishes a restrained architectural foundation, while fabrics, upholstery, rugs, and furniture introduce warmth and texture. “These tactile details make each space feel inviting and personal, while preserving the simplicity that is so central to Scandinavian design,” says Riveire.

An artwork depicting a figure on a beach anchors the atrium stairwell, bringing Nordic art into one of the ship’s main circulation spaces. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Tapping Into Nordic Heritage

Throughout Viking’s fleet, Nordic heritage appears in a collection of regional artwork, including pieces by celebrated Norwegian painter Edvard Munch. Textiles and decorative objects extend those cultural references, featuring fabrics by Dutch designer Hella Jongerius alongside tabletop accessories by Finnish architect Alvar Aalto.

“That heritage connects the design to Norwegian culture and folklore, while exploration reminds us that this is a ship designed for people who are curious about exploring the world,” says Riveire. “So maps, globes, libraries, constellations and other references to exploration play a meaningful role in our designs.”

A traditional Norwegian Marius blanket brings a familiar geometric knitting pattern into the staterooms, referencing a longstanding Norwegian textile tradition. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Mastering Materiality and Craft

Material choices help give Viking’s expansive public spaces a more intimate, residential character. Light woods, natural woven textiles, and stone bring warmth, texture, and visual depth to the staterooms and public areas. “Craftsmanship is fundamental because it gives a space a sense of humanity and authenticity,” says Riveire. “Our design calls for furniture, artwork, textiles, and decor that evoke handmade traditions or honor historic craftwork.” These references to craft appear throughout the ship, in details such as woven leather lounge chairs in the living room areas, the intricate ceiling latticework, and sculptural wood wall accents.

Dramatic glass walls surround the spa’s hydrotherapy pool, pairing natural stone, sculptural driftwood, and wood loungers to create a serene retreat that brings Scandinavian simplicity indoors. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Integrating Biophilic Design

The Nordic landscape—where birch trees, flora, and wildlife symbolize renewal—informs both the materials and sensory details throughout the ships. References to driftwood, lichen, and moss can be found in staterooms and lounges. In the public bathrooms, guests hear birds softly chirping in the background, while birch tree branches in planters and floor-to-ceiling forest wallpaper appear throughout the common areas and spa. “It’s where the landscape and wildlife outside become part of the indoor experience,” says Riveire.

In the spa, amenities such as a snow grotto, cold plunge, and thalassotherapy pool draw on Nordic bathing traditions. “Water reinforces the connection to the outdoors, making the spa an extension of the Nordic design philosophy,” says Riveire. Slate, stone, and glass reinforce the spa’s restrained, minimalist material palette.

Navy and slate blue stripes add a measured dose of color to the upholstered headboard, complementing the stateroom’s otherwise understated palette. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Layering Subtle Color

Rather than relying on stark whites or an entirely neutral palette, the ship’s interiors layer warm creams, sandy tans, and other earthy tones drawn from nature. Navy and slate blue stripes appear on upholstered stateroom headboards, while translucent aqua glass side plates and water glasses introduce light-reflecting accents to restaurant table settings. Orange and rust accents in throw pillows and carpets provide contrast without disrupting the overall palette. “These touches reflect a more expressive side of Scandinavian design,” says Riveire. “We want guests to experience color, but without introducing a lot of visual noise.”

A wall of glass frames ocean views from the pool deck, where pale loungers, blue-and-white textiles, and natural wood maintain the understated palette. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Framing the Natural Landscape

Floor-to-ceiling windows do more than admit daylight; their placement turns the passing landscape into a focal point of the interiors. “A Norwegian fjord, European river, or Antarctic landscape becomes part of the room, an ever-changing piece of art within the interior,” Riveire says. Sightlines and spatial planning help connect the rooms to their changing surroundings.

On land, designers can anticipate the position of the sun and how daylight will move through a home or building. “At sea, the sun is far more variable—not only in direction, but also in duration,” Riveire says. “During Arctic summers, for example, it may never set.” The interiors must therefore accommodate changing light conditions throughout a voyage. Architectural openings, curtains, and shades adjust as conditions shift, balancing dynamic daylight with interior comfort and privacy. “Spaces are connected to the world outside, while also creating more intimate moments indoors,” says Riveire.

A large window frames the passing river landscape, while pale wood, soft neutrals, and streamlined furnishings keep the suite’s interior visually quiet. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Concealing Technology and Softening Acoustics

A residential atmosphere depends on more than visual details. Sound, temperature, and lighting also shape how comfortable a space feels. To the interiors’ uncluttered appearance, technology is integrated discreetly into wood joinery and architectural details, such as underfloor heating in stateroom bathrooms or subtle lighting controls. Acoustic design plays an equally important role in creating a sense of hygge at sea; sound-absorbing textile wall panels, acoustic carpet underlays, and upholstered surfaces help reduce ambient noise in public areas. “In the Scandinavian tradition, design serves human comfort without demanding attention,” says Riveire. “Technology and acoustics are integrated so subtly that you don’t notice how the space functions—only how calm it makes you feel.”

Maritime artifacts, books, a globe, and warm wood shelving create a residential-style display that reflects Viking’s Scandinavian emphasis on craftsmanship and history. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Designing Intimate Spaces at Scale

Capturing a lived-in, residential warmth can be challenging on a vessel with expansive public areas like multi-level atriums, especially when Scandinavian design favors intimate and approachable spaces. “Creating a residential feeling within a ship requires careful attention to proportion,” says Riveire. “At Viking, we subscribe to a ‘cocoon’ principle—a house-within-a-house concept—where the interior provides a quiet and serene onboard environment, rather than constantly competing for attention.” This principle guides the arrangement of open, social hubs and smaller, quiet seating nooks. “Every furniture grouping has been carefully considered for how guests actually move through and use the space,” adds Riveire.

Throwback Thursday: Panasonic’s quarter century of camera innovation

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s twenty-five years this week since Panasonic released its first digital cameras, and it’s contributed a lot to the industry in the meantime

DPReview

It was twenty-five years ago this week that Panasonic’s first digital cameras hit the market: the DMC-LC5 and DMC-F7. This means it’s also the twenty-fifth anniversary of the “Lumix” brand.

Panasonic, then itself a sub-brand of Matsushita Electric Industrial Co (which has subsequently adopted the Panasonic name for the whole company), was a little late to the digital camera game, as one of its senior directors acknowledged to Reuters in an article quoted in DPReview’s coverage, just before the first cameras reached the shelves.

The company hasn’t ever quite hit the 10% market share target it set for itself (though it apparently made up 10% of the full-frame market in Europe, earlier this year), but it’s produced some excellent and innovative cameras in the meantime. And its influence on modern cameras and the camera industry transcends such simple metrics.

The original Panasonic Lumix DMC-LC5, one of the two cameras with which the company launched the Lumix brand. Despite the Leica branding, the lens seemed familiar.Panasonic

The original DMC-LC5 was a pretty ambitious first camera, with the larger Type 1/1.76 (40mm2) sensor usually reserved for enthusiast cameras and a design that looks a lot like its contemporary, the Canon G2, with a tunnel-type optical viewfinder, top plate display and mode dial.

The LC5 had a Leica-branded lens: the beginning of a relationship with the German company that continues to this day. Though, interestingly, that lens has exactly the same 7-21mm, F2.0-2.5 specifications as the lens Canon was using in its G2. A fractionally larger sensor meant that the Panasonic delivered a slightly wider 33-100mm equiv range from its zoom, but the likelihood of there being two different lenses with such closely matching specs is incredibly low.  

The remarkably LC5-like Leica Digilux 1 would be launched the subsequent year. And that pattern of Leica-branded variants of many of Panasonic’s most interesting (from our enthusiast photographer perspective) models was to continue.

The Panasonic TZ3 put a 10x zoom in a small camera body. And unlike the compact superzooms that had gone before it, it did so with a lens starting at a wideangle, 28mm equiv.DPReview

Highlights include the FZ series of long zoom ‘bridge’ cameras, starting with the FZ1 in 2004 and encompassing a great range of sensor sizes, as well as zoom lengths, through the years. The TZ series (later rebranded as ZS in the American market) helped define and give a name to the compact superzoom style of travel zooms, and were among the first to use digital distortion correction to allow wideangle zooms in a small camera body. And then there were LX compact enthusiast models, whose ultimate successor is this year’s DC-L10, bringing short but bright lenses, larger sensors and the idea that aspect ratio should be a creative option.

DPReview

Leica wasn’t the only company has Panasonic worked closely with, though. In 2004, it joined Olympus and Kodak’s Four Thirds system, producing two DSLRs before spearheading the reinvention of Four Thirds as Micro Four Thirds: the world’s first mirrorless (interchangeable lens) camera system.

It’s strange to look back and realize that there were only five years between the launch of the first wholly designed-for-digital DSLR and the first mirrorless camera. The original Four Thirds DSLR system wasn’t formally discontinued until 2017 but Panasonic had ushered in a new era the day it announced the G1.

It’s hard to imagine Panasonic (or any company, frankly) having such a profound impact on the industry in a single move again, especially as seems unlikely that the organic film/CMOS hybrid sensor its parent company has developed, will make its way across to consumer cameras.

It looked, and operated, a lot like a contemporary DSLR, but the Panasonic DMC-G1 would fundamentally change the industryDPReview

But the creation of Mirrorless camera isn’t Panasonic’s only contribution to camera technology that it’s easy to now take for granted.

Its Dual IS system was the first to use in-lens and in-body stabilization simultaneously, in a co-ordinated manner. Other brands have subsequently adopted this approach, but Panasonic was several years ahead of most.

Likewise, we’re pretty sure Panasonic was the first brand to adopt context-sensitive noise reduction in its JPEG engine: applying more noise reduction in what it interpreted as low-detail regions and less in areas where details needed to be preserved. It was also one of the first to offer subject recognition autofocus trained by machine learning, though it didn’t think to promote it as “AI”-powered, so it didn’t necessarily get the recognition this warranted.

It’s impossible to cover Panasonic’s contribution to the industry without mentioning its GH series of video-forward hybrid cameras

The other very visible impact Panasonic has had on the camera industry has been in raising the expectations of the video capabilities of hybrid cameras. Particularly through its GH series, Panasonic has been the first to shoot 4K, the first to capture 10-bit video, the first to offer Log gamma, the first to have a waveforms display, the first to let you choose the conversion gain mode and the first to be able to capture 32-bit float audio. All of them features that were once the preserve of dedicated video models, many of which have become commonplace on the most mainstream models, because the R&D to provide them has already been done.

Panasonic’s contribution to cameras isn’t something to solely be discussed in the past tense. The S1II is at the cutting edge of what the latest cameras can offer.Richard Butler

This is far from a complete reflection of the impact Panasonic has had. A quarter of a century of progress has seen it deliver a great many interesting cameras and innovations, and cameras like the S1II and L10 suggest there will be plenty of more interesting things to come. But whether you think of the company as Panasonic or Lumix, and regardless of whether you’ve owned and loved one of its products, it’s impossible to ignore its contributions over the past 25 years.
