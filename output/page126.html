‘Look what I brought home for you’: GOP embraces earmark revival as Election Day looms

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With voters sour on Republican governance, GOP candidates in the most competitive House and Senate races are reaching for a time-tested message that has grown out of fashion in their party: bringing home the federal bacon.

A POLITICO analysis of the dozen closest congressional races for Republican incumbents revealed that most are highlighting earmark success stories in the final days before voters head to the polls.

The ability to secure federal funding for pet projects back home — something many in the GOP have spent years trying to curtail — has given those candidates a positive talking point as President Donald Trump’s flagging approval ratings and widespread voter dissatisfaction over issues like the Iran war, high gas prices and tariffs weigh on other messages.

“When the president's job approval is in the 30s, it is helpful for Republican candidates to be able to create some distance from the president. It's challenging to do that without encouraging the president's wrath,” Republican pollster Whit Ayres said in an interview. “But focusing on bringing home the bacon might be one very good way to do it without antagonizing the president.”

In Michigan, Rep. Tom Barrett is out with an ad about a $750,000 earmark for youth mentoring; in New York, Rep. Mike Lawler is talking about how he secured $120 million for local projects.

Rep. Rob Bresnahan of Pennsylvania is telling voters he has “brought back more than $110 million to our community,” including millions in earmarks, while Rep. David Valadao has run ads showcasing $500 million in earmarks for water projects in California’s Central Valley.

Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins, a GOP moderate seeking to project independence from Trump in a competitive contest, has repeatedly touted the exact count of the earmarks she has locked in over the last half-decade for her home state of Maine.

The sum: 672 projects totaling $1.5 billion, “from Madawaska to Kittery, from Eastport to Rumford,” as she said in her opening statement earlier this month in her first debate against Democrat Troy Jackson. This past week, Collins has peppered voters with county-specific text messages detailing how much she’s secured for each area.

There are countless other examples of earmark campaigning among vulnerable Republican incumbents, though many conservatives remain reluctant to associate themselves with the practice. It still carries a stigma of corruption after two Republicans were sent to prison on related bribery charges in the mid-2000s, paving the way for the GOP to do away with earmarks in 2011.

That left GOP candidates unable to play up the clearest spoils of incumbency ahead of the “blue wave” of 2018. But Democrats revived them in 2021 with new safeguards, and now Republicans are taking advantage as they navigate another tough midterm cycle.

“That word ‘earmark’ can carry some negative baggage, especially among the old fiscal conservatives,” nonpartisan pollster Mike Noble said in an interview. “But when you describe that same expenditure as funding for a local water system, highway, public safety project — voters evaluate that differently.”

Recognizing the advantages, Republicans opted to keep earmarks around under unified party leadership in 2025, resulting in the enactment of almost $16 billion worth of earmarks this year. For those who still consider earmarks anathema to the GOP’s values of fiscal restraint, it’s causing complications on the trail.

Republican Rep. Bill Huizenga of Michigan is fielding criticism for not requesting any earmarks for his district. While he airs ads calling himself “tight with a buck,” his opponents are telling voters that lawmakers like Huizenga are increasing their net worth “while turning down community project funding.”

Some recent polling shows Huizenga trailing his Democratic challenger, state Sen. Sean McCann, by 1 percentage point.

McCann said in an interview he will “go to bat” for earmarks if he defeats Huizenga: “That's an expectation that people have when you want to represent them — that you do the things that are important and secure crucial investments for the district.”

Huizenga defended himself in a statement, saying “earmarks have a long history of waste and abuse by politicians looking to enrich themselves in office.”

Huizenga noted that he has “helped secure hundreds of millions in federal investments for Southwest Michigan without requesting a single earmark,” including a $400 million grant last year for a nuclear power plant and $51 million for a housing redevelopment project.

He isn’t the only Republican incumbent in a tight race who has foregone earmarks. Alaska Sen. Dan Sullivan, who is facing a competitive challenge from former Democratic Rep. Mary Peltola, refused to request cash for district-specific projects as part of the annual government funding process. Instead, he points to his support for efforts made possible through the 2021 infrastructure law signed by then-President Joe Biden, as well as other legislation and grants the Trump administration doles out for local efforts.

Trump officials have traveled to Alaska in recent weeks to stand alongside Sullivan in announcing the delivery of hundreds of millions of dollars in federal funding for the state, including $38 million in energy grants and $250 million for rail infrastructure.

Sen. Roger Marshall (R-Kan.), who appears to be in a dead heat with his Democratic challenger Adam Hamilton, also didn’t ask for any federal funding this year. But he is getting flak for seeking a $4.2 million earmark in 2024 to finance improvements to increase water to a wildlife refuge that borders his ranch.

Spokespeople for the Sullivan and Marshall campaigns did not respond to requests for comment.

Whether voters perceive earmark requests as positive or negative likely depends heavily on how a candidate frames the talking point, Noble said, acknowledging that incumbents rarely use the word in campaign ads or town hall speeches and instead speak broadly about how they “deliver” for their district.

“Voters, at the end of the day — it's about direct benefit. What are they getting out of this deal? That's one of the powers of incumbency. They have that ability to say, ‘Hey, look what I brought home for you,’” he said. “But again, what else are they going to talk about?”

Haunting the Cepheus Flare

Explanation: Spooky shapes seem to haunt this dusty expanse, drifting through the night in the royal constellation Cepheus. Of course, the shapes are cosmic dust clouds visible in dimly reflected starlight. Far from your own neighborhood, they lurk above the plane of the Milky Way at the edge of the Cepheus Flare molecular cloud complex some 1,200 light-years away. Over 2 light-years across and brighter than most of the other ghostly apparitions, vdB 141 or Sh2-136 is also known as the Ghost Nebula. It lies at lower right in the starry field of view. Inside the Ghost Nebula are reddish telltale signs of dense cores collapsing in the early stages of star formation. With the eerie hue of interstellar dust reflecting bluish light from hot young stars of NGC 7023, the Iris Nebula stands out against the dark just left of center. In the broad telescopic frame, these fertile interstellar dust fields stretch almost six full moons across the sky.

Shrouded in ice in the lonely Weddell Sea, Vega Island is an eerie sight to behold. On this speck of land east of the Antarctic Peninsula, ice, rock outcrops, and glacial moraines combine to create the appearance of a smirking skull in profile looking toward Devil Island.

These images show Vega Island’s creepy countenance at the start of austral autumn (left) and in spring (right). They were acquired with the OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 on March 22, 2025, and October 10, 2026, respectively. (Clouds obscured the island in March 2026.) A nunatak jutting out of the ice has the appearance of an eye socket, a lateral moraine forms a mouth, and Bahía del Diablo (Devil’s Bay) Glacier constitutes a jawbone. In the March 2025 image, a layer of snow coats the ice, which is typical of the region’s wettest month. That contrasts with the October 2026 image, when snow is absent from large areas and darker ice surfaces are visible.

Above-average temperatures in winter 2026—particularly in June—were partly responsible for so much bare ice in October, said glaciologist Mauri Pelto of Nichols College. The ice exposed on Vega and nearby islands indicates limited new snow accumulation over the winter, further reduced by sublimation and wind scouring, he said. “The winter season was no treat for these glaciers.”

The melt season ahead may also be more trick than treat. Bare ice is darker than snow and absorbs more solar energy, meaning that melting is poised to speed up as the days grow longer and warmer. “Even a thin cover of new snow from a spring storm would be helpful in the short run,” Pelto said. “When entombed in snow and ice, the skull is preserved. But when exposed to melt, it weathers away, which will lead to a changing jawline and expanding eye socket.”

October 10, 2026 NASA Earth Observatory/Lauren Dauphin

The first comprehensive mapping of Vega Island’s ice was aided by Landsat imagery. In a study published in 1982, scientists described the island’s four ice caps with protruding rocky cliffs and outlet glaciers. They leveraged early Landsat observations along with aerial photos and topographic maps to catalog 29 glaciers on Vega.

Of those glaciers, Devil’s Bay Glacier has been closely monitored since the austral summer of 1998-1999, with researchers measuring an overall decline in its mass. Satellite images acquired in recent decades show ice loss from other glaciers on the island as well, with increased areas of exposed bedrock.

Long before scientists began tracking its glaciers, Vega was a bright spot in a tale of terror born out of early exploration of the area. The Swedish Antarctic Expedition of 1901-1903 set out to study the geography, geology, climate, and wildlife on and around the eastern Antarctic Peninsula. The party became split into three groups during their explorations, isolated for a winter in different locations. Adding to the horror, they lost their ship to the pack ice. Cape Well-met, as it would come to be known, off the “forehead” of the Vega Island skull, was the site of a happy reunion of two of the groups in spring 1903. Soon thereafter, an Argentinean expedition rescued the survivors of the ordeal.

MoMA, Centre Pompidou, and M+ Will Treat Their Holdings as One Collection in Unprecedented Alliance

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Six years ago, the director of Hong Kong’s M+ museum, Suhanya Raffel, told cultural strategist András Szántó, in an interview for the book The Future of the Museum, that in the years to come museums will need to “stand together and work to help one another to deliver a more sustainable baseline.” Collaboration, she went on, “will be a key aspect of the next chapter of museum work.” Her words seem to have been prophetic: on Friday morning in Hong Kong, M+, the Museum of Modern Art in New York, and the Centre Pompidou in Paris announced a wide-ranging cultural alliance unprecedented in its scale. At the heart of the alliance is an agreement that the three institutions will treat their respective holdings as a shared collection, curated by staff spread across three continents.

“If we succeed,” Laurent Le Bon, the Pompidou’s president, told ARTnews in Paris, during a joint interview that also included Raffel and MoMA director Christophe Cherix, “we will be more than the sum of our parts, and I think that is something quite new.”

The combined holdings of MoMA and the Pompidou mean the total shared collection will number in the hundreds of thousands of objects, and the three institutions are also stewards of voluminous archives. The collection at M+, founded six years ago, numbers around 8,000 objects, but the museum has a definitive collection of contemporary Chinese art, much of it donated by Swiss businessman and former diplomat Uli Sigg. The Pompidou houses the largest modern art museum in Europe (its holdings are a national collection owned by the French state). The potential audience will be enormous: based on current figures, the museums will have a combined 10 million annual in-person visitors and 125 million online visitors.

The collection sharing will kick off with several initiatives. Beginning in late 2027, a selection of major works from M+’s collection will be installed on long-term loan in MoMA’s collection galleries, and in 2028, a long-term Centre Pompidou gallery will also open at MoMA, with similar, parallel plans in development at the other museums. (The Pompidou is currently closed for a five-year restoration of its building and is scheduled to reopen in 2030.)

As part of the alliance, the partner institutions will, over the next five years, co-organize an exhibition exploring the complex concept of “the contemporary,” which will include works from all three collections and be presented at all three institutions; curators at the Centre Pompidou and M+ will join MoMA’s C-MAP (Contemporary and Modern Art Perspectives), a cross-departmental research program founded in 2009 with the aim of exploring art histories outside North America and Western Europe.

Additionally, a joint steering committee, made up of the three museum directors along with department heads and curators from the three museums, will begin to work on such priorities as exhibitions and collection sharing, research, conservation, and professional development. The committee will meet monthly, and there will be one major in-person meeting a year, the first of which will take place next month at MoMA. (Subsequent annual meetings will rotate among the museums.)

To a certain extent, the alliance comes out of previous partnerships between M+ and the other two institutions. In February 2025, M+ and MoMA signed a memorandum of understanding that encompassed collection sharing, joint research, and curatorial exchanges. Earlier this year, M+ and the Centre Pompidou announced their own multiyear strategic partnership, which included plans for the Pompidou to inaugurate its renovated space with an exhibition of works from both museums’ collections, followed by a presentation of that show at M+.

In the interview with ARTnews in Paris, the three directors acknowledged that the new cultural alliance grew out of the previous bilateral partnerships. They stressed, however, that it also grew out of the three institutions’ commonalities, such as cross-disciplinary collecting and a focus on the art of the present, as well as the lineage the museums form: nearly a century of collecting contemporary art, starting with MoMA’s founding in 1929 and continuing through the Pompidou’s opening in 1977 and M+’s in 2021. Raffel has acknowledged that Centre Pompidou was “one of the key institutions” that M+ “looked to that helped us guide the principles around building an institution like this for Asia.”

“We each have things that are absolutely unique in the world,” said Cherix. “Our first role is of course preservation. But how can we make those collections work harder for us and touch people well beyond the walls of our institutions?”

This alliance differs from past models such as traveling exhibitions, co-organized shows, and collaborations on specific projects, Cherix explained, in that it is a long-term commitment to building programs together from the ground up, one that will involve “bringing our teams much closer together.”

The new arrangement also aims to take advantage of economies of scale and to make things like shipping more sustainable. Raffel and Cherix pointed out that, for instance, sending parts of M+’s collection of contemporary Chinese art to MoMA for extended periods would not only allow those works to rotate through the MoMA galleries but also make them more easily available to other museums in the United States.

In a world in which museums want their collections to reflect global perspectives, sharing makes more sense than continuing to accumulate separately. “We can’t just keep collecting, so we are sharing instead,” said Raffel. This raises the bar for acquisitions: to be added to the shared collection, a work will, as Cherix put it, “need to have no equal” in any of the three collections.

“Even if we want to be devoted to the art of our time, we become a historical collection,” Cherix continued. “How can we reset that? I think this alliance gives us exactly the capacity to have a different scope, and a forward-looking vision.”

More museums have partnered in recent years to share collections, pool resources, and expand their audiences. The strategy has evolved from standard short-term exhibition loans to deeply integrated models that include joint acquisitions, shared private donations, regional networks, and long-term international exchanges. Joint acquisitions have become increasingly common since 2003, when the Whitney Museum, Tate, and the Centre Pompidou teamed up to purchase Bill Viola’s video installation Five Angels for the Millennium.

An arrangement somewhat similar to the MoMA–Pompidou–M+ model came in 2018, when Chinese Indonesian collector Budi Tek partnered with the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA) in a collection-sharing arrangement with Shanghai’s Yuz Museum, which Tek had founded. (China rejected attempts to make the Yuz Museum a public institution; Tek died in 2022.) In that case, a new foundation was formed to oversee the collection, which would live in China but otherwise travel between Los Angeles and Shanghai for temporary exhibitions. Works from LACMA’s collection, for example, traveled to the Yuz Museum in 2009 for the exhibition “In Production: Art and the Studio System,” while in 2021, 20 pieces from Tek’s collection went on display at LACMA for the exhibition “Legacies of Exchange.”

More recently, museums have agreed to be the joint stewards of certain collectors’ holdings. In 2022, around 100 works from the collection of Greek industrialist Dimitri Daskalopoulos became a shared gift to the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum and the Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago. In 2024, three Los Angeles museums—the Hammer, LACMA, and the Museum of Contemporary Art—jointly received some 350 works owned by Jarl and Pamela Mohn. Those three institutions continue to add to that collection, called the Mohn Art Collective (MAC3).
