Trump DOJ’s California noncitizen voter list riddled with errors

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

More than 40% of people flagged by the Trump administration as suspected noncitizen voters in California aren’t on the state’s current voter rolls, according to a new analysis filed in federal court Saturday, raising fresh questions about the Justice Department‘s efforts to intervene in ballot counting days before the midterm elections.

Researchers with UCLA’s Voting Rights Project examined a list of 20,930 people identified by federal officials as potentially ineligible voters. They found that 8,880 names, or 42%, could not be matched to California’s current voter file.

The analysis also identified at least 22 people who appear to be U.S. citizens, with researchers warning that the number of citizens included on the list could be considerably higher.

The findings were submitted in litigation brought by the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights, which is challenging the administration’s efforts to use federal immigration data to identify suspected noncitizen voters.

James O’Keefe election sting ends in his own felony voter fraud charges

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Conservative activist James O’Keefe set out to expose alleged vulnerabilities in Minnesota’s election system. Now, he faces felony charges stemming from the undercover operation.

O’Keefe, formerly of Project Veritas and current CEO of the O’Keefe Media Group, and conservative influencer Cameron Higby were charged Wednesday with unlawful voting and identity theft after allegedly impersonating registered voters at Minneapolis polling places during the state’s Aug. 11 primary.

According to Hennepin County prosecutors, the men wore disguises, used fake accents and provided election workers with the names of registered voters without their knowledge or permission. They filmed the encounters and later posted videos claiming to expose weaknesses in Minnesota’s voter registration procedures.

The operation targeted the state’s *vouching* system, which allows registered voters to verify the residency of people seeking to register on Election Day. The process requires sworn statements and identifying information from prospective voters.

Neither O’Keefe nor Higby cast a ballot or signed the required vouching oath. Prosecutors contend that misrepresenting their identities while beginning the voting process was itself unlawful.

O’Keefe defended the operation as investigative journalism, calling the prosecution an attack on First Amendment rights. Higby also denied breaking the law, saying the pair intended to expose election vulnerabilities.

The charges are the latest controversy involving O’Keefe’s undercover tactics. In 2017, reporters at the Washington Post caught him trying to sell them a false story. In 2009, he collaborated on hidden-camera investigations targeting Planned Parenthood and gained national attention for videos involving the community organization ACORN. He later settled a lawsuit brought by a former ACORN employee for $100,000 without admitting wrongdoing.

But Minnesota officials say the latest operation demonstrated something different from what O’Keefe intended.

Election workers noticed suspicious behavior and reported it, prompting an investigation. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty said the state’s system for identifying and responding to potential misconduct functioned as intended. This vouching process itself accounted for fewer than 0.6% of votes cast in Minnesota’s 2024 general election, according to state election officials.

O’Keefe and Higby are scheduled to appear in court Nov. 16. Neither has been convicted.

This Sunday, conservative pastors will preach against Democrats’ claims to Christian faith

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

When Robert Jeffress takes the pulpit at First Baptist Dallas on Sunday, he’ll give his 16,000 congregants a not-so-subtle message: Beware of false faith.

Jeffress will preach about the Tower of Babel, a topic that shares “some parallels to what's happening in the election,” he said in a recent interview with POLITICO.

“My goal as a pastor is not to get people in the voting booth, but to keep them out of hell,” the archconservative megachurch pastor, televangelist and longtime Republican backer said. Texas Democratic Senate nominee James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminarian, “seems harmless,” Jeffress continued, “but the toxic faith he is preaching will lead people away from heaven, not toward it.”

On Sunday morning, conservative evangelical pastors across the country will be sharing a similar message, with the hope of galvanizing their congregations to support right-wing candidates and causes on Election Day.

But the big difference from recent years: They’ll be preaching against Democrats in four battleground U.S. Senate and House races who’ve made their faith a key part of their campaign message.

Talarico is a few classes short of becoming an ordained minister. Kansas Senate Democratic nominee Adam Hamilton is pastor of the largest United Methodist church in the country. In Iowa, Presbyterian pastor Lindsay James and Lutheran minister Sarah Trone Garriott are running for Congress. All four are in tight races in red territory, according to polls.

They’ve all made their faith an explicit part of their closing message.

“Jesus said, ‘Do unto others as you'd have them do unto you.’ This is really important. All of these things guide us,” Hamilton says in his stump speech.

“I approve this message because my faith teaches me that caring for my neighbor is not optional,” James says in a face-to-camera ad.

It’s a marked deviation from decades of the Democratic Party’s gradual march toward a less religious tone that has matched their increasingly secular voting base, and an effort to win over politically moderate believers who have grown disgruntled with Trump and the Republicans.

These Democrats' campaigns have put the country’s conservative evangelical pastors in unfamiliar territory: needing to convince their congregants that self-described fellow believers deserve their condemnation, not their vote.

“There are some Christians that don’t know how to respond to it, because [the candidates] are using all the same language that we do, but they are advocating for a lot of things that we are not for,” said Josh Bingaman, lead pastor of the First Church Open Bible in Des Moines, Iowa.

Many conservative pastors contend these Democrats are wolves in sheep’s clothing, pointing out disagreements on abortion, same-sex marriage, transgender rights or even the nature of God.

In Wichita, Kansas, Rob Rotola of Word of Life Church will lay out the stakes of the election in his Sunday sermon.

He said he has “no problem” calling Hamilton a false prophet, and imagines when his sermon is through, his congregants will “feel sorry for Adam Hamilton and would pray for him, but would tell their friends where he stands.”

In Iowa, Mike Demastus of Fort Des Moines Church of Christ plans to remind his congregants of “the importance of every single one of us, as believers, to go and vote and be engaged in this process.” He doesn’t include Trone Garriott among those “believers”: “She's a poser and a fake, and most conservative evangelical Christians see through that as quickly as she can say it.”

Bob Vander Plaats, an influential evangelical leader in the state, called Trone Garriott a “dangerous politician” and dismissed James as straying from the true path.

“To hijack Scripture to make it fit your agenda, versus to embrace the comprehensive nature of Scripture to fit God's agenda, that's a very, very risky thing to do,” Vander Plaats said in an interview.

A spokesperson for Talarico pointed to the candidate’s past statements on faith, including his assertion that “when I take on the politicians who are serving themselves and not the people, I’m doing it on biblical grounds.”

Trone Garriott spokesperson Zach Schenkman said that “while others try to divide Iowans and dictate how every Christian should practice their faith, Sarah is focused on showing up to make a difference for her neighbors.”

James spokesperson Jackson Smith said: “No politician gets to define or dictate how any Iowan practices their faith, and Iowans are disgusted by this kind of politics.”

Hamilton’s campaign declined to comment.

Trump has long enjoyed overwhelming support from white evangelical Christians: In 2024, exit polls found more than eight in 10 white evangelical Protestants voted for the president. Even now, as his approval rating among other demographic groups has declined sharply, Trump’s support remains relatively strong with white evangelicals, according to polls.

Some conservative evangelical leaders have long mixed faith and politics, criticizing Joe Biden for how he practiced his Catholicism, questioning whether Hillary Clinton was really a practicing Methodist, and attacking Barack Obama’s sincerity as a Christian (Jeffress once said Obama was "paving the way for the future reign of the Antichrist”).

Other pastors feel newly empowered by the Trump administration’s moves to loosen restrictions on tax-exempt churches engaging in partisan political activity.

Joseph Hall, lead pastor of Crossroads Church in Council Bluffs, Iowa, said that for weeks he has dedicated a portion of his Sunday sermon to talking about the midterms: “I am promoting ‘vote red.’ Most every Republican stands closer to our beliefs and people of faith’s lifestyle than obviously the Democrats.”

Jeffress, a longtime Trump booster, says he will not be explicitly endorsing Republican candidates this cycle. He argues his dalliance into the election is a moral engagement, not a political one.

“As a pastor, I'm reacting to what James Talarico is saying about his faith more than his politics,” Jeffress said. “Look, James Talarico's religious beliefs don't disqualify him from being a senator, but they do disqualify him from being a Christian pastor, or even a Christian.”

Only 32% of Americans have high confidence that news orgs will report midterm election results accurately, survey finds

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

About six in 10 Americans said they have at least “some” confidence that news organizations will report the upcoming midterm election results accurately, according to a new national survey. But only 32% said they had “quite a bit” or “a great deal” of confidence that journalists would do the job.

In the survey from Medill Local News Initiative and the Alliance for Trust in Media, 37% said they had either “not too much confidence” or “no confidence at all” that news outlets would accurately report the election results.

The survey was completed by a nationally representative sample of 1,057 U.S. adults either online or by telephone earlier this month. The findings echo other national surveys, including a Gallup survey that showed trust in media to report “fully, accurately, and fairly” at 33% — an increase from an all-time low last year.

The Medill/Alliance survey found about half of Americans said they had “not too much confidence” or “no confidence at all” in news outlets to investigate voter fraud claims thoroughly. U.S. President Donald Trump has cast doubt on elections on 70% of days since returning to office — starting with false claims about voter fraud on the first day of his second term.

In another bleak result from the same poll, slightly more survey respondents said journalists manipulate the news for political or commercial purposes than report it accurately. Across the survey, Democrats reported being more confident in (and trusting of) news organizations than Republicans. Nearly 80% of Republicans said journalists try to manipulate the news compared to 38% of independents and 24% of Democrats.

“It is the job of national apparatus groups to go on air early and support Republican nominees,” he said. “We of course are disappointed that more national Republican dollars are not matching the national Democrat dollars being spent on Presidential swing states like Arizona.”

While the RGA has stayed out of the race, the Democratic Governors Association has spent at least $10 million.

Hobbs, who was elected to the purple state’s governorship in 2022, launched her first general election TV ad in May, while Biggs was still focused on his late July primary. At the time, Turning Point mocked Hobbs for "spending like a drunken sailor" by launching spending so early.

But now, Republicans say that Hobbs was able to entrench her lead in the race by spending early while Biggs was dark on the airwaves — and blame Turning Point along with other GOP outside groups like the Republican Governors Association for not spending earlier to keep the race competitive. The RGA declined to comment on their decision not to spend in the race.

In a state like Arizona, where an incumbent governor hasn’t lost reelection since 1966 and some 80% of voters cast ballots before Election Day, a late deluge of cash is less likely to sway the race.

“If you're spending that much money, a big portion of that would've been really helpful earlier in the race when Andy was completely broke, he didn't have two nickels to rub together, and Hobbs was dominating the airwaves with no response and beating the crap out of Andy,” said Salmon.”A lot of us are scratching our heads.”

Biggs’ campaign reported just $1.26 million cash on hand in July, while Hobbs’ campaign reported double that. Many donors to former GOP Gov. Doug Ducey have stayed on the sidelines this year.

One Republican operative working on other Arizona races, granted anonymity to speak candidly, said that at this point, “No amount of spending is going to change the fact that Biggs is going to lose.”

“In all likelihood, the top statewide seats are going Democrat. And Turning Point owns it,” the strategist added. “They wanted to be the player. They wanted to be the guy. Guess what? They are.”

Many local Republicans are frustrated that the RGA never spent a penny on the top race in a state Trump won in 2024. While Biggs was getting hammered early on the airwaves, the RGA was spending nearly $4 million on the Wisconsin Democratic primary to boost a democratic socialist who narrowly lost.

For several cycles, Turning Point has muscled statewide candidates through messy Republican primaries, only to see them falter in November. In 2022, it pushed hardliners Blake Masters and Kari Lake as nominees for U.S. Senate and governor, only to see both lose. In 2024, they backed Lake’s bid for Senate; even as Trump won Arizona by five points, Lake lost by two.

Biggs, some Republicans thought, would be different. He had high name ID statewide and a track record of public service for Arizona, both in the state Senate and the U.S. House. Kirk himself personally urged Biggs to enter the governor’s race, Biggs told the Arizona Republic, even though Trump had already endorsed Karrin Taylor Robson, a Phoenix attorney and businesswoman, early in the race.

After Biggs entered the race in January 2025, Trump split his endorsement between Robson and Biggs — and Turning Point took a victory lap.

“Turning Point Action will marshall our organization's full resources, ground game, and support behind the Biggs for Arizona campaign, and we’re honored to be able to do so,” Charlie Kirk said in a May 2025 statement. “He has my full and complete endorsement.”

Robson later dropped out, and Turning Point helped Biggs romp to a massive victory in the primary. Next Tuesday will test if their general-election acumen has improved.

Jefferson Fisher Has The Secret to Getting Along Better With Absolutely Everyone

📅 Jul 27, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

If being a parent, a husband, a man, a human has taught me anything at all it’s that communication is everything. Also, most of us suck at communication.

For once, our poor communication is not a product of big external forces — neither isolationist politics nor overcooked capitalism nor a failing of modern masculinity. We can only blame ourselves because, hey, communicating is hard. Getting your point across to a group, your nuclear family, or one single person takes practice and discipline, mistakes and learning. I don’t think we ever get it entirely right.

Well, most of us don’t. There are some communicators in this world that are … blessed. Jefferson Fisher is one such individual. Fisher is a Texan, dad, husband, and personal injury attorney who has made a name for himself through social videos that teach us all how to communicate better.

The videos offer pithy lessons like this: Instead of saying I disagree, “tweak it a little and say, I see things differently.” Why? “When you talk in terms of perspective, you find more connection.” Here’s another: “How I talk to my kids will affect how they talk to their kids.”

Simple, pointed, and poignant. You can see why millions tune in.

Fisher’s goal is to make people talk more and argue less. It says just that on the cover of his fantastic, practical, wonderfully written book The Next Conversation — a must read for all of us who strive to get along a little better with those around us.

We caught up with Fisher to talk about his new book and, naturally, anoint him as the latest Cool Dad. Here are some excerpts from our talk, including extras from the Cool Dad Questionnaire and his advice for the most important conversations you’ll ever have.

What’s one thing you wish your kids had today from your childhood?

Probably more of a reckless abandon about what they ate. I grew up in just every Little Debbie from Cosmic Brownies to ... everything you could think of that was probably terrible for us, but we didn't know any different.

What does your partner love about you?

I mean, this is my guess ... I would say what Sierra loves about me is my steadiness. When things don't go to plan, I'm pretty rock steady. So when there is something unexpected or the kids go into what we call their midnight madness, when it's right there at bedtime and they're absolutely losing their minds, that's when I'm able to step in and I think I'm able to really notice when she's overwhelmed, and take over.

And what do you think they maybe find annoying about you?

I mean, there's lots. I'm trying to find a really good one like she finds the most annoying about me is sometimes I can be a little bit occasionally of like a party-pooper where the kids are just ... all going nuts and I'm like, "Can we just not yell so much in our house?" So there's that.

Probably the bigger thing she gets more annoyed about is my relationship with money. Its just money. So I'm the spender and so she is the saver. If it's something that's like, "Hey, but this would be fun, even though it's not something we exactly need right now, but it would be a cool memory," I think there's a lot of that.

“Fatherhood... is a daily devotion to putting your wife, your spouse, your partner, your kids before you.”

What does fatherhood mean to you in our current world?

Fatherhood, with the caveat of doing it well to be a good father, I think, is knowing it's a discipline and not a destination. It's a daily devotion to putting your wife, your spouse, your partner, your kids before you. And it's this idea of ... are you inconveniencing them for your comfort or is it the opposite, are you willing to inconvenience yourself for their comfort?

There are times where it's going to be uncomfortable. I don't want to be the one carrying all the bags all the time, but is it what my family needs in that moment in the airport? Yeah, that's what they need. Can I do it? Yeah.

I got to say that the carrying bags all the time is a wonderful metaphor.

Yeah. You're the one that is supposed to shoulder the heavy. So you're supposed to carry what they can't. I'm sure there's a similarity to this phrase of to be what others cannot, you must be willing to suffer what others will not, and so it's like that. To be the dad that others can't, you got to be able to do what others won't.

How do you better communicate with your partner in parenting? Do you think there's ways that you, something that don't apply, or do you follow the advice in your book and have great arguments and conversations with your spouse?

I wouldn't say there are things that don't apply. I would say what it would need to go deeper on is the theme of repair specifically. So you could have the mindset of arguments and that's something that's very helpful. There's the idea of saying things with control, meaning be regulated and knowing when you need to take that timeout, and using the pauses. But the turn the book doesn't make with regard to marital relationships is repair.

We are talking about words of affirmation, affirming somebody's feelings, being able to take ownership of your words and what that actually sounds like ... how to truly use words of empathy and affirm how they feel, how to say things that are going to bring. We hit on connection in the book, but more in the way of framing non-relationship conversations.

In repair, there is a lot of something as simple as, "Yes, I said that. Yes, I did that. I absolutely can see how that would have frustrated you or upset you," or "I think it's totally reasonable for you to feel this way. I don't blame you for being upset about that. I could have done that better." And then how to actually, words of apology, how to repair after a rupture, that to me is, that's a whole different ballgame, whole different set of skills.

“There's a difference between [that and] saying, ‘How are you and how's your heart?’”

What do you think about checking in? I'm wondering where that falls in the communication line. It's maybe a little bit of the repair, but is that an important thing to do or how do you feel about just what a felt of unspoken understanding between family?

There's an element that my wife and I do and that's check-ins and check-outs. I mean, it's what we call them, things that we do in the morning and things that we do in the evening. Otherwise, there are times you may never talk to your spouse in the day and you know what that's like. Before you know it, you're just two people, somehow there's a woman who lives with me. I don't know who she is, but she's here and you can be roommates that way if you're not checking in with each other. The other is that unspokenness of knowing when something is wrong and that you feel like you might be approaching things from a different vantage. I like asking the phrase, "What's coming up?" "What's coming up for you?"

Instead of me saying, "What's wrong with you" or even, "What's up with you?" That tends to get a pretty defensive response. But if I say something as simple as what's coming up, there's a difference between [that and] saying, "How are you and how's your heart?"
