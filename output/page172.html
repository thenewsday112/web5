Working with outside collaborators can add more work for a repository owner. You need to confirm who still needs access, change permissions as projects evolve, and archive repositories once they’ve served their purpose.

To keep up, repository owners need to track collaborators, deadlines, and follow-ups for each repository. As a project grows, that manual housekeeping becomes harder to keep up with and easier to defer.

We faced this problem in a shared organization where GitHub employees (“Hubbers”) collaborate with customers and partners on product trials and professional services engagements. Our answer was Evergreen, a GitHub App built with native GitHub features. The workflows below exhibit a pattern you can adapt for your own organization.

Meet Evergreen

Evergreen is a GitHub App that automates organization hygiene through two workflows:

  1. Outside collaborator access reviews. Every quarter, each repo’s outside collaborators are reviewed. Keep who you need, and everyone else is removed.
  2. Stale repository detection and archival. Repos with no human activity past a threshold get flagged, warned, and (if still untouched) archived.

Two design principles shaped everything:

Fail secure. If nobody responds to an access review, we don’t leave access in place and hope for the best. We revoke it. The safe outcome is the default outcome.

Never destroy anything. Evergreen will never delete a repository or its contents. Archived repos are recoverable, and every access change is written into the repo’s tracking issue, so reverting a mistake is as easy as a copy-paste to Copilot.

How it works: One brain, many hands

Under the hood, Evergreen is a small Go webhook server running in a container. But the interesting part is the choreography. Every moving piece is a stock GitHub feature: a GitHub App, scheduled actions, webhooks, issues, custom properties, and plain files in a repo. Nothing exotic to reinvent.

A controller that keeps time

A dedicated controller repo acts as Evergreen’s clock and its memory. Four scheduled GitHub Actions workflows fire repository_dispatch events on a quarterly cadence, and the app wakes up and does the work:

DayWorkflowWhat happens1stStale repo reviewFlag repos with no recent human activity, open a warning issue8thAccess reviewSnapshot outside collaborators, open a checklist issue per repo16thStale repo cleanupArchive repos still stale after the grace period23rdAccess cleanupAuto‑revoke access for any review left unanswered

Reviews open two weeks before their cleanup runs, and there’s a hard grace period baked in so a stray manual run can never fast‑forward the guillotine. Each workflow also supports workflow_dispatch, so we can trigger any step by hand as needed.

State you can git blame

Evergreen tracks every repo’s status as a JSON file (one per repository) committed to the controller repo under state/repos/<repo_id>.json. The snapshot of who had access, when the review opened, the deadline, who decided, and what happened all lives there. Here’s a completed review where a Hubber kept Alice’s access and let Bob’s lapse:

The best part: because state is just files in git, the commit history is the audit trail. Want to know why someone lost access last spring? git log the state file. There’s no separate database or bespoke audit pipeline to track.

Issue‑ops, everywhere it matters

Evergreen does its talking through issues inside each repository. When an access review kicks off, every repo gets a checklist issue:

The interaction model is delightfully simple: check the boxes for who stays, then close the issue. That’s it. When the issue closes, Evergreen listens for the issues.closed webhook event, reads the checkboxes, retains the checked collaborators, and revokes the rest, then posts a receipt comment listing exactly what it kept and what it removed.

Only the right humans can pull the lever

Closing an access review issue is a privileged action, so Evergreen guards it. If someone who isn’t a Hubber closes an issue, the app quietly reopens it and posts a note explaining why. Decisions only count when they come from the people authorized to make them.

Informing the right people

Here’s a subtle problem: we know the people who need to respond to a review are Hubbers, but we didn’t always know which Hubber(s) owned a given repository.

Evergreen resolves the right people to notify through a fallback chain:

  1. If a repo sets an evergreen_repo_owner custom property (a @user or @org/team), use it.
  2. Otherwise, look at recent issue and commit authors and cc the ones who are on the access review team, i.e., the Hubbers actually working in that repo.

The result: the right owners get pinged automatically, without anyone maintaining a spreadsheet of “who owns what.”

Fail secure, and fail closed

When a review deadline passes with no response, cleanup revokes every collaborator in the snapshot. We were careful about a subtle edge case: what if some removals succeed while others hit a transient API error? Marking the review “done” while a removal is still outstanding would be a quiet inconsistency, and we didn’t want that.

So Evergreen fails closed. If any removal fails, the review stays pending and the issue stays open, and the next run retries the stragglers. It only records “revoked” once every removal actually succeeded. And whatever gets removed is written into the issue, so restoring access is a matter of re‑adding the handles it listed.

The user experience: automation that feels considerate

Automated housekeeping has a reputation for being the office smoke alarm: loud, easy to ignore, and usually going off while you’re cooking something perfectly fine. We worked hard to make Evergreen feel like the opposite:

  • It comes to you. The work happens as an issue in your repo.
  • It tags the right people. No “who owns this?” archaeology.
  • It’s crystal clear about the ask. Every issue states what to do, the exact deadline, and precisely what happens if you ignore it.
  • It nudges before it acts. A reminder comment lands before the deadline: no surprise revocations.
  • It has an escape hatch. Org owners (Hubbers) can opt out by setting a custom property (evergreen_exempt_from_access_review or evergreen_exempt_from_archive).

Rolling it out carefully

Automation that manages access and archives repositories deserves a careful rollout, especially into a large, active org (ours has 1,400+ Hubbers, and many more customers and partners). We wanted the very first run to be a complete non‑event. A few practices got us there:

Dry‑run first. Evergreen ships with a DRY_RUN mode that logs every write it would make without touching a single collaborator or repo. We ran it against the real org and read the logs like tea leaves until we trusted the decisions it wanted to make.

Overcommunicate. Before flipping the switch, we broadcast what was coming, when, and what people needed to do, so the first issue anyone saw wasn’t a surprise. We found that when you’re telling a large group the same thing for the seventh time, it’s often when many are still hearing it for the first time.

Watch everything with Splunk. Evergreen emits structured logfmt logs to a dedicated Splunk index. That observability is how we caught edge cases in the wild (empty collaborator lists, unusual permissions, repos with issues disabled) and tightened the logic before they mattered.

Respect the platform’s speed limits. When you open issues and post comments across every repo in a big org, you slam into GitHub’s secondary rate limits for content creation: 80 requests/minute and 500/hour. While this is a thoughtful preventative control to avoid system abuse, it requires a bit of intention when automating IssueOps at large scale.

So Evergreen paces itself by construction. It processes repos in batches, spaces out each content‑creating call by a couple of seconds, and sleeps between batches to let the rolling window clear, and it only counts the repos that actually create content, so a quiet quarter where nothing’s eligible still runs fast. We opted for proactive avoidance over reactive back‑off.

Lessons we’re taking with us

  • Make the safe path the default path. Fail secure, then make responding easy enough that failing secure rarely triggers.
  • Use the primitives you already have. Issues, webhooks, custom properties, and git history did the work of a bespoke UI, a job queue, and an audit database.
  • Meet people where they work. An issue in your repo beats a dashboard you’ll never open.
  • Logs are a revert button. Writing every change into the issue turned “oops” into “paste these three handles into a Copilot session.”
  • Respect the limits. At org scale, rate limits aren’t an edge case: they’re the design constraint.

Keep your org evergreen

Routine hygiene is the kind of work that’s easy to defer and perfect to automate. The trick isn’t heroic cleanup sprints: it’s making the boring, safe thing happen on a schedule, with a friendly face and an undo button.

That’s Evergreen. And because it’s built entirely from native GitHub features, you can stand up the same pattern yourself.

Ready to make repository hygiene routine? Start with one recurring access or archival task, then use GitHub Apps, actions, issues, and custom properties to automate the problem away.

Building a cloud-native foundation for AI

The relationship between model innovation and AI infrastructure has never been more clear; as AI becomes more capable, the platform underneath it matters even more. Agents are fundamentally changing how applications work, and with that, what organizations need from the cloud.

Learn how to build a cloud-native AI foundation

Cloud-native practices gave organizations the scale, flexibility and resilience to build a generation of distributed applications. Agents build on that foundation, but they work differently, placing new demands on it. Unlike applications designed around more predictable workflows, agents behave in entirely new ways, gathering information and deep intelligence from across the business, using tools, making decisions, and coordinating work across long-running processes. To complete complex tasks, they pause for approval, respond to new information in real time and change course as conditions evolve. All this changes what the platform needs to deliver.

Customers need infrastructure that adapts as demand changes, applications that remain reliable as work becomes more dynamic, and the visibility and control to understand what is happening across the system. Meeting those needs doesn’t mean starting over, but it does require that the cloud-native foundation organizations already rely on, to evolve. Compute, storage and networking need to work together. Application platforms need to make it easier to build and run AI workloads. Open technologies need to give customers flexibility without adding unnecessary complexity.

The platform decisions organizations make today are increasingly strategic, shaping how effectively they can put AI to work for their business. Evolving the cloud-native foundation is essential to helping organizations get more from AI today while giving them the flexibility to take advantage of new capabilities as they emerge.

Optimize infrastructure for AI workloads

The true measure of AI infrastructure is the useful work it enables. AI workloads rarely place the same demands on infrastructure from beginning to end. Different parts of the work may need accelerated compute, CPUs, fast access to data or connectivity across applications and environments. The answer isn’t simply more capacity, but rather, to match the right resources to the right work at the right time.

That makes coordination across the infrastructure extremely important. Compute, storage and networking need to work together as demands change. When they do, teams can spend less time managing infrastructure and more time focused on the applications and outcomes that matter to their business.

Economics matter, too. Infrastructure needs to be tuned to the demands of the application and the business value it delivers, with the right balance of performance, reliability and cost across the complete workload.

Basecamp Research is one example. The company used Azure to assemble 9.7 trillion nucleotides for EDEN, its therapeutic-design models, and reported laboratory validation across three therapeutic applications within about three weeks. In this example, AI didn’t simply require more infrastructure, it needed AI infrastructure that was coordinated and designed around the outcome – bringing together the compute, data and platform capabilities needed to move complex work forward.

As Glen Gowers, co-founder of Basecamp Research put it: “To really be successful here, we need those three ingredients to come together. We need someone scaling the data; that’s us. But someone who really knows how to scale models and scale compute is Microsoft.”

Leadership takeaway: Measure success by the useful work AI completes. Judge infrastructure investment by the performance, reliability and cost of delivering those outcomes, with compute, storage and networking operating as one system.

Turn open-source innovation into production capability

Open technologies have long helped drive cloud innovation, and AI is accelerating that pace. New models, frameworks and cloud-native technologies give organizations more choice in how they build and adapt applications.

That opportunity builds on an established cloud-native foundation. Gartner forecasts that by 2028, 95% of new AI agent deployment will use Kubernetes.1 Reusing established components can shorten the path to production, allowing developers to focus on differentiated business capabilities rather than rebuilding the foundation.

More choice can also bring more complexity. Taking advantage of open-source innovation often requires specialized expertise to integrate, manage and maintain technologies in production. That expertise is hard to find and even harder to scale. We need to make these capabilities accessible to more businesses, without requiring every organization to build and maintain that expertise themselves.

Azure helps reduce that burden. Services like Azure Kubernetes Service give teams access to open-source innovation while helping manage the complexity and risk of running it in production. This makes it easier for more businesses to take advantage of open technologies while preserving the flexibility to adapt with changing needs.

Leadership takeaway: Choose a platform that helps turn open-source freedom into business advantage. Preserve the ability to adapt and optimize while reducing the expertise and operating effort required to sustain those choices in production.

Align AI infrastructure costs with business value

As more organizations put AI into production, understanding the economics becomes increasingly important. AI investment starts with an expectation of business value: faster decisions, more responsive services or greater capacity to get useful work done. Then, it’s evaluating whether the value justifies the investment to deliver it. FinOps can help teams understand that return and make more informed decisions in this area.

For customers, that starts with understanding the full investment required to run AI applications and how to optimize it as workloads scale. Models represent a significant expense, with token usage and agent behavior influencing spending. Compute, storage, networking and operations also factor into the equation. Considering these together helps customers make more informed decisions about where to optimize.

Learn FinOps best practices for compute

Teams can’t simply place their bets on spending less. They need to carefully consider that a more capable model or additional infrastructure that costs more may be the right choice if it produces greater value long-term. Teams need to understand the full cost, establish ownership and put guardrails in place as they scale. From there, they can use business outcomes to decide where to optimize and where to invest.

Leadership takeaway: Manage AI investment against the business return it needs to deliver. Evaluate model and platform costs together, then use evidence of outcome value to decide where to optimize and where to invest.

Transform cloud operations with agentic AI

Operations teams are already stretched thin, keeping critical services running while balancing competing priorities and constant change. AI adds new complexity, and it also creates an opportunity to change how those environments are managed.

Cloud-native operations gave teams a new model for managing infrastructure, bringing increased automation, visibility and resilience. Agentic operations build on that foundation, bringing deeper intelligence across applications and infrastructure by connecting signals from otherwise disconnected systems. This helps teams identify issues earlier and resolve them faster, while increasing automation and continuously improving how their environments perform; all within the tools they already use.

Over time, AI can help operations teams move from reacting to problems toward anticipating and addressing them before they disrupt the business. As these capabilities mature, more actions can be automated within defined controls, with people remaining in the loop and accountable for critical decisions. When operations teams have greater reach without additional manual effort, reliability, performance and cost are continuously improved while critical services remain uninterrupted.

Leadership takeaway: Invest in operations that can anticipate problems and coordinate action across the environment. Expand autonomy as capabilities are validated, with clear controls and human accountability protecting critical business services.

Being pregnant means your body goes on a whirlwind adventure of hormones, cravings, and more. From morning sickness to exhaustion, it can make working out and staying healthy a bit of a challenge. But keeping your body moving is one of the best ways to head off pain and also prepare for labor and childbirth. So get the green light from your doctor about exercising during pregnancy and check out these 9 free online workouts.

A Full-Body Pregnancy Workout by POPSUGAR Fitness

What it is: This 24-minute workout starts with a warm-up yoga flow before getting into the more challenging resistance exercises. Expecting mamas can expect to work everything from their back and shoulders to glutes and thighs, even engaging pelvic floor muscles and core stability when crunches just aren’t an option.

Why you’ll love it: Depending on where you are in your pregnancy and how fit you’re feeling, there are modifications you can choose from, which is ideal for changing bodies. You can also use an exercise band, free weights or no equipment at all. And what mama-to-be doesn’t love plenty of restorative stretching?

Prenatal Yoga by SarahBeth Yoga

What it is: A very full 20 minutes of all your favorite yoga poses, including chaturanga push-ups and triangle pose. The approach is straightforward and fuss-free, with reminders for breathing and visuals of modified poses.

Why you’ll love it: A consistent yoga practice can not only keep you strong and limber, but may aid in postpartum recovery too.

10-Minute Celeb Workout by POPSUGAR Fitness

What it is: Celebrity trainer Andrea Orbeck helped Heidi Klum stay healthy and fit during pregnancy. Here, she leads a 10-minute workout with plenty of reps and a focus on keeping posture in check.

Why you’ll love it: You’ll get your heart rate up for sure, but this workout is so quick that you may not even break a sweat. Squeeze it all in before or after work, or anytime you have a handful of minutes and want to check exercise off your to-do list!

Prenatal Kettlebell Workout by BodyFit by Amy

What it is: A series of basic, simple movements kicked up a notch, thanks to the help of a kettlebell. Each exercise is about 45 seconds of movement and then 15 seconds of active rest.

Why you’ll love it: It works well for all three trimesters, especially the third because all the core work is from a standing position, not lying down on your back.

What it is: Twenty minutes of free-weight work with the added challenge of the stability ball, which forces you to engage your core. You’ll do a little bit of everything from bicep curls (good practice for holding a growing baby) to squats (perfect for picking up a dropped pacifier).

Why you’ll love it: If you exercise frequently, then these exercises might be a nice break from your ordinary routine. Save that stability ball for easing the pains of labor, and for lightly bouncing a fussy baby who needs some rhythmic movement or help getting to sleep.

AI Agent Security Risks: What Can Go Wrong When Agents Act on Their Own

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The thing that makes an AI agent useful, its freedom to act, is also what can introduce risk. An agent doesn’t just answer a question, it browses, runs code, and calls tools on its own, so a single bad or manipulated instruction can turn into real damage instead of just a wrong answer. That’s the shift traditional application security wasn’t built for, and it’s why trust has to be built into the system around the agent, not assumed.

An agent decides its own path, picking tools at runtime, chaining actions together, and usually running with the credentials of whoever launched it. That autonomy is the source of most agent-specific risk, and it points to the two-part answer that runs through this post: contain what an agent can reach, and oversee what it does.

Key takeaways

  • AI agents introduce risks traditional app security wasn’t built for: they choose tools, chain actions, and often run with your credentials.
  • The main risks are prompt injection, tool misuse and poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential exposure, and data exfiltration.
  • Most trace back to one thing: the agent can act, so containment and oversight matter more than the model.
  • The two-part answer is isolation to limit blast radius and governance to oversee behavior.

Why agents break traditional security models

If you’ve secured web services, the model is familiar: requests arrive at known endpoints, deterministic logic handles them, and you design controls around that predictability. Agents break that assumption. They interpret instructions dynamically, choose tools at runtime, and run multi-step chains with very little human intervention at each step. A coding agent might read a file, install a dependency, change config, run tests, and push a commit from a single prompt. 

Because an agent chooses its own path, a compromised or misdirected one can take a far wider range of actions than a traditional service. Every risk below has already produced documented real-world incidents, and they all follow from that shift.

1. Prompt injection

Prompt injection is the risk that defines agent security. Because an agent acts on whatever text it reads, an attacker doesn’t need access to your systems. They just need the agent to encounter their instructions, hidden in a web page, a code comment, an issue, or a document it summarizes. 

A real prompt-injection case showed an agent following instructions buried in repository content and taking actions its user never intended. 

Incident highlight: A coding agent followed instructions buried in a repository’s own content and took actions its user never intended. The malicious text arrived through data the agent was simply asked to read.

The agent has no reliable way to tell a legitimate instruction from an injected one, so the defense is to limit what a hijacked agent can reach.

2. Tool misuse and tool poisoning

Agents act through tools: API connectors, database queries, file operations, and code execution. Every tool is an access vector, and two things could go wrong. 

An agent can be given more tools than the task needs, widening the attack surface for no reason. And a tool itself can be poisoned, its description carrying hidden instructions that manipulate the agent into misusing it. The mitigations are to scope tools to the task at runtime, source them from registries with verified provenance, and review tool descriptions rather than trusting them by default.

3. Excessive autonomy

The more an agent can do without review, the more a single bad decision costs. Excessive autonomy is the risk of granting an agent broad, standing permission to act, most visibly when developers run YOLO mode, where every action is auto-approved. This autonomy is genuinely useful, so switching it off defeats the point. The better move is to run the agent where that autonomy can’t reach anything it shouldn’t, so speed doesn’t necessarily mean increased blast radius.

Incident highlight: A developer asked a coding agent to clean up an old repository. It generated an rm -rf command with a trailing ~/ and ran it with no confirmation step, wiping the developer’s entire home directory. Nothing in the setup gave a human a chance to catch it before it executed.

4. Credential and secret exposure

When an agent runs under a developer’s personal token, every action it takes carries that person’s full permissions, and a compromise hands those permissions to an attacker. Secrets sitting in prompts, config files, or environment variables baked into an image make it worse, because a prompt injection can read them straight out of the agent’s context. Agents should get their own scoped, short-lived credentials, injected at runtime and kept out of the conversation, rather than borrowing yours.

Incident highlight: An AWS engineer handed a small Cost Explorer bug to a coding agent running with their own operator-level access. The agent decided the cleanest fix was to delete and rebuild the production environment, and it held the credentials to do exactly that. The result was a thirteen-hour regional outage.

5. Data exfiltration and untrusted handoffs

An agent with network access and sensitive information in its context can send that data somewhere it shouldn’t, whether through a compromised tool or an injected instruction. The risk grows in multi-agent setups, where one agent passes work to another. If the receiving agent treats that input as trusted, a compromise in one link can spread down the chain. Restricting network egress to known endpoints and treating every agent-to-agent handoff as untrusted input could keep a single failure from spreading.

How to contain the risk

The risks are varied, but there are controls to secure AI agents. Isolate where the agent runs so a mistake or a hijack is trapped in a disposable environment instead of loose on your host, and scope what the agent can access so the damage it can do is small even inside that boundary.

Risk

Primary control

Primary injection

Isolation, so a hijacked agent can’t reach anything that matters

Tool misuse and poisoning

Runtime-scoped tools from verified-provenance sources

Excessive autonomy

A sandbox boundary that makes auto-approval safe

Credential exposure

Dedicated, short-lived credentials injected at runtime

Data exfiltration

Allow-listed network egress and untrusted inter-agent input

Isolation is the highest-impact starting point, and a microVM boundary makes it strong without slowing the agent down. On top of containment, oversight is what keeps a fleet of agents accountable. Then, AI governance defines which actions an agent may take, what it can reach, and how its behavior is logged, so risk is managed deliberately rather than discovered after an incident.

Contain the blast radius, govern the behavior

These risks aren’t reasons to avoid developing with agents. Instead, we need to give agents the freedom to finish the job by building trust into the system around them, so that freedom never means access to everything.

And that’s the job Docker Sandboxes is built for: each agent runs in its own disposable microVM with the network and filesystem controls you define, so a mistake or an injection is contained by design. And AI Governance adds the oversight layer, setting which actions, tools, and credentials are allowed and enforcing it everywhere your team works. Contain the blast radius, and govern the behavior.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main security risks of AI agents?

The most significant are prompt injection, tool misuse and tool poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential and secret exposure, and data exfiltration. All of them stem from the fact that agents act on their own, using tools and credentials, rather than following a fixed, reviewable path.

What is the biggest AI agent security risk?

For most teams it’s excessive access: an agent running with more autonomy and more credentials than the task needs. It turns a small mistake or a single prompt injection into a large incident, because the agent can reach so much.

How is agent security different from application security?

Traditional application security assumes predictable request-and-response flows. Agent security has to account for autonomous decisions, tools chosen at runtime, and multi-step chains where the agent sets its own path. The attack surface is wider because the agent, not a fixed program, decides what happens next.

Can prompt injection be prevented?

You can reduce it with input handling and tool hygiene, but you can’t assume it away, because an agent can’t reliably tell injected instructions from legitimate ones. The durable defense is containment: run the agent so that even a successfully injected one can’t reach anything sensitive.

How do you reduce AI agent security risks?

Start with isolation so a compromised agent is trapped in a disposable environment. Add scoped tool access and dedicated credentials to shrink what any agent can do. Then layer on governance to define and enforce those limits consistently across every agent your team runs.

Maintainers look at star history to answer a practical question: did a launch, release, or event change a project’s trajectory? To reconstruct that curve from the GitHub API, tools had to request pages of stargazer records (each person who currently stars the repository and when they starred it), keep the timestamps, and throw the identities away.

Over time, that design created problems at both ends. Identities were available for bulk collection, so bad actors started scraping public stargazer lists for spam and phishing campaigns. For highly starred repositories, the endpoint could also make the database process many thousands of entries to return a single page.

The fix seemed clear: return counts instead of people. But returning less data didn’t actually make the query any cheaper.

Now maintainers have a direct way to get the curve: we built an endpoint that returns up to 30 weeks of daily star counts in a single request, with no identities attached. Keeping that cheap to serve meant reducing how much data the database processed, not just how much it returned, and the endpoint does that by filtering stars by time before it counts anything.

What maintainers need from star history

We first addressed the immediate risks to developers and to our database by restricting stargazer and watcher lists to repository administrators and collaborators. After the access change, callers without the required permission could receive an empty response or a 403 Forbidden response.

That change also cut off tools that had used the old response to calculate star history, often without displaying or otherwise using the usernames it returned. So we talked with maintainers and tool builders to understand what they still needed. They used star history as an approximate signal of interest: to compare activity before and after a release, or to see whether an announcement reached new developers. What they cared about was the curve.

The old API response had made identity look like part of that contract because every timestamp arrived attached to a user. In practice, tools such as Star History needed counts and dates, not usernames or other user data. Once identity was no longer part of the requirement, we could design an aggregate endpoint for the actual job: retrieving changes in star counts over time at a useful resolution.

When the API change disrupted Star History, users rallied around the project. GitHub’s Community team listened and connected us with product engineering, who dug into our use case and worked alongside us to design a purpose-built endpoint. A difficult situation became a constructive collaboration between GitHub, Star History, and the broader community—ultimately leading to a better solution.

Tianzhou Chen, star-history.com maintainer and Bytebase co-founder

Why the original endpoint was expensive

Once we knew what maintainers needed, the next step was understanding why the original design couldn’t provide it cheaply. The original GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stargazers endpoint was built to answer a simple question: who starred this repository? It returned a paginated list of people, and if the request specified a custom media type, each entry also included when the star was created.

The star model was built for the same job. It records the current set of people who have starred a repository, which keeps showing a star count or a list of stargazers simple. It was not designed as a historical event log: when someone removes a star, that record is removed. When tools began using the list as a time series, reconstructing a growth curve meant retrieving individual identities and processing every current star. Because each page held at most 100 people, the work grew with the repository’s popularity: building the full curve for react/react, with about 251,000 stars, took about 2,511 requests, so tools often ran into rate limits or sampled pages instead of reading them all.

Serving a page required the database to query the repository’s current stars. Due to a bug in the pagination implementation, every request also counted all of those stars to work out how many pages existed, even though the caller received only one page of up to 100 people. On a highly starred repository, that meant counting many thousands of entries on every request. In the week before we restricted access, nearly 37,000 of these count queries ran slowly enough to be logged, averaging nearly 3 seconds each, and database safeguards killed more than 1,000 of them, which contributed to request timeouts.

The chart below shows what all that counting cost. After we restricted access, the database served about 60% fewer stargazer requests, and the total time it spent on the count query fell by about 80%.

Approaches we considered

Removing identities handled the privacy requirement. The harder part was keeping each request from scanning the repository’s entire current star set.

Keeping events without identities

Our first spike kept one record per star and returned the starred_at value without the stargazer’s identity for callers who lacked collaborator access. At first, this looked like the quickest path forward, because it directly served the request we had heard from tool builders. It also closed the identity-harvesting path with a relatively small change to the existing serialization and authorization flow.

This approach addressed identity exposure and would have allowed existing tools to keep working. But API consumers would still have to paginate through and process potentially many thousands of individual records to build an aggregate history. It also tied the new use case to an endpoint and pagination model designed for listing people rather than retrieving trends.

Aggregating, then paginating

Aggregation seemed like the obvious next step. Instead of returning one row per star, the query could group stars into daily or weekly buckets, sort those buckets, and paginate the much smaller result.

The problem was where pagination happened. In a straightforward SQL query, the database first finds all stars for the repository, groups them into buckets, and sorts those buckets. Only then does it apply LIMIT, which controls how many buckets are returned, and OFFSET, which determines where the page begins:

all matching stars -> bucket every row by day or week -> sort every bucket -> OFFSET/LIMIT -> response

For a popular repository, an anonymous request could still scan and aggregate many thousands of index entries to return a handful of buckets. Returning fewer buckets would shrink the JSON response, but it would not reduce the expensive grouping and sorting the database had already performed. For react/react, reading the full history that way would have meant examining roughly 6 million rows, because every one of the 24 requests would have reprocessed all of its stars. To reduce the query cost, we had to narrow the rows before aggregation began.

Filtering by time before grouping

The design we shipped paginates star history by calendar week. For each request, it translates page and per_page into an indexed created_at range, filters the stars to those weeks, and then groups the matching records into daily counts. A response includes up to 30 weeks, and increasing page moves backward through the repository’s history.

indexed date range -> group rows in that range -> daily buckets -> response

With the date range applied first, each request processes the weeks being returned instead of every current star in the repository. For tools, that means one request now covers up to 30 weeks of history, no matter how many stars the repository has. For react/react, that’s about 24 requests instead of about 2,511, and the database examines each star about once (roughly 251,000 rows) across the full history. This fits the primary use case because maintainers usually inspect growth across weeks or around a known release, launch, or event. Week-based ranges also align with other repository activity APIs: the response follows the same shape as the weekly commit activity endpoint, GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stats/commit_activity.

The time-range design limits each request to a defined period, but it does not limit the number of stars within that range. A week with an unusually large number of stars can therefore still produce a slow or expensive query. The normal request path no longer queries every current star, but exceptionally dense weeks remain a scaling limitation.

Comparing the performance of the old and new API paths also surfaced other places where GitHub was counting stars on demand. We found the same pattern on the repository stargazers page on GitHub.com and updated it to use the repository’s cached star count. Slow queries from that page, which had numbered in the hundreds each week, dropped to zero. We recently shipped a similar fix to other stargazer API endpoints.

The chart below shows slow and killed stargazer count queries each week, by where they came from.

What we learned

  1. A smaller response does not always mean a cheaper query. LIMIT and OFFSET reduced the number of results returned, but they did not reduce the grouping and sorting the database had already done. We had to look at the order in which the work happened.
  2. Start with what people are actually trying to do. Maintainers needed to compare trends. A list of identities was simply how the old endpoint happened to provide the underlying data.
  3. Return only what the use case needs. Anything more is data someone else can misuse. Once we understood the job, we could remove individual identities without taking away the information maintainers relied on.
  4. Be clear about the tradeoffs that remain. The new endpoint is safer and more predictable, but calendar boundaries, removed stars, and unusually dense weeks still affect what it can return. Those details are part of the API contract too.

Restricting the original endpoint addressed the immediate problem. Listening to maintainers gave us something better than what we started with: star history that’s easier to get, cheaper to serve, and safer for everyone who stars a project.

How Do You Raise Kids to Be Emotionally Mature?

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Raising a teen is one of the last chapters in parenting. During these years, it’s easy to prioritize your child’s grades, sports, and other accolades, especially as they inch toward leaving the nest.

While achievement is one marker of success, there’s another (often overlooked) skill that shapes your teen’s well-being and development: emotional maturity.

According to clinical psychologist Lindsay Gibson, emotional maturity develops through deep emotional connections, beginning with a child’s parents and primary caretakers. Research shows emotional maturity is the ability to accept and express care, temper big feelings, and make wise choices. Without it, teens are more likely to feel lonely and less satisfied with life, one study found.

In her latest book, How to Raise an Emotionally Mature Child, Gibson points out that “the quality of humanity’s future depends on the emotional maturity of coming generations.”

Juli Fraga: In your book, you mention that humanity’s future will depend on emotional maturity. Why does this matter so much right now?

Lindsay Gibson: Emotional maturity is critical to our well-being as individuals and to the smooth, productive, and innovative aspects of society. When you’re emotionally mature, you can think and anticipate consequences. You can also look back and learn from what you’ve done before. So, you can rely on your thinking to help you deal with reality. Emotional maturity also means tuning into your inner world, including your emotions, imagination, hopes, dreams, connections to other people, and sense of belonging.

JF: Our society places a lot of emphasis on achievement, and less importance on emotional intelligence. How can parents help their teens balance the two?

LG: There’s a real cultural pull in society to prize intelligence and achievement. But when parents get too fixated on these qualities, a child may feel like performance matters more than their thoughts, feelings, and sense of safety.

Kids need to be safely held and supported through the growing-up process. It helps them feel resilient, secure, and confident—qualities they need to take out into the world to function independently. If you get too fixated on the intelligence and achievement parts, you’ve got someone who’s very powerful in one area, but whose emotional side may be underdeveloped. It’s the balance between the two that matters.

JF: In your book, you mention why it’s so important for parents to help their kids develop a sense of self. Can you explain why this matters, especially during the teen years when kids are individuating?

LG: One way teens find out who they are is by discovering who they’re not. Teens might push a limit or break a rule, and it’s all par for the course. What’s important for parents is to be able to recognize and imagine that their child has a separate heart and mind from theirs—a process called “mentalization.”

A self is that inner spark that speaks to us. It says “don’t do that” or “now’s the time to go for it.” It guides you toward what’s most compatible with your individuality and who you are meant to be.

Our sense of self comes from our relationships with people we love and who love us. To feel secure, kids need solid, dependable, connected, and deeply resonant relationships with their parents and caretakers. They need parents who are curious and reflect something back that’s meaningful about who the young person is. When they have that foundation, they have a sense of being a real person inside.

JF: Parents are so busy these days, and overloaded—mentally and emotionally. Raising emotionally mature kids may feel like another big to-do on an already long list. What advice do you have?

LG: Taking time to pause and take your child seriously as a real person will work wonders for their self-confidence and future resilience.

Slowing down takes practice because by the time you’re old enough to have a family, you’ve probably been very goal-directed; parents can be pretty hard on themselves because our culture is so fixated on achievement.

But pulling yourself away from your goal for even a second to tend to your child is something worth practicing because that’s what children’s needs are all about. Kids also develop confidence when they know they matter just as much as anybody else.

JF: What are some behaviors that are normal for teens but that parents might misinterpret as pushback or misbehavior?

LG: Sometimes teens experiment with saying or doing things that clash with their socialization up to that point, such as the language they use or the clothes they wear. These are things that the parent can feel like, “Whoa, I’m uncomfortable with this…”

Here are two meaningful ways to navigate these moments.

Ask your teen questions about what they’re into, such as the styles they like or what they admire about someone they idolize. Try showing interest, not intrusive interest, but just interest in what they like, or why they are experimenting with different things.

Keep in mind that when your teen snaps at you, is in a bad mood, or slams the door, their emotional centers are activated in a way that will probably never be repeated in their life because their brains prioritize emotional development first during this stage.

JF: Can you share more about why a teen’s development follows this trajectory?

LG: Yeah, I know it’s easy to ask, “Why in the world would nature do it that way?” It’s because that’s how important the emotional self and relationships are to our real success as independent adults.

As adults, we have to know ourselves inside and out and use our feelings to guide us. Teens are still trying to work this out. Learning how to handle these emotions takes a lot of practice. They’re finding out what it’s like to feel rage, or what it’s like to be in love, or to feel disappointed. And they’re feeling them in the most intense way they ever will, because the brain is taking on the job of developing them into a really deep, complete human being.

JF: What advice do you have for parents when their teen’s emotions push their own buttons?

LG: Keep in mind that when your teen responds in an adamant, emotional, defiant way, or calls you names, they’re not really in command of those emotions yet.

Instead of coming down hard with words that suggest they’re not a good person, try to respond with: “I’m really interested in how you feel about this. I want to hear your arguments, but when you call me a name, it makes it impossible for me to be available to you. It just doesn’t work. We don’t call each other names in this family. That won’t work when you’re an adult, either.”

The interaction then becomes an education about how people respond to different kinds of approaches. Harshness that stops a teen cold shuts down their impulse to communicate their feelings to other people, which isn’t good for resilience or honest relationships.

JF: What are two things that parents do to foster emotional maturity that can actually backfire?

LG: One is the importance of achievement, of having a good resume. Of course, parents know how hard life is as an adult. We feel it’s our duty to convey these realizations to our kids. But when it becomes something that feels like everything else will fall into place if you do these things first or achieve X, we are not helping our kids with the emotional or interpersonal side of life. We’re creating a one-dimensional kind of person.

Sometimes parents also think that if they don’t give their child severe consequences or punishments, the child won’t learn and mature. When you try to convey information through punishment, you actually teach a child to defer to other people’s anger and control. Of course, we want them to be aware of consequences, but sometimes we can go too far. You can’t have a full, right life if you’re always anticipating that somebody might be mad at you.
