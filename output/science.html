Animal life exploded in a burst of evolution, and scientists may have discovered why

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Evolution often unfolds over millions of years, yet many of the major animal groups alive today made their first clear appearance in the fossil record during a remarkable burst of diversification about half a billion years ago.

Scientists call it the Cambrian explosion. In a new review, a team of geologists proposes that changes in the solid Earth set it off.

The chain they describe runs from a supercontinent breaking apart, through mountain belts thousands of miles long, to a magnetic field that weakened to about a tenth of today’s strength.

In the authors’ view, no single change explains the burst, which came from several geological processes acting together and from an unlikely coincidence.

Jinlong Yao, a geologist at Northwest University in Xi’an, China, led the review with six colleagues, among them Guochun Zhao, who also works at the University of Hong Kong.

The team drew on decades of published research rather than new measurements.

Most proposed links between the deep Earth and early life are still untested ideas, the authors acknowledge.

A supercontinent split, and ice spread

Rodinia, a supercontinent that began pulling apart roughly 850 million years ago, is the first link. As it split, enormous volcanic eruptions poured out floods of lava and released carbon dioxide.

Basalt, the dark rock that lava hardened into, then did the opposite. It breaks down in rain faster than most rock, and that breakdown pulls carbon dioxide out of the air.

With less carbon dioxide trapping heat, the planet cooled until ice spread across much of it, a period known as Snowball Earth.

The freeze was hard on life. Ice blocked sunlight, photosynthesis fell, and much of the ocean lost its oxygen.

The authors cite evidence that organisms built from many cells held an advantage over single cells in those conditions.

Meltwater at the edges of the ice may also have kept pockets of oxygen-rich water where complex cells survived.

When the ice melted, an intense greenhouse climate followed.

That swing, according to the researchers’ theory, opened the way for the first large, complex life: soft-bodied organisms of the Ediacaran period, just before the Cambrian.

Mountains fed the oceans

A new supercontinent, Gondwana, came together through a series of continental collisions. Between about 670 million and 440 million years ago, those collisions raised mountain belts.

The longest of these ancient mountain belts is estimated to have stretched about 5,600 miles (9,000 kilometers).

Most of the ranges were in low to middle latitudes, in warm, wet monsoon belts where rock breaks down quickly. Rain wore the mountains down, and rivers carried the debris to the sea along with nutrients such as phosphorus.

In the ocean, those nutrients fed algae and bacteria, which released more oxygen through photosynthesis.

Fast-piling sediment also buried dead organic matter before it could rot and use up that oxygen, so oxygen built up in the oceans and the air.

Seawater chemistry from the time fits that picture.

The ratio of two forms of the element strontium climbs as more continental rock washes into the ocean, and it reached its highest level in Earth’s history during this interval.

Modeling work cited in the review found that more sediment reaching the sea went along with a wider variety of marine animals.

How sinking plates affected climate

Yao’s team also makes the case that plate tectonics took its modern form during the same stretch, with ocean floor and even continental crust sinking deep into the mantle worldwide.

One sign is a mineral that forms only under extreme pressure, found in Cambrian rock in western China. It suggests crust was dragged about 186 miles (300 kilometers) down.

Geologists still argue over that timing. Proposed dates run from more than 2.5 billion years ago to the Cambrian, partly because geologists don’t agree on how to define modern plate tectonics.

Sinking plates carried carbonate rock down with them. Heated deep underground, the carbonate gave off carbon dioxide, which escaped through chains of volcanoes above the plates.

Volcanic carbon dioxide kept Earth from freezing over for good, while weathering mountains kept drawing carbon dioxide back down.

The pairing worked like a thermostat, and the authors argue it gave animals a long, stable window of warm, oxygen-rich seas.

The magnetic field grew very weak

The last piece of the argument comes from Earth’s core. Around the boundary between the Ediacaran and Cambrian periods, the planet’s magnetic field fell to its lowest known strength, roughly a tenth of today’s.

Over most of Earth’s history, the field has reversed direction about two or three times per million years. At that boundary it reversed 20 to 25 times per million years.

The field normally turns charged particles from the Sun away from Earth.

At that strength, its outer edge would have pulled in to less than half its present distance, letting more radiation in and possibly thinning the ozone layer.

The authors suggest that increased ultraviolet radiation may have contributed to the decline of some Ediacaran organisms, although evidence for this connection remains uncertain.

Animals that could burrow, grow shells, or swim away would have had an edge, and that pressure may have sped up the Cambrian explosion. Some researchers disagree with this idea.

Going further, the authors propose that the weak field came from Earth’s solid inner core first forming, and that the core formed because sinking plates had cooled the planet’s interior. Both links are still unproven.

Testing the geological connection

The argument depends on timing. The authors compared when events show up in the rock record and built a chain of causes from that, but they didn’t test any single link directly.

Yao’s team names the weak point itself. Geologists don’t have long, measured records of how much tectonic and volcanic activity took place over time.

Without them, events deep in the Earth are hard to compare with changes at the surface. How those processes were connected, and which mattered most for sea life, is still unsettled.

For now, the proposed connections remain unproven. But the review offers a striking possibility.

The extraordinary diversification of animal life may have depended not only on changes in the oceans and atmosphere, but also on geological forces operating deep beneath Earth’s surface.

The full study was published in the journal Continent and Life Evolution.

Why your brain pays more attention to emotional conversations

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A heated conversation at the next table might seem impossible to ignore.

But new research suggests that emotional words alone may not be enough to draw the brain’s attention away from another conversation.

When people listened to two conversations at once, their brains tracked the ignored conversation no more closely when it involved accidents and harassment than when it involved everyday topics.

The conversation they had chosen to follow was another matter. When it carried emotional weight, their brains followed the rise and fall of the speakers’ voices more closely.

Pyry Heikkinen and colleagues at the University of Helsinki ran the experiment on 31 adults, who listened to scripted conversations spoken in flat voices.

When a voice rises and falls, activity in the listening brain rises and falls with it. From the brain recording alone, the team could estimate how closely the brain followed the rhythm of speech.

“We could predict the rhythm of speech a participant was hearing more accurately from their brain activity alone when the conversation contained emotional meaning,” Heikkinen said.

Two conversations at once

Each volunteer’s brain was scanned at the BioMag Laboratory of Helsinki University Hospital with a device that records the brain’s faint magnetic fields a thousand times a second.

On a screen, two people talked through a six-line exchange lasting about 30 seconds. Through the same speakers, a second pair of voices held an unrelated conversation 10 decibels quieter.

The job was to follow the people on screen and ignore the rest, as a listener does in a crowded room.

After each trial, volunteers answered four true-or-false questions about what the people on screen had said.

The team wrote 128 short conversations in Finnish. The emotional ones were about upsetting things: a tragic accident, a fight, harassment, being shut out of a group, an illness.

The neutral ones covered grocery shopping, a lecture, cooking, lunch, and the weather.

The people in the videos kept their faces still and used no gestures. They spoke without the rise and fall people normally put into bad news, so only the words carried the emotion.

Some conversations were scrambled: the team mixed lines from different exchanges, so a remark about gardening got an answer about shopping.

Emotion sharpens one voice

The brain tracked the conversation on screen far more closely than the one playing underneath it. Researchers are already trying to turn that well-known result into better hearing aids.

The brain also tracked emotional conversations more closely than neutral ones, but only when listeners were paying attention to them.

Emotional content in the ignored conversation did not significantly increase neural tracking.

The upsetting dialogues used shorter, less common words than the everyday ones.

The team checked whether differences in word choice or the amount of information in each story could explain the effect, but neither did.

Memory told a different story. People got 89% of the questions right after a conversation that made sense and 80% after a scrambled one.

Whether the conversation was upsetting or dull didn’t reliably change the score. So the closer tracking of emotional talk didn’t translate into remembering more of it.

Scrambled talk settles later

Next, the team looked at tracking line by line through each conversation. When an emotional conversation made sense, the brain followed it most closely in the early lines and then eased off.

When an emotional conversation jumped from topic to topic, tracking kept climbing and peaked later.

“These findings suggest that the brain handles emotional speech differently depending on how the conversation flows,” Heikkinen said.

“Sudden topic changes may require more work to process, while connected comments help the brain use the previously established context to predict what comes next and thus process speech more efficiently.”

On the team’s reading, the brain puts extra resources into something that matters.

Then it settles into a more efficient mode once it can predict what comes next. A scrambled conversation doesn’t give it that chance.

In earlier studies, co-author Patrik Wikman found that the brain’s tracking of a conversation shifts from one speaking turn to the next. Heikkinen’s team adds that emotion and coherence together set the pace.

Emotion acts at two moments

The brain’s response to each change in a voice came in two waves.

About a tenth to a quarter of a second after the sound changed, emotional content boosted the response, and where it did so depended on how the conversation flowed.

In scrambled conversations, the boost showed up in hearing and language regions along the sides and toward the back of the brain.

In coherent ones, it showed up toward the front, in areas tied to language.

A little later, from about a quarter to just under half a second, emotional content boosted the response along the temporal lobe above the ears.

That held whether the conversation made sense or not. The later wave matches the stage at which the brain is thought to weigh meaning instead of only registering sound.

Wikman is also affiliated with Georgetown University. Co-authors also came from Aalto University and the University of Helsinki’s Faculty of Social Sciences.

Beyond the laboratory

Every emotional conversation in the study involved upsetting, emotionally charged situations between people.

Whether good news or an exciting story sharpens attention the same way is untested.

The voices were flat and the faces still. That left out the tone of voice, the speaker’s face, and the gestures that carry much of the feeling in a real exchange.

And the volunteers only listened. In a real conversation, a person also has to plan a reply while the other person is still talking. Speakers often switch within a tenth to a fifth of a second.

Heikkinen’s team suggested that the closer tracking of emotional speech may help with that rapid back and forth. It may also be part of what makes some conversations feel engaging.

The study didn’t measure engagement, so that is the authors’ reading of the data, not a result.

As a next step, the authors proposed recording people taking part in a conversation instead of overhearing one, free to use their voices and hands.

The findings reveal that emotional meaning can strengthen how the brain processes speech, but only when that conversation already has our attention.

The full study was published in the journal The Journal of Neuroscience.

NASA Seeks Proposals for Reboosting the Hubble Space Telescope

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →
The Hubble Space Telescope "will plunge back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s," reports Ars Technica. So now NASA has released a Request For Information (RFI) "asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service, or a controlled disposal of Hubble." Specifically, NASA is looking for information about companies' current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope's performance, or guiding Hubble to a controlled reentry. "The requirement includes that the boost and/or servicing mission must not harm the HST and that this mission is expected to occur by 2032," NASA wrote in the RFI. The contractor would be responsible for providing the launch vehicle, servicing spacecraft, mission operations, and "crew if applicable." NASA is not committing to buying a Hubble rescue mission, but officials want to know who might be interested in mounting such a mission, how much it would cost, and which companies would have a realistic shot at reaching Hubble before it falls out of orbit and burns up on reentry. Orbital dynamicists currently predict that it will likely happen in 2033 or 2034 without intervention, but reentry forecasts can change based on solar activity... A reboost to NASA's requirement of 373 miles (600 km) would likely keep Hubble in orbit for at least 25 more years. "NASA last raised the orbit of Hubble in 2002," the article points out, "when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited the observatory for a servicing mission." The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope's orbit, and with the retirement of the shuttle fleet two years later, Hubble has steadily lost altitude with no way to stave off eventual destruction... NASA's newer flagship observatories, sometimes described as successors to Hubble, are not actually one-for-one replacements. Hubble is tuned to observe the Universe in visible and ultraviolet light, while the Webb and Roman telescopes peer deeper into the infrared Universe. Astronomers will likely have to wait for the launch of NASA's Habitable Worlds Observatory in the 2040s for an improvement on Hubble's visible and ultraviolet vision.

Why Your Hex Converter Lies to You: Two's Complement, the 53-Bit Wall, and Radix Magic

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Every programmer eventually encounters that moment of quiet confusion during a late-night debugging session: you cast a signed 8-bit -1 to hexadecimal, expecting something clean and human-readable, only for the debugger to spit back 0xFF. Widen it to a 32-bit register, and it suddenly balloons into 0xFFFFFFFF.


Where did the minus sign go? Why didn't it output -0x01? And why does running 0x20000000000001 through your standard JavaScript parser silently yield the wrong integer altogether?


Number base conversion is often treated as a trivial Computer Science 101 exercise—something solved with a simple parseInt(val, 16) or a textbook division-by-radix loop. But beneath high-level abstractions, radix shifts are inextricably linked to hardware registers, two’s complement mathematics, IEEE-754 rounding traps, and data-encoding strategies.


Here is what is actually happening under the hood when bits change their radix, and why understanding these low-level mechanics prevents catastrophic bugs in production.

1. The Illusion of the Minus Sign: How Two's Complement Works

Hardware processors don't have dedicated silicon cells reserved for storing an aesthetic - glyph. In CPU registers, negative numbers are encoded via Two’s Complement.


In an NNN-bit register, the most significant bit (MSB) acts as a negative weight:

Value=−bN−12N−1+∑i=0N−2bi2i\text{Value} = -b_{N-1} 2^{N-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{N-2} b_i 2^iValue=−bN−1​2N−1+i=0∑N−2​bi​2i


When you assign -1 to an 8-bit signed integer (int8_t):

  1. Invert all bits of positive 1 (0000 0001 →\rightarrow→ 1111 1110).
  2. Add 1 (1111 1110 + 0000 0001 = 1111 1111).


In hexadecimal, each character represents an exact 4-bit nibble. Thus, 1111 1111 maps directly to 0xFF.

c #include <stdio.h> #include <stdint.h> int main() { int8_t small_neg = -1; int32_t wide_neg = small_neg; // Sign extension occurs here printf("8-bit hex: 0x%02X\n", (uint8_t)small_neg); // 0xFF printf("32-bit hex: 0x%08X\n", (uint32_t)wide_neg); // 0xFFFFFFFF return 0; }

The Sign Extension Trap

Notice what happened when widening int8_t to int32_t: the single byte 0xFF became 0xFFFFFFFF.


If the hardware merely padded the higher 24 bits with zeros (0x000000FF), the value would evaluate to +255, silently mutating your application state. To preserve the mathematical value of -1, the CPU performs Sign Extension: it duplicates the MSB (which is 1) across all newly allocated upper bits.


Pro-Tip for Packet Inspection:

When inspecting raw network packets or memory dumps, you can instantly determine if a hexadecimal value represents a negative signed integer without converting to decimal: look at the leading character.


Because 8 through F have their highest bit set to 1:

  • 0x8... through 0xF... →\rightarrow→ Negative (in signed contexts)
  • 0x0... through 0x7... →\rightarrow→ Zero or Positive

2. Arithmetic Shift (>>) vs. Logical Shift (>>>): A Subtle Production Hazard

Understanding two’s complement makes the difference between bitwise shift operators crystal clear:

  • Arithmetic Right Shift (>>): Preserves the sign bit. Vacated positions on the left are backfilled with whatever the MSB was.
  • Logical Right Shift (>>>): Agnostic to sign. It unconditionally backfills vacated positions with zeros.
typescript const value = -42; // Binary: 11111111 11111111 11111111 11010110 (in 32-bit) console.log(value >> 1); // Output: -21 (MSB remains 1; arithmetic integrity preserved) console.log(value >>> 1); // Output: 2147483627 (MSB replaced with 0; immediately converts to large unsigned integer)

In telemetry, hashing routines, and protocol deserialization, accidentally using >>> instead of >> (or vice versa) produces bugs that unit tests with small positive integers never catch.

3. The 53-Bit Wall: Why JavaScript Silently Corrupts Large Radixes

Consider this seemingly benign conversion snippet:

javascript const highSeqId = "9007199254740993"; // 2^53 + 1 console.log(Number(highSeqId).toString(16)); // Expected: "20000000000001" // Actual: "20000000000000" (Precision collapsed!)

Standard JavaScript Number types conform to the IEEE-754 double-precision floating-point specification. Double floats allocate:

  • 1 sign bit
  • 11 exponent bits
  • 52 explicit fraction bits (providing 53 bits of precision with the implicit leading 1)


Any integer larger than Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER (253−12^{53} - 1253−1, or 9,007,199,254,740,991) cannot be uniquely represented. When you convert an arbitrary-large hexadecimal hash or a 64-bit Snowflake ID via standard floating-point numbers, the least significant bits get silently rounded to the nearest representable floating-point neighbor.

The Fix: BigInt and Limbs

Arbitrary-precision arithmetic operates outside the Floating-Point Unit (FPU). Instead of relying on a single hardware register, runtimes allocate heap arrays of 64-bit integer chunks (often called machine limbs):

javascript // Lossless 64-bit / 128-bit Radix Transformations const safeVal = BigInt("0x20000000000001"); console.log(safeVal.toString(10)); // "9007199254740993" (Exact) console.log(safeVal.toString(36)); // "2000000000001"

4. Radix 36: The Workhorse of URL Slugs and Distributed IDs

While base-2 (binary), base-8 (octal), and base-16 (hexadecimal) map directly into CPU word architectures because they are powers of two (21,23,242^1, 2^3, 2^421,23,24), higher non-power-of-two radices serve specialized architectural purposes.


Chief among them is Radix 36 (0–9, a–z).

While Base-64 yields the highest raw density, its reliance on uppercase versus lowercase characters makes it vulnerable to collisions on case-insensitive filesystems and legacy databases. Furthermore, standard Base-64 requires URL-safe variations (RFC 4648) to avoid collision with URI reserved characters.


Radix 36 hits the sweet spot: it is totally case-insensitive, yields compact strings, and requires zero URL encoding.

5. Visualizing & Validating Complex Edge Cases

When designing custom bitmasks, verifying register overflows, or calculating variable-width two's complement strings, hand-calculating bit shifts invites human error.


If you are debugging packet headers or writing firmware-level parsers, you can cross-reference your math against this interactive Number Base Converter sandbox, which calculates signed/unsigned representations, arbitrary-width sign extensions, and custom radices from 2 to 36 without floating-point precision drops.

Summary Checklist for Systems Developers

  1. Check your signedness: 0xFF is 255 in an unsigned 8-bit byte, but -1 in a signed two's complement register.
  2. Be cautious with bitwise shifts in JavaScript: JS converts operands into 32-bit signed integers before running bitwise operators. >> sign-extends; >>> zero-extends.
  3. Beware the 53-bit boundary: Never parse 64-bit IDs or large hashes with parseInt() or floating-point primitives—always enforce BigInt.
  4. Use Base-36 for safe slugification: When you need human-readable, URL-safe, compact sequential keys without case-sensitivity bugs, reach for Radix 36 before Base-64.

A ghostly cat is lurking in space, and NASA captured it

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Just in time for Halloween, NASA has revealed a ghostly figure lurking in a distant cloud of gas and dust. It looks remarkably like a cat, complete with a glowing eye and a long, shadowy body.

The eerie formation, nicknamed the Cat’s Head Nebula, lies about 7,500 light-years from Earth within the enormous Carina Nebula.

New images from the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes reveal this cosmic cat in extraordinary detail. 

But behind its haunting appearance is something even more fascinating: a turbulent region where new stars are taking shape.

A cat in the cosmos

The Cat’s Head Nebula gets its nickname from a towering column of dust that resembles a cat sitting with its back toward the viewer.

Its head appears turned to one side, revealing a single glowing eye. A narrow trail of dark material extends downward like a tail.

The resemblance is a coincidence, created by the forces that shape clouds of gas and dust in space.

Powerful ultraviolet radiation and stellar winds from massive nearby stars have gradually carved away the surrounding material.

Denser portions of the cloud have resisted this erosion, leaving behind the distinctive feline silhouette.

The structure is not permanent. Over time, radiation and winds will continue breaking apart the cloud, changing its appearance as new stars emerge.

Hubble reveals the cat

Hubble’s visible-light image shows the cosmic cat as a dark brown pillar surrounded by brilliant shades of blue, yellow, orange, and red.

Its glowing red eye may mark a young star that remains partly hidden inside the dusty structure.

Along the right edge of the pillar, a bright yellowish border reveals where radiation from nearby massive stars is affecting the gas.

This radiation can strip material away through a process called photoevaporation, gradually dispersing the cloud.

The surrounding colors also carry scientific information.

Hubble’s observations capture light associated with oxygen, hydrogen, and sulfur, along with starlight scattered by dust.

Together, these signals help astronomers understand the physical conditions inside this active stellar nursery.

Webb sees through dust

While Hubble reveals the nebula’s dramatic outline, the James Webb Space Telescope provides a different perspective.

Webb observes infrared light, allowing it to detect structures that are difficult or impossible to see in visible light.

In its near-infrared image, the cat’s dark body becomes more transparent. Intricate patterns emerge within the dusty pillar, while numerous stars appear throughout the surrounding region.

Some of these stars remain partly obscured, but others shine clearly through the material.

Webb’s sensitive instruments even reveal faint background galaxies far beyond the nebula.

The cat’s glowing eye remains visible, appearing yellow rather than red in this infrared view.

The observations suggest that the bright source may be a young star still surrounded by the material from which it formed.

Hidden cosmic details

Webb’s mid-infrared observations reveal another layer of the nebula’s structure.

Warm dust and carbon-based molecules, energized by starlight, produce emissions that outline delicate strands and clumps of material.

In this image, the cat’s body appears bluish-gray against a predominantly red background.

Wispy formations to the left of the main pillar become especially noticeable, revealing details that are much harder to distinguish in Hubble’s visible-light image.

Another bright source appears to the right of the pillar, potentially marking an infant star embedded in dust.

A narrow filament seems to cross the source, although the image alone cannot establish whether the two structures are physically connected.

These different observations allow scientists to investigate the same region using several kinds of light.

Each wavelength reveals different features, providing a more complete picture of the gas, dust, and developing stars.

Stars are taking shape

The Cat’s Head Nebula is part of the much larger Carina Nebula, one of the Milky Way’s most remarkable regions of star formation.

Within such stellar nurseries, gravity draws clouds of gas and dust together. As dense pockets collapse, they can eventually form new stars.

At the same time, existing massive stars release powerful radiation and winds that reshape the surrounding environment.

These competing processes make star formation complicated. The same radiation that disperses a cloud can also influence how its remaining material evolves.

By combining Hubble’s visible-light observations with Webb’s infrared views, astronomers can examine these processes in greater detail.

The cosmic cat will not keep its familiar shape forever. Its dusty silhouette will gradually change and eventually disappear.

When you think of the fall season, chances are that gorgeous foliage, brisk sweater weather, and Halloween decorations come to mind. Deer, on the other hand, think of sex. 

Deer breeding behavior, known as the rut, aligns with the changing length of daylight. As daylight decreases, buck (male deer) and doe (female deer) hormones increase. While in southern states the rut can last several months, it only runs for a short period of time during the fall in the Northeast. 

During the rut, bucks seek out does that are ready to breed, and the amorous pursuit can be intense. Because some bucks are so obsessed with breeding, they can eat little and burn through up to 20 percent of their body weight during the season. And just like love-crazed high schoolers, deer during the rut are more distracted and less cautious, as well as more active during the day than they usually are. 

“During this period, many yearling bucks leave their birth areas in search of new territory, sometimes dozens of miles away. At the same time, adult bucks are actively roaming their home ranges in pursuit of doe, often chasing those they encounter,” according to Raven Ridge Wildlife Center in Pennsylvania. 

What’s more, “The end of daylight-saving time means more vehicles will be on the road during dusk and dawn, which are the peak hours for deer movement.”

In fact, most deer-vehicle accidents take place in October through December. While there are things you can do to lessen your chances of hitting one—such as staying alert and paying close attention when you see deer crossing signs—sometimes accidents are unavoidable. So what should you do if you hit a deer?

Safely pull over and turn your emergency flashers on. While your instinct might be to go see the deer or move the body off of the road, resist the temptation. Care for any injured passengers, and call 911. The dispatchers will send the appropriate authorities to help. 

And if you narrowly avoided one deer, remember that, “deer often travel in groups and walk single file,” per the Raven Ridge Wildlife Center. “So even if one deer successfully crosses the road in front of a driver, it doesn’t mean the threat is over. Another could be right behind it.”

Heads up: The clocks are about to change. The evening of Halloween gets an extra hour as the clocks fall back an hour, meaning you can sleep in on Sunday, November 1. 

Complaints about daylight savings are as old as daylight savings itself, and pretty much everyone has an opinion about the practice. As with any such topic, there are all kinds of myths and misunderstandings out there. 

Farmers didn’t ask for daylight savings

This one comes up a lot: the idea that farmers are the reason we have daylight savings. In this version of the story, farmers, who work outside and need to align their workdays with the daylight, were the ones who made the argument for the practice. 

In truth, farmers were the main group opposed to daylight savings during World War I. According to the Library of Congress: “The agriculture industry fervently opposed the measure because farming schedules are based on sunrise and sunset not the clock.” 

After the war there was a bill to repeal daylight savings, which passed through both houses of Congress. President Woodrow Wilson vetoed that bill, and his statement about the veto acknowledged this wouldn’t make farmers happy. 

“I realize the very considerable and in some respects very serious inconveniences to which the daylight-saving law subjects the farmers of the country,” he wrote. Wilson sided with the industrial sector. The veto was overridden, leading to a long period where daylight savings was practiced only in certain areas. For example: New York City observed daylight savings, but rural areas in New York State did not (not what you’d expect if farmers broadly supported daylight savings). 

None of this is to say that some farmers don’t support daylight savings time today—it’s just that farmers aren’t the reason the practice exists in America. If anything, they tried to stop it. 

Benjamin Franklin didn’t invent daylight savings

There are some “facts” repeated so often that hardly anyone questions them, and “Ben Franklin invented daylight savings time” remains one of them. The problem is that it’s not true. 

I’d understand if you’re skeptical—I’m a Canadian journalist trying to steal an achievement from one of America’s founding fathers. But you don’t have to take my word on this one—The Franklin Institute, a museum dedicated to Franklin’s legacy, states that “Daylight saving time is one thing that Franklin did not invent.” The United States didn’t even implement daylight savings until 1918, well over a century after Franklin passed away. 

So where did the idea that he invented it come from? Probably from a satirical Journal de Paris article Franklin penned back in 1784. 

In that piece he jokingly encouraged the residents of Paris to wake up earlier in the morning, in order to spend less money on candles. He also proposed a tax on shutters, rationing of candles, a prohibition of carriages on the streets after sunset, and church bells ringing at the crack of dawn every day. He, it goes without saying, wasn’t being entirely serious. 

So where did the idea for daylight savings come from? New Zealand entomologist George Hudson first proposed intentionally shifting the clock in an 1895 talk to the Wellington Philosophical Society, then followed up with a paper in 1898. 

The US already tried year-round daylight savings

This isn’t a myth, exactly, but it is something that rarely comes up in contemporary discussions about daylight savings. Multiple US states have proposed doing away with standard time entirely. Nationally, the Sunshine Protection Act, which passed the House over the summer, would institute the change from sea to shining sea. 

Here’s the thing, though: this isn’t the first time. The US tried making daylight savings permanent during the height of the 1970s energy crisis. The problem was that people hated it. What was supposed to be a two-year experiment lasted less than a year because of a massive change in public opinion. 

Support for the change started high, as an October 1974 New York Times article lays out: 79 percent of the public favored the idea in December of 1973, but by February of 1974, that dropped to 42 percent—a 37-point swing. A bill to restore standard time in winter passed the House by a 362 to 16 vote. 

What caused the backlash? Dark winter mornings. Particularly prominent were complaints from parents, who didn’t like their children walking to school in the dark. 

A 1975 study by the US Department of Transportation concluded that a year-round switch possibly isn’t a great idea, proposing instead a schedule change. “We conclude that modest overall benefits might be realized by a shift from the historic 6-month DST system (May through October) to an 8-month DST system (March through October),” the paper concludes. This, notably, is pretty much what the US has been doing since 2007. 

The past isn’t a prediction of the future—it’s possible switching to year-round daylight savings would go differently today. Walking to school is less common, for one thing, and streets are better lit. All the same, it’s worth realizing this has been tried before.

November 12-ish: Mercury, Venus, Mars, and Jupiter in Alignment

Mercury is generally difficult to spot. It’s our solar system’s smallest planet and is often lost in the sun’s haze. But in the middle of this month, this speedy boy is flitting in to join the party that Venus, Mars, and Jupiter are throwing all month. The three larger planets will be in alignment for all of November, festooning the morning sky just before  dawn. Venus will be in the southeastern sky, with Mars high in the eastern sky and Jupiter a bit lower, but also towards the east. And Mercury? Look low in the southeast, and it should be visible just before the sun emerges over the horizon.

November 16: Mars and Jupiter Conjunction

As the month continues, Mars and Jupiter will edge closer and closer to one another, casting shy glances at one another across the sky like teenagers at a school dance. They’ll finally slide in for a waltz just before sunrise on November 16, where their conjunction in the eastern sky will be chaperoned by the bright star Regulus. Look to the east, but make sure you stop looking once the sun begins to peek its way into the firmament. No planetary conjunction is worth cooking your eyes.

November 18: Leonids Meteor Shower Predicted Peak

And once the music stops and Mars and Jupiter will slip gracefully away, the fireworks will begin. The Leonids will be visible all month, but their peak is predicted for November 18. At that time, their radiant point—the point from which the meteors appear to emerge—will be right next to the two disengaging dancers in the constellation Leo. This shower usually produces 10 to 15 meteors per hour. However, in 1966 it exploded into one of the most spectacular displays of celestial pyrotechnics ever seen, with thousands of meteors visible every minute. So you never know what you might see!

November 24: Full Beaver Supermoon

This month’s full moon is the first of three consecutive supermoons. As per the ever-indispensable Farmer’s Almanac, it will reach peak illumination while below the horizon at 9:50 a.m. EST on November 24. This month’s moon takes its name from the industrious, dam-building rodents of North America, whose long summers of hard work start to pay off in November, when they retreat to their lodges to wait out the winter. Several other Indigenous nations have names that also refer to what animals get up to during the fall: the Tlingit call it the Digging Moon, to the Algonquin it’s the Whitefish Moon, while the Dakota and Lakota call it the Deer Rutting Moon. 

November 27: Comet 161P/Hartley–IRAS Perihelion

The final event of November: a visit from Comet 161P/Hartley–IRAS. The space rock  will reach perihelion—the point at which it’s closest to the sun—on November 27. At that point, its magnitude will be about 12, which means you’ll want a decent telescope to spot it. The International Comet Quarterly suggests at least using a telescope with at least a 7.8 inch (20 centimeter) aperture. If you’ve got such an apparatus, look to the east-northeast, just above the horizon.

The Request for Information seeks commercial interest in: potentially conducting a mission to boost Hubble to a higher altitude to extend its science lifetime; servicing Hubble to enhance its capabilities; or performing a safe, controlled re-entry into Earth’s atmosphere or orbital disposal at the end of Hubble’s mission.

“Hubble is a national icon that has fundamentally changed how humanity views our universe for more than 35 years,” said Nicky Fox, associate administrator, Science Mission Directorate, NASA Headquarters in Washington. “Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing. Justices to debate when an established rule is “previously unavailable” in habeas case

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court will hear oral argument on Nov. 4 in Guerrero v. Johnson, a case about the limited circumstances in which a state prisoner can return to federal court for a second round of post-conviction review.

But let’s back up a bit: If inmates want to challenge the grounds for their detention, they must typically bring a habeas claim. The Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996, seeking to curb the number of such petitions, made it significantly more difficult to bring successive applications. Specifically, under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2) of AEDPA, a new claim in a second federal habeas petition is barred unless it falls under one of two exceptions. The first is when such a claim “relies on a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable.” The second exception covers claims based on “the facts underlying the [initial] claim,” but only if those facts would show “by clear and convincing evidence that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable factfinder would have found the applicant guilty of the underlying offense.”

Texas inmate Dexter Johnson brought his claim under the first exception, contending that he cannot be put to death based on the court’s 2002 ruling in Atkins v. Virginia, which held that executing people with intellectual disabilities violates the Eighth Amendment’s ban on cruel and unusual punishment. Johnson did not present the Atkins claim in his initial petitions but, after qualifying as intellectually disabled under revised diagnostic standards, raised this in a subsequent petition. The question before the justices is whether Johnson’s Atkins claim was “previously unavailable” to him.

Some background

In 2007, Johnson was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas for his role in the double murder of 23-year-old Maria Aparece and 17-year-old Huy Ngo. At trial, a defense expert testified that Johnson’s IQ was between 74 and 88 and he “did not have obvious deficits” with regard to “personal independence and social responsibility when compared to other people of similar age and sociocultural background.” Under the diagnostic standards in place at that time, the expert concluded that Johnson was not intellectually disabled. (Johnson did not raise an Atkins claim in his initial habeas petitions but invoked Atkins in those petitions to argue – unsuccessfully – that its protections should extend to people with mental illness.)

In May 2013, while Johnson’s federal petition was pending, the American Psychiatric Association published the fifth edition of its diagnostic manual, known as the DSM-5, which Texas’ highest criminal court adopted in 2018. The new edition de-emphasized IQ cutoffs and recognized that someone whose score is above 70 may still be intellectually disabled. In April 2019, an expert gave Johnson an IQ test on which he scored 70, and a second expert agreed that Johnson met the DSM-5’s criteria for intellectual disability.

Johnson filed a successive habeas application in the district court, in which he claimed that he could not be executed under Atkins. After an evidentiary hearing, the trial court determined that Johnson’s Atkins claim was previously unavailable and consequently stayed his execution. The state appealed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit, which in July 2025 affirmed the district court’s decision. The full 5th Circuit then declined to rehear the case by a vote of 9-7. In February, the state asked the Supreme Court to weigh in, which the Supreme Court agreed in June to do.

The parties’ arguments

In its brief, the Office of the Texas Attorney General maintains that “Johnson’s Atkins claim does not rely on a change in the law.” Instead, it emphasizes, Atkins was decided well before Johnson filed his initial habeas application, and “before Johnson even committed the capital murder for which he was sentenced to death.” Nor, according to the Texas AG’s office, would permitting Johnson to bring such a claim serve the purpose of enabling successive petitions, which is to allow for new evidence as to whether a person is “guilty of the underlying offense.”

The state further presses the point that no “rule” was previously unavailable here. “An object—or a rule—can be available even when it is not needed or useful. A carpenter, for example, likely has a hammer ‘available,’ even when working on a task for which a hammer would not be necessary or useful,” the state writes. The publication of the DSM-5 may have allowed Johnson to discover a new factual predicate, according to the state, but it does not change the availability of any rule. Instead, the 5th Circuit’s approach “erases the line Congress drew between new law and new facts and effectively writes § 2244(b)(2)(B)’s limitations out of the statute.”

Johnson’s attorneys counter that something is “available” only if it is “capable of being used by a person, for a purpose”; prior to the publication of the DSM-5, no such usage was possible. “No one would say the hammer was available to the carpenter if it were locked in a toolbox he couldn’t open,” Johnson’s team writes. “Mr. Johnson did not change; his intellectual and adaptive characteristics are the same as always. What changed was the framework that made Atkins capable of protecting him. Atkins was not available to him before; now it is.” Nor, contrary to the AG’s claim otherwise, Johnson’s lawyers argue, would this open the “floodgates” to many other such challenges. Because very few rules are retroactive, such issues rarely arise.

Perhaps unsurprisingly given its stance on capital punishment, the Trump administration filed a “friend of the court” brief supporting Texas. Echoing several of the state’s arguments, U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer asserts that “[s]tandards promulgated by a board of psychiatrists cannot transform a decision of this Court that predates a prior habeas application into a ‘new’ and ‘previously unavailable’ rule of constitutional law.” Louisiana and 21 other states, also supporting Texas, call the 5th Circuit’s rule “a recipe for eternal Atkins claims and zero finality,” pointing out that a professional association also moved the age by which intellectual disability must appear from 18 to 22 in 2021 – raising the possibility that such age-based claims could also be brought and questioning the possibility that the habeas floodgates would remain closed were the court to agree with Johnson.

On Johnson’s side, the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers compares Johnson’s claim to second petitions based on new science about “microscopic hair comparison analysis, arson science, and shaken baby syndrome.” According to the NACDL, “claims of constitutional error grounded in faulty science in a second or successive petition” must be allowed to go forward because “flawed analytical methods may not be debunked until” it is too late.

One final thing worth mentioning: Last term, after hearing argument in Hamm v. Smith, a dispute over how courts should weigh multiple IQ scores in Atkins cases, the justices dismissed the case as improvidently granted (known as a “DIG”) – that is, they decided that it was a mistake to have agreed to hear the case in the first place and did not resolve it. At the end of his brief, Johnson asks the court to do the same in his case on the ground that the state has continually changed its arguments. Whatever the merits of Johnson’s position on that front, it seems unlikely the court will DIG two Atkins cases in successive terms.

The decision in Guerrero v. Johnson is expected by late June or early July.

How ADHD Science Fails Women in Menopause

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Menopause aggravates and elevates ADHD symptoms for a staggering 97.5 percent of women with the condition, according to a survey of 600 women cited in the Journal of Psychiatric Research.1 Complaints include increased difficulty with attention, emotional regulation, and managing everyday demands – symptoms that significantly degrade quality of life yet remain largely unaddressed in midlife ADHD treatment plans.

What Explains This Disconnect?

The Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) – one of the standard measures for assessing common ADHD symptoms – may not fully capture how much menopause interferes with daily life because it was not specifically designed to measure changes in ADHD across hormonal transitions.

While nearly all of the 600 women in the survey reported worsening ADHD symptoms during menopause, their ASRS scores were similar across different hormonal life stages. This apparent contradiction deserves attention. A woman may experience a major change in mental clarity, working memory, motivation, emotional regulation, and managing day-to-day responsibilities without necessarily showing a large change on a standard ADHD symptom checklist.

This points to a critical need for better ways to assess ADHD across the lifespan and to understand how hormonal changes may alter its presentation and impact.

Addressing Hormonal Changes

Today, we know about ADHD in menopause thanks, largely, to women’s personal accounts. Though surveys and self-reports cannot establish cause and effect in the same way as controlled clinical trials, they are an essential starting point. They can identify patterns and raise questions that researchers need to investigate.

One central question is regarding the safety and efficacy of prescribing hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and/or ADHD medication to women in perimenopause and menopause. Sadly, evidence specifically supporting combined HRT and stimulant treatment remains limited. We need well-designed studies to determine which women might benefit, what treatments are most effective, and how the medications may interact.

Shrouded in ice in the lonely Weddell Sea, Vega Island is an eerie sight to behold. On this speck of land east of the Antarctic Peninsula, ice, rock outcrops, and glacial moraines combine to create the appearance of a smirking skull in profile looking toward Devil Island.

These images show Vega Island’s creepy countenance at the start of austral autumn (left) and in spring (right). They were acquired with the OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 on March 22, 2025, and October 10, 2026, respectively. (Clouds obscured the island in March 2026.) A nunatak jutting out of the ice has the appearance of an eye socket, a lateral moraine forms a mouth, and Bahía del Diablo (Devil’s Bay) Glacier constitutes a jawbone. In the March 2025 image, a layer of snow coats the ice, which is typical of the region’s wettest month. That contrasts with the October 2026 image, when snow is absent from large areas and darker ice surfaces are visible.

Above-average temperatures in winter 2026—particularly in June—were partly responsible for so much bare ice in October, said glaciologist Mauri Pelto of Nichols College. The ice exposed on Vega and nearby islands indicates limited new snow accumulation over the winter, further reduced by sublimation and wind scouring, he said. “The winter season was no treat for these glaciers.”

The melt season ahead may also be more trick than treat. Bare ice is darker than snow and absorbs more solar energy, meaning that melting is poised to speed up as the days grow longer and warmer. “Even a thin cover of new snow from a spring storm would be helpful in the short run,” Pelto said. “When entombed in snow and ice, the skull is preserved. But when exposed to melt, it weathers away, which will lead to a changing jawline and expanding eye socket.”

October 10, 2026 NASA Earth Observatory/Lauren Dauphin

The first comprehensive mapping of Vega Island’s ice was aided by Landsat imagery. In a study published in 1982, scientists described the island’s four ice caps with protruding rocky cliffs and outlet glaciers. They leveraged early Landsat observations along with aerial photos and topographic maps to catalog 29 glaciers on Vega.

Of those glaciers, Devil’s Bay Glacier has been closely monitored since the austral summer of 1998-1999, with researchers measuring an overall decline in its mass. Satellite images acquired in recent decades show ice loss from other glaciers on the island as well, with increased areas of exposed bedrock.

Long before scientists began tracking its glaciers, Vega was a bright spot in a tale of terror born out of early exploration of the area. The Swedish Antarctic Expedition of 1901-1903 set out to study the geography, geology, climate, and wildlife on and around the eastern Antarctic Peninsula. The party became split into three groups during their explorations, isolated for a winter in different locations. Adding to the horror, they lost their ship to the pack ice. Cape Well-met, as it would come to be known, off the “forehead” of the Vega Island skull, was the site of a happy reunion of two of the groups in spring 1903. Soon thereafter, an Argentinean expedition rescued the survivors of the ordeal.

Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals.

Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century.

A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. 

None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries.

A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds.

Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. 

At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds.

But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. 

So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. 

Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control.

Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. 

The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels.

The goal isn’t to make school easier for students with dyslexia

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

One of the most important distinctions we can make when educating students with dyslexia is the difference between making learning accessible and simply making it easier. Only the former does them any favors.

Students with dyslexia need support. They may need explicit reading instruction, more time to complete certain tasks or another way to access grade-level content while they develop their reading skills. But none of those things tells us what a student’s potential is.

When a student has difficulty reading, educators and parents tend to simplify the work itself. We shorten the text. We reduce the amount of material. We lower the complexity of the questions. Those choices may sometimes be appropriate, depending on the instructional goal. Still, they can also create a second problem: the reading disability begins to limit the student’s access to ideas they are perfectly capable of understanding.

Dyslexia is a specific reading disability. Absent other hurdles, students with dyslexia are capable learners, even when their reading performance doesn’t reflect their broader abilities. Because the learning difference can be difficult to see, early reading struggles are sometimes attributed instead to motivation, maturity or effort. 

The challenge for educators is to give students the support they need without letting their challenge areas determine what we expect of them elsewhere.

Start with the barrier

Supporting a student with dyslexia begins with understanding what is getting in the way of learning.

“Struggling reader” may describe what we observe, but it doesn’t tell us why a student is struggling or what to do about it.

This becomes especially important as students get older. A student who has difficulty decoding may need systematic instruction at the word level, including work with syllables, morphology and multisyllabic words. Another student may read words accurately but struggle to understand or retain what they have read. A third may comprehend a short passage but have difficulty holding ideas in mind across a longer text because of attention, stamina or executive function.

Those students can receive the same low comprehension score and need very different instruction. 

If we don’t identify the barrier, we risk responding to all three students in the same way. Worse, we may mistake difficulty accessing the material for an inability to understand it.

We see the importance of that distinction regularly. For example, two seventh-grade students came to us with nearly identical reading comprehension scores. After working with each of them, we understood that one of the students was guessing at longer words, and she needed systematic instruction in areas like advanced decoding skills and morphology. The other student was reading much more accurately but more slowly. He had difficulty holding ideas in mind and lost the thread of what he was reading. He needed more targeted support in text chunking and monitoring for understanding. Both students struggled with fluency, one with accuracy and the other with rate. Each needed different strategies. Neither would have benefited from “easier” texts.

Support should be precise

Once we understand why a student is struggling, instruction should address that need directly.

For students with dyslexia, that means evidence-based, explicit reading instruction delivered with enough frequency and intensity to make a difference. As students get older, instruction must also grow with them. An adolescent who still needs help with decoding should receive it, but the materials and context should reflect that the learner is now an older student.

We shouldn’t stop teaching foundational reading skills because a student has reached middle school, nor should we make a middle schooler feel as though they are back in grade school to receive them.

The same principle applies beyond reading intervention. If decoding is preventing a student from accessing a science lesson, for example, we need to think separately about the reading skill we are trying to remediate and the science content we want that student to learn.

This becomes increasingly consequential as students move through school. From roughly fourth grade on, students are expected to use reading to learn across subjects, just as texts become more complex and demand greater vocabulary, background knowledge, fluency and working memory. When word recognition requires significant effort, students expend cognitive resources on decoding that would otherwise be available for meaning. 

High expectations

There is another side to this conversation. “High expectations” can become an empty phrase if we aren’t giving students the instruction and time they need to meet them.

A student who hasn’t developed a skill won’t acquire it simply because we keep asking for it.

Students who are behind need to make accelerated progress to close the gap. That requires time. Educators must identify the student’s needs, match instruction accordingly and provide enough time for that instruction to be effective. 

At The Southport School, where we serve students in grades 2 through 8 with language-based learning differences and ADHD, we see what can happen when instruction is explicit and sufficiently intensive. In our most recent graduating cohort, students averaged 1.9 years of reading comprehension growth per year of enrollment. At entry, 59 percent scored in the low-average range or below in reading comprehension. By graduation, 89 percent scored in the average range or above. 

Those gains come from understanding what each student needs to make progress and then providing instruction accordingly.

Students should understand the difference, too

Students know whether reading is harder for them than for their classmates. Years of difficulty can change how they see themselves. Some begin to avoid tasks that repeatedly frustrate or embarrass them, which reduces their practice and makes progress even harder. By middle school, students may conclude that they cannot learn, rather than recognizing the specific skills they haven’t yet developed. 

Part of our responsibility as educators is to help students develop a more accurate understanding of themselves. They should know where they struggle. They should also understand why, recognize where they are strong and learn to advocate for the support they need.

October is Dyslexia and Learning Differences Awareness Month, a time for administrators and educators to reflect on what support means for the students we serve. Awareness is important, but our instructional choices are where that understanding becomes consequential.

Renovation Diaries: The House Envelope and Other Lessons in Efficiency

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: This is the latest in a series of posts by Sally Kohn— journalist and CNN political commentator, TED talk giver, and design aficionado—chronicling her adventures in remodeling.

Above: The exterior as it is now.

Let’s level set. I live in a 1780s barn that was turned into a house in the 1950s, and as far as I can tell the windows and the siding haven’t been changed since. The fact is, many parts of our money pit farmhouse need updating. Some, like the 1950s asphalt roof built directly on top of a much older shake shingle roof, we replaced last year. Other things we’re still waiting on, like the vintage 1950s bathroom tile with vintage crumbling grout. But since we can’t close many of our windows without pushing on them from the outside, and we have an inexplicable leak behind our very ancient siding, we put off the inevitable long enough. It is time for some grand-scale renovations.  

Stay tuned for some of the other things we’ll be changing, including a kitchen refresh. But first up, if we’re going to go to all the trouble—and, ahem, expense—of replacing all of our siding and windows, can we make our old home more environmentally responsible in terms of both materials and energy efficiency? Let’s find out.

My guide on this journey is Shannon Pendleton, principal of Sanderson Sustainable Design and co-host of Passive House Accelerator. In addition to being a friend and neighbor, Shannon is a leading expert on how to make homes more sustainable and energy efficient. And while those concepts might immediately make you think of solar panels and the like, Shannon explains that in energy efficiency, an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. In other words, using green energy to power your home is great. But designing your home to need less energy in the first place is even better.  

But with apologies to Shannon, when she explained the science of the “building envelope,” all I could think about is that envelope feels like the wrong word. Maybe it should be “building envelop”—as in, how enveloped is your building. Because your “building envelop” is what stops the outside environment from getting in and the inside environment from getting out. That means everything from bugs and dust to wind to heat and cold.  

Above: A tighter envelope = warmer interiors.

So I’m making two big changes to my “building envelop,” both of which create an opportunity for more energy efficiency. Let’s start with the siding. We have very old, very stained, very falling-apart cedar. I could just replace it with cedar, maybe even sustainably harvested cedar. But the fact is, our old cedar is cupping and splitting. Yes, more maintenance would help, but that’s hard to do with second-story wood. Also I’m lazy. Alternatively, I could have gone with some plastic product or chemically treated wood but, like I said, we care about the environment and also want to preserve the look and feel of our historic home. So we’re going with Thermory siding.  

Thermory takes sustainably forested wood—in our case, we’re using pine—and puts it through an intense heat-treating process that makes the wood more stable and rot-resistant. It’s an updated twist on the Japanese shou sugi ban process, where wood is charred to make it water and rot resistant. Thermory is that, without the blackening (though they actually do have a product that mirrors the shou sugi ban look, but with the added benefits of Thermory’s advanced thermal modification). In other words, Thermory gives us everything we like about natural wood plus all these added benefits.  

Using a more stable rot-resistant siding definitely envelops our house better (take that, flying squirrels), but the energy efficiency upgrades come from what will be behind the siding. Our contractor—the wonderful, lovable, fantastic-to-work-with John Gemmi and his company, Gemmi Construction—were already planning to weather-seal behind the siding, and of course put new insulation behind that where we find any lacking. But Shannon suggested installing “a rain screen, a continuous air barrier, and continuous exterior insulation” to give us the most enveloped envelope possible. Ultimately, we settled on Zip R sheathing, which does all three of those things in one system. So that’s our plan.

Above: Me, my wife Sarah, and the current siding and windows. Stay tuned for the final reveal.

As for the windows, well…. The most energy-efficient option is triple-pane glass, but in our case, since we’re replacing a gajillion windows, we just couldn’t make it work in our budget. We have two walls of windows in our great room, most of which are currently fixed panes that we’re excited to replace with operable awning windows. We looked into doing triple-pane for just those windows, but that would have made the windows too heavy to open. So yes, we’re choosing a little less efficiency there but counting on the fact that having new windows that don’t require a screwdriver and grunts to close will nevertheless up our efficiency. Plus, thanks to Ambiance Design, we’re adding retractable Duette Honeycomb shades over those large window walls, which will give us some extra energy efficiency on super hot days.  

Niece Lumber helped us pick out Lincoln Windows, a family run U.S.-manufactured brand with two advantages. First, we could get retractable screens which we’re very excited about. And second, they offer custom colors on their exteriors. You’ll have to stay tuned to figure out what color choice we’ve made. It’s gonna be…dramatic.

When the twenty-two-year-old Sylvia Plath (October 27, 1932–February 11, 1963) wrote to her mother one bleak January day, both women were wading through a darkness of the soul. Science was only just beginning to hone the tools with which to begin dissecting the elemental mystery of what makes us who we are: how much of our psychology and what we experience as our personhood — whether we call it self or soul or spirit — is a product of our physical constitution, of the particular biochemical processes coursing through our particular infrastructure of matter that we experience as our body. Neuroscience was then an infant science — it still is — and the helix of heredity had just been discovered, hinting at the promise of new clarity on the ancient puzzlement of nature versus nurture, new insight into how much of our psychology is a biological inheritance and how much an ongoing composition continually revised by the confluence of chance and choice we call experience.

Sylvia Plath by Rollie McKenna (Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery)

Several years earlier, the teenage Plath had begun contouring her consciousness and mapping its psychological promontories, its luminous surfaces and its dark edges, rhapsodizing about the joy of living, thinking deeply about free will and what make us who we are, and composing her first tragic poem in response to a minor domestic accident. Two years before she shaded in what was becoming an all-consuming darkness in “The Disquieting Muses,” she wrote to her mother in a letter included in the posthumously collected Letters Home: Correspondence 1950–1963 (public library):

I don’t know whether it is an hereditary characteristic, but our little family is altogether too prone to lie awake at nights hating ourselves for stupidities — technical or verbal or whatever — and to let careless, cruel remarks fester until they blossom in something like ulcer attacks — I know that during these last days I’ve been fighting an enormous battle with myself.

But then, with the presence of mind and the triumph of spirit that allowed her to live through the remaining years of her life — years she filled with some of the most timelessly exquisite poetry ever written — she adds:

But beyond a point, fighting only wears one out and one has to shut off that nagging part of the mind and go on without it with bravo and philosophy… Your present life is the important thing.

There is a dangerous fallacy — a biological falsehood, a feebleness of empathy, an ethical failing — in the view that people who die by suicide after living with mental illness have somehow failed at life. It is one thing to feel deeply the tragedy of that loss, to rue the help not available to them in their time of struggle; it is quite another to fault the faulty instrument itself. It is impossible for any one consciousness to truly know what living inside another is like in the first place — we make art and poems and songs to try to show each other what it is like to be alive in this body-mind. But it is especially unfathomable for a mind coursing with fairly ordinary biochemistry, housed in a brain with fairy ordinary neurophysiology, to grasp what it might be like to live with a mind inflamed by ceaselessly misfiring neurotransmitters or a mind housed in a brain with a large tumor pressing against the amygdala at every moment of every hour. To survive even a single day with such a mind is no small feat. To have not only survived thirty-one years, as Sylvia Plath did, but to have filled those years with works of staggering beauty, with poems that irradiate generations of lives — that is a rare triumph of the spirit.

The bottleneck in every classroom: Working memory, cognitive load, and the conditions for learning

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Throughout my career, I have held various roles (teacher, principal, director of curriculum and instruction, author, speaker, and solutions provider) but I fundamentally see all of them as forms of teaching. The core principle remains unchanged: once a teacher, always a teacher. My actions are consistently guided by one fundamental question: how can we best support students’ learning processes based on cognitive science principles to achieve optimal understanding and retrieval?

That question eventually led me to the science of learning, and specifically to cognitive load theory. It’s a simple idea with enormous implications for how we design classrooms, and I think every educator and every EdTech company that builds classroom products needs to understand it.

The bottleneck we all share

For learning to happen, information has to pass through two memory systems. Long-term memory is where lasting knowledge lives, and it’s effectively unlimited. But nothing reaches it directly; everything has to move first through working memory, the small mental workspace where we actively hold and make sense of new information. That’s the bottleneck every lesson has to move through. Working memory can hold only three to four items at once (Cowan, 2010). If those items make the transfer into long-term memory, they stay with us. That’s how students build durable learning. If they don’t make it all the way through, we forget them.

Everyone deals with this. It’s why we break phone numbers into short chunks instead of holding all ten digits at once: working memory can only juggle a handful of items in any given moment, and chunking is how we work around that limit. Miller’s classic estimate (1956) put the number higher, seven, give or take two, but more controlled research since has revised it down to about four (Cowan, 2010). Either way, the lesson holds: it’s also why we forget someone’s name two minutes after meeting them at a gathering. This isn’t a student limitation or a young-brain limitation. It’s a human-brain limitation.

Two kinds of load — and only one of them helps

Cognitive load theory splits the demand on that bottleneck into two categories (Sweller, 1988; Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 1998). Intrinsic load is the necessary difficulty of the content itself, the load required for students actually to learn the material. Extraneous load is the load imposed by the surroundings: distraction, clutter, anything competing for the same mental space as the learning. Only extraneous load is pure waste. Our brains are built to focus on one important thing at a time, so anything extraneous simply crowds out the material we actually want students to keep.

That distinction reframed how I think about technology in the classroom. My instinct isn’t to keep equipping classrooms with more technology. It’s a better question to ask whether a given tool is reducing cognitive load, removing barriers, and protecting the relationship between the teacher, the student, and the task they’re working on.

Why the explicit, guided approach wins

This is where the research is unambiguous. Novices learn far better with clear, guided, step-by-step teaching than they do being left to discover material on their own, and it comes down to protecting that same narrow channel. Rosenshine’s Principles of Instruction (2012) and Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark’s foundational 2006 paper on minimally guided instruction both point in the same direction: every step protects working memory:

●      Small steps that introduce one element at a time

●      Modeled examples and discussion before students go it alone

●      Guided practice with support before release

●      And review spaced out until recall becomes automatic.

In practice, this means chunking and pacing instruction: seven to eight minutes of instruction, then retrieval, so students can encode what they just heard before you move on. Retrieval isn’t a quick quiz or exit ticket tacked onto the end of a lesson. It’s the mechanism of the learning itself. When you think about the classroom, think about what conditions are most conducive to durable learning — the barriers we can remove, and what the research says deep learning actually requires.

A real classroom example: The acoustic environment

Extraneous load isn’t abstract, and it isn’t rare. Recent classroom measurements found that ambient noise during active instruction averages around 50 decibels, well above the range where researchers consistently see cognitive performance decline (Visentin & Prodi, 2026). I’ve seen this play out in real classrooms over the years. Consider a classroom that doesn’t have equal voice distribution. A student in the further from the teacher doesn’t hear the same lesson as one who happens to be closer to the teacher. Sound diminishes sharply with distance, so that student is already at a disadvantage before a single distraction even hits. Add HVAC noise, hallway sound, and the classroom next door, and the brain now has to reconstruct words it didn’t fully hear.

That reconstruction isn’t free. Recent research on listening effort confirms as much: even after a word is correctly understood, the brain keeps spending working memory tracking down what it thinks was said, because comprehension and reconstruction draw on the same limited resource (Flaherty & Klug, 2026). When noise creates a gap, that reconstruction effort comes straight out of the capacity a student needs for the actual content.

I’ve seen classrooms move from no voice distribution at all to a teacher wearing a microphone paired with classroom audio, so every seat receives that same voice at the same strength, regardless of where a student sits. That’s not a nice-to-have. It’s a direct reduction in extraneous cognitive load, and it benefits every student in the room, not just the ones who already have a formal accommodation in place, with the clearest gains for younger students, whose auditory processing is still maturing, and multilingual learners who are decoding an unfamiliar language on top of new content.

Next steps for the classroom

If you’re an educator or a leader deciding what to invest in next, the question isn’t, “What new tool should we add?” A better question is, “What’s currently competing with learning for a student’s limited working memory, and how do we remove it?”

  1. Think about the classroom learning conditions and what research says is most conducive for deep learning. What barriers can we remove?
  2. Audit the environment itself: noise, visual clutter, and redundant text and narration, with the same rigor you’d apply to a lesson plan.
  3. Explicit, direct instruction is the most well-supported, evidence-based instructional strategy, and that doesn’t mean lecture alone. It includes guided independent practice, worked examples, modeling, and retrieval: all of it requiring direct communication between teacher and student.
  4. For teachers, chunk instruction for seven to eight minutes. Then practice retrieval, so students can encode. Then provide another seven to eight minutes of instruction. This helps students move information from working memory to long-term memory.
  5. The pedagogy matters, but so does whether every student can actually hear it. Fixing the environment is not separate from good teaching. It’s what makes good teaching reach every student in the room.

What’s Important to Us When Making Political Decisions?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A core assumption in any democracy is that citizens with access to information will make reasoned decisions on political candidates and policy issues. But a new study by UC Berkeley social scientists finds that Americans usually don’t consider the full scope of difficult issues, instead basing their opinions on mental shortcuts that sidestep complexity.

The paper offers striking insight into the differences in cognitive reasoning patterns among Americans. While some people in the study did closely analyze a range of issues before coming to an opinion, others did a quick back-of-the-napkin tally of plusses and minuses, or even more commonly based their opinions on just one factor, the authors found.

“When people make complicated choices with many considerations—which political candidate to support, which immigrant they would rather admit to the country, which climate policy they prefer—we tend to assume they’re weighing all the factors,” said Berkeley political scientist Kirk Bansak. But, he added, “we find that most of them aren’t. Most commonly, people rely on a mental shortcut, effectively deciding based on the single factor they care about most and largely ignoring the rest.”

For example, when asked to choose between two fictional U.S. Senate candidates based on age, gender, race, education, military experience, and political experience, many of the study participants would make their decision based on just one of those factors.

That doesn’t mean that people are irrational, said coauthor Nidhi Banavar, a Berkeley cognitive scientist. “The kind of rationality that we define in the paper is quite different from what many humans actually do in real life,” she explained. “We have these amazing brains, but they’re not the infinite computational machines that would be necessary in order to do the kind of ‘rational’ we imagine in our paper.”

Still, the findings have clear implications for politics and political communication. For example, Banavar said, if a leader knows that public opinion on climate change is dominated by pocketbook concerns to the exclusion of other issues, then that single factor might weigh heavily in political advertising. Complex science issues, meanwhile, might be downplayed.

The paper, “Classifying Strategy Use in Multi-Attribute Subjective Choice: Application to Conjoint Experiments in Political Science,” was published recently in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Bansak is an associate professor of political science at UC Berkeley; Banavar is a postdoctoral researcher in political science at UC Berkeley.

The foundational ideals of American democracy grew from the Enlightenment era some three centuries ago. Central to that framework was a belief that humans, being rational, are capable of governing themselves—and justified in demanding political power. A core concept has been the marketplace of ideas, a constant competition between political and policy goals, plans, and analysis that would inform citizens and help them to govern themselves.

While many eras in U.S. history feature episodes in which reason faltered, modern social scientists have detailed the ways in which cognitive bias, rational ignorance, and other behaviors short-circuit reasoning. Still, the hopeful assumption about human reasoning persists.

Deep patterns in decision making

Social scientists have long studied human decision making at the aggregate level. Banavar and Bansak set out to build a new kind of cognitive model, a complex tool that can enable researchers to see an individual’s decision making mid-process, in detail.

The model doesn’t give them a view into the minds of their research subjects, but the researchers can study hundreds or thousands of decisions by an individual; from these patterns, they can perceive the values and reasoning processes that shape their decisions.

In all, nearly 3,800 people were involved in the study. Each participant sat at a computer screen, and the computer generated a random set of choices in three areas: which migrants they would rather admit to the United States, which Senate primary candidate they would rather vote for, and which climate policy package they would prefer the United States to adopt.

Consider the basic question comparing two hypothetical migrants to the U.S. Their profiles appeared side by side, each described by six factors: region of origin, education level, gender, age, English proficiency, and whether they were seeking economic opportunity or asylum. The research subject would have to choose which of the migrants they preferred to admit to the U.S.

The computer randomly presented 30 different sets of hypothetical migrants. If a participant consistently chose the younger migrant or the migrant with greater English skills, for example, the researchers could conclude that that was the most important factor for that participant.

The same decision framework was presented for comparing potential climate change policies and fictional U.S. Senate candidates.

The research did yield some insights about basic priorities: Participants preferred younger political candidates. They preferred climate change policy that minimized economic impact on their household. They preferred immigrants who were seeking asylum as opposed to seeking economic opportunity, and those who were below retirement age.

But that wasn’t the primary purpose of the study. Banavar and Bansak were less interested in what participants decided, and more interested in how they decided.

Thoughtful reflection or hot takes?

The results were striking: In 68% of the cases, study participants used shortcuts and did not deeply assess all six factors when choosing between hypothetical Senate candidates, migrants, or climate policies.

Nearly 54% showed a pattern of making choices dominated by one of the six factors. Another 14% took a different shortcut: Given six factors for each candidate or policy, they counted the total number of factors that they preferred for each, then chose the one containing the greater number.

How many used a comprehensive evaluation of all six factors—a rational assessment, without any shortcuts? Just under 30%.

Interestingly, Banavar said, a majority of participants did not consistently stay with the same strategy. Some might rely on just one factor in choosing a climate policy, while more fully weighing all the factors in deciding which migrant to admit.

“You can’t put people into buckets to say, ‘OK, this is someone who’s actually only going to care about only one thing,’” she said. “People are actually relating differently to each of these different domains and choosing to use different levels of effort, for want of a better term.”

To understand why people shift strategies will require further research, she said.

The study comes with some important caveats: If a participant favored female candidates, for example, or migrants who were seeking political asylum, and if both choices had those attributes, the participants had to consider other factors. That reduced, but did not eliminate, the pattern of making a choice based on one factor.

But when the researchers asked the participants to rate each profile before choosing one of them, the participants were more inclined to consider all factors in making their choices.

Further, the researchers allowed for only two attributes in their comparisons—male or female, for example, white or Black, or aged in their 50s or 70s. That’s not ideal, Banavar said, but to include multiple races, for example, is too complex for the cognitive model, and would dilute the patterns in the data. In the choice of Senate candidates, the study did not include the fictional candidates’ political parties. That question could provoke such strong, reflexive reactions that it would cloud the findings, she said.

Implications for real-world politics

The authors say their research has important implications not just for cognitive research, but for real-world, on-the-ground U.S. political processes.

Often, Bansak said, our understanding of public opinion is based on surveys that ask people to choose between complex options defined by many factors. The new research shows analysts that individuals often base their opinions on a single factor, and that can create a clearer picture of what people want in public policy, and why they want it.

“This bears directly on how we interpret the kind of public-opinion and preference data that routinely informs policy debates and news coverage,” he said.

And that understanding, Banavar said, can shape the policy process—and political communication. If leaders present issues in layers of complexity, “how much are people going to read or listen to that?” she asked. “What is the thing that’s really important to them, given a particular issue? That’s completely lost when you don’t think about the decision-making process.”

Traversing the Saiga Silk Road

📅 Aug 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

As we skidded off the main road and onto a mud path maintained only by tire treads, lines began to blur.

Flanking our two-track trail were a series of parallel lanes no more than a foot wide, which forked, merged, and crisscrossed our own. In the distance, I saw what looked like a ghost train, faintly visible and thrust forward by howling Siberian winds.

Through borrowed binoculars, I soon got my first glimpse of an ancient tableau, seemingly untouched since Neanderthals roamed the earth. On the far horizon, hundreds of nomadic travelers pierced the frigid air with their ringed horns, inhaling the brittle shards through Snuffleupagus snouts as they sprinted across the desolate steppe.

Watching them charge through the treeless plain at full bore, I was reminded of a saying I’d heard shortly before arriving in this remote corner of Kazakhstan: “The saiga is a freedom-loving animal.”

Since time immemorial, saiga antelope have blazed trails across Central Asia while other civilizations rose and fell around them. They once inhabited a world dominated by woolly mammoths, saber-toothed cats, and giant deer with antlers 12 feet across—and outlived them all. When pioneering human caravans first attempted to navigate this unforgiving land, they considered the saigas to be their guides.

Ahead of my arrival, seasonal rains transformed once-green grasslands into sludge punctuated by tufts of withering brush. Driving through such muck is, I would soon discover, akin to controlled hydroplaning. The steering wheel bucked wildly as our tires swerved, shredding deep into the gunk and desperately gripping anything remotely solid enough to push off from.

In the front seats were Alexandr and Albert, two team members from the Association for the Conservation of Biodiversity of Kazakhstan, or ACBK. Between them, they have more than twenty-five years of experience following saigas across the steppe, and I’d arranged to join them for the western leg of one of their field audits.

Our visit was timed to coincide with the rutting season, a critical part of the year for saigas. This is a gathering time, when antelope from across the region sprint together in massive single-file lines, before congregating for a series of competitive battles and subsequent mating rituals.

For males, it’s a time for strutting and headbutting to demonstrate one’s physical prowess to female onlookers—an activity so energy-intensive that many will forget to eat and keel over from extreme physical exhaustion. For females, this is a time of choosing. In addition to assessing fitness, they pay particular attention to one distinctive feature: a potential mate’s floppy, elongated nose. The saigas’ sniffers are essential for filtering out dust in the semidesert plains and for warming frigid winter air before it reaches their lungs. A large nose is the ultimate sign of virility, so as the males marched into these annual gatherings, they were doing everything they could to accentuate their schnozzes.

Because of the saigas’ preference for large-scale migrations across a landscape almost entirely devoid of trees, they have long been a favorite of global wildlife trackers. The isolation and starkness of the saiga world is a strength when it comes to GPS collars, which deliver a reliable stream of daily pings without risk of visual obstruction. Prior to such breakthroughs, conservationists would sometimes spend weeks roaming the boundless steppe unsuccessfully trying to locate the nomadic herds.

The wildlife sanctuary we were traveling through was created only a year prior, with its boundaries informed by tracking data that Albert and his team personally collected. He seemed pleased that the animals were continuing to use the newly designated space as intended. Standing along the roadside, Alexandr spotted a stream of the famously skittish ungulates wrapping around the contours of the earth. As he did, ripping wind and snow started blowing in sideways—the outer ring of an encroaching storm.

These were no fluffy flakes. This was the harsh, crunchy ice of a walk-in freezer, which instantly hardened our truck’s caked-on mud like liquid nitrogen.

Seemingly unperturbed by the dropping barometer, Albert chirped, “Who’s hungry?” Setting up an impromptu picnic, he and Alexandr dug into a prepared meal of fresh bread, tea, and dried meats of unknown provenance. In a muted attempt to thaw my face, I opted to guzzle spicy instant noodles.

A tradition of nomadism has loomed large in this corner of the world since at least the Early Iron Age. The very word kazakh is thought to be derived from the Turkic word qaz, which means “wanderer” or “vagabond.” For millennia, small human groups routinely migrated to higher altitudes in the hot summer months, where melting snow made for abundant water and greenery, before returning to sheltered valleys ahead of winter’s whipping winds. At various times of the year, disparate nomads would also congregate for formal meetings and large cultural festivals, complete with physical challenges like wrestling and tug-of-war. When the gatherings were finished, the nomads dispersed once again.

Historical analysis has shown that nomads’ seasonal migrations and time-honed transit corridors may have helped pave the way for portions of the Great Silk Road, which was less a singular route than an ever-evolving web of pathways and bypasses that linked together reliable stopovers. At the Silk Road’s peak, this corner of western Kazakhstan along the fertile banks of the Ural River became a vital transcontinental crossroads. To this day, the river still serves as a natural boundary between Asia and Europe, meaning this part of Kazakhstan is, quite literally, where East meets West.

The land above the Caspian Sea also served as a main artery that linked together key transit points. Strategic settlements along the riverbank allowed weary caravans to rest their heads, unwind in large public baths, exchange knowledge, stock up on essential supplies, and regroup before moving onward.

Among overland voyagers traversing the continental divide, saigas have long been spoken of with an almost mythical reverence. Cave paintings of the antelope created as far back as the seventh century BCE depict them as resilient, elusive, and charismatic. Expert navigators who embody the true spirit of a most inhospitable terrain.

In human society, traditional nomadic practices persisted even as the land itself was variously claimed and reclaimed by Turkic tribes, Genghis Khan, and the Russian Empire. Only with the rise of the Soviet Union in the twentieth century did mandatory sedentarization efforts successfully manage to lock nomadic people in place, force them to farm, and extinguish a cultural flame that had burned brightly for millennia. Denied the ability to live in perpetual motion, people’s navigational muscles began to atrophy.

This transition was viewed by many as an inevitable shift toward “modernization,” but it also represented a significant diversion from our own history as a species. Of the roughly 300,000 years that Homo sapiens have existed, we spent the first 288,000 of them living in small, mobile groups that followed the migratory patterns of other animals. Forcing nomads into agricultural plots was also a colossal failure because much of the steppe soil was unsuitable for growing crops, which is why so many people were nomadic in the first place. Iconic filmmaker Werner Herzog once remarked that “whatever went wrong and makes our civilization something doomed is the departure from the nomadic life.”

Whether by choice or necessity, most Kazakh people now lead stationary lives in urban centers. The world that nomads once freely roamed has also been feverishly transformed, not only by agriculture but through the construction of new roads and railways.

For the saigas, whose nomadic days never ceased, these new layers now represent an existential threat.

With the weather taking a turn for the worse, Albert and Alexandr ultimately decided to call it a day and start fresh in the morning. After a blistering drive through whiteout conditions, we hunkered down in a tiny village less than 10 miles from the Russian border, where Albert pulled out his laptop and showed me how saiga movements have changed over the years.

“They would routinely trek hundreds of miles as part of an expansive migratory route across the Eurasian steppe,” he said. “Saigas could be found from the Caucasus Mountains to Mongolia, and everywhere in between.”

For millennia, they lived with few barriers and obstructions on the landscape. Then, seemingly overnight, rattling railways and roaring roads began to crisscross the region, fragmenting the saigas into smaller, more isolated groups. As the interior steppe got more accessible to vehicles and passengers, development begat more development.

On his screen, Albert clicked open a recent map that showed how a population of GPS-collared saigas once routinely traveled deep into Uzbekistan before being unceremoniously clipped off by a new rail line in 2014. The data from the years prior to the railway’s construction showed a flurry of squiggly lines representing individual animals zipping back and forth with the seasons. The post-construction slide showed those same animals spinning around despondently on the northern side of the tracks, while the fresh grasses and water they had come to rely on withered away to the south.

Such interruptions can lead to a complete collapse in spatial knowledge—a loss that science writer Ben Goldfarb once wrote is “as thorough as the erasure of a language.” Just as human nomads forced to stay in one place eventually lost connection to the physical landmarks of their ancestors’ journeys, young saigas learn where they can reliably find food and shelter, as well as places to stay away from, through active participation and observation. This cultural exchange keeps their world in orbit and is one of the reasons they have been successful at navigating a terrain fatal to so many before them. As the land they know is cleaved and partitioned, whole stretches of the Saiga Silk Road begin to fade from memory.

To unwind this damage, Albert’s team has partnered with the Kazakh government to construct dozens of saiga-friendly crossing points, where slightly sloping dirt ramps rise up to match the height of the train tracks, making it appear from a distance as though there’s a natural gap in the rail line. Cattle-guard fences positioned far away from the crossings ensure that trains and saigas never actually run into each other at these intersections, which would shatter the illusion of safety.

ACBK has also worked with the Uzbek government to reshape its border fences from a barbed-wire wall to an X-shaped barricade that blocks vehicles from driving through but leaves just enough space for saigas to pass. Over time, Albert hopes such efforts can rebuild the trust necessary for them to confidently return to a plateau they once relied on.

The next morning, I was astonished by how austere the steppe looked compared with the day before. Gone was the slippery muck and browning grasses of shifting seasons, replaced by a blanket of crunchy snow. Against this stark black-and-white backdrop, the fresh crystals functioned like fingerprint dust, illuminating the only remaining pops of color: the rust-red streaks of human and nonhuman pathways in the dirt.

Parked in an otherwise empty tract of prairie land, we watched in stunned silence as thousands of saigas seized the day, flying like banshees across the periphery, their hooves gouging the frozen crust and stamping pigment into an earthen canvas.

As I gazed east toward the colorless horizon, I tried to envision how it might have looked in the thirteenth century, when this corner of the world boasted a vast network of caravanserai, or road inns, where exhausted travelers could rest and recover before continuing onward into the great unknown.

I pondered what it must have felt like for an intrepid merchant to know that they would not see another human being for many days, maybe weeks. For there is life beyond humanity’s margins, and among nonhuman communities, that in-between space is the center of everything. That, I imagined, must have offered some measure of assurance that the road ahead, perilous though it may be, is well traveled.

What is implantation?

Implantation occurs when an egg travels through the Fallopian tube and is fertilised. The fertilised egg attaches itself to the lining of the uterus. This process signals to your body that it’s time to start producing the human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), the hormone that helps maintain a hospitable environment for early pregnancy.

When does implantation happen?

Implantation usually occurs between 6 and 12 days after ovulation, although the exact timing varies between women. The first signs of implantation usually occur within the first few weeks after fertilisation. According to an older study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, late implantation is associated with an increased risk of miscarriage.

It usually occurs within 6 to 12 days after ovulation, when a fertilized egg gradually embeds itself. For some pregnant women, common symptoms of light pink or dark brown spotting, or vaginal bleeding might occur; this is also referred to as implantation bleeding.

Implantation bleeding

Not all women experience implantation bleeding. According to a 2025 article published in the Journal of International Medical Research, implantation bleeding occurs in around a quarter of all pregnancies.

Implantation bleeding:

  • Is light spotting or light bleeding
  • Is pink or brown of varying shades (not bright red as experienced with your menstrual period)
  • Generally only lasts a day or two.

Implantation cramps

Implantation cramps are gentle cramps that occur around the time of ovulation. You might notice cramping in your lower back around the time of implantation. However, it’s easy to mix them up with PMS cramps, so it’s not a definite sign of a successful implantation.

Some women report tugging, tingling or mild abdominal pain around the time the embryo implants; however, it’s difficult to pinpoint the exact moment or to say with certainty that it’s the result of successful implantation.

Other symptoms of implantation

Remember, not all women experience implantation symptoms, so if you don’t experience implantation symptoms it doesn’t mean you haven’t conceived. It’s also worth keeping in mind that several signs of implantation are similar to PMS.

Other potential symptoms of implantation are:

  • Nausea
  • Fatigue
  • Mood swings
  • Tender breasts.

Can you feel implantation?

When implantation occurs, the zygote is only about 2 mm long so it’s unlikely you’ll feel it attaching to your uterine lining. Some women, however, experience implantation bleeding (light bleeding or spotting), cramping following implantation, which can indicate that implantation has occurred.

What are the earliest signs of implantation?

Implantation bleeding, implantation cramps, nausea, fatigue and sore breasts are usually the earliest implantation symptoms. Implantation symptoms generally occur at the time of implantation or shortly afterwards, which is usually about 6 to 12 days post-ovulation. However, these symptoms can easily be mixed up with symptoms of PMS, so you won’t know whether or not they are implantation symptoms until you take a home pregnancy test.

Can you have signs of pregnancy before implantation?

No. Technically, your body won’t know it’s pregnant until the fertilised egg embeds itself into the lining of your uterus. It is this process that triggers the hormonal changes associated with early pregnancy, such as rising hCG levels. Changing levels of pregnancy hormones, such as hCG, oestrogen and progesterone, are responsible for early pregnancy symptoms, such as morning sickness, nausea, mood changes and fatigue.

What are the four stages of implantation?

Four main stages occur during the process:

1. Migration/Hatching

After fertilisation, the egg nuclei (fusion of sperm and egg) transforms into a zygote and continues its migration down the Fallopian tube towards the uterine cavity. During this journey, the fertilised egg sheds its protective layer, becomes a blastocyst and prepares to embed in the uterine wall.

2. Apposition

During this stage, the blastocyst enters the uterus and makes first contact with the endometrium lining.

3. Adhesion

During adhesion, the blastocyst firmly attaches to the uterine lining and continues the cell division.

4. Invasion

Now that the blastocyst is firmly attached to the uterine lining, the cells begin to invade the endometrium.

How long does implantation take?

The implantation process time varies between pregnancies. Once the blastocyst enters the uterus, the process will be completed within a couple of days.
