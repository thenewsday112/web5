What is changing is the competitive environment surrounding these games. Through organized GameZone events, familiar card games can be presented through qualification rounds, rankings, tournament stages, and championship competitions.

For 2026, Tongits provides the clearest example of this approach through the Tongits Plus Champions Cup (TPCC) and the GZone Tour. Pusoy and Pusoy Dos are also included in GameZone’s card-game ecosystem, while PokerPlus provides a poker-focused option.

Understanding How GameZone Events Work

GameZone events can include organized competitions and promotional activities, with individual formats depending on the game and event.

A competitive card-game event may involve several components:

  • Qualification rounds
  • Tournament-specific mechanics
  • Rankings or scoring
  • Advancement to later stages
  • Championship rounds
  • Prize pools or other rewards

The purpose of these elements is to create a defined competitive pathway. In casual play, each match is generally an individual experience. In a tournament circuit, however, performance can contribute to progression through different stages.

The GZone Tour and TPCC demonstrate this model particularly well because Tongits players can compete through qualifying stages before reaching higher-level competition.

Understanding the structure is therefore just as important as knowing the rules of the card game itself.

Why Tongits Leads the 2026 Competitive Lineup

Among the card games associated with GameZone events, Tongits currently has the most clearly documented competitive program for 2026.

The GZone Tour uses Tongits Plus as its competitive foundation. The first TPCC 2026 leg culminated in a live championship in March, bringing together 36 finalists.

The event featured a ₱10 million total prize pool, including a ₱5 million prize for the champion. The championship was held at the PNB Events Hall in Pasay City.

These figures naturally attract attention, but the tournament structure is equally important. A competitive circuit gives players a framework for progressing from qualification to higher-level competition rather than treating every game as a completely separate contest.

This also introduces additional considerations for tournament preparation. Players need to understand the relevant mechanics, monitor rankings when applicable, and become familiar with the format used in competitive rounds.

Chance remains part of card gaming, so no amount of preparation can guarantee a particular result. What preparation can do is help players understand the rules and make more informed decisions during competition.

The GZone Tour and the Development of Competitive Tongits

The GZone Tour gives competitive Tongits a more organized structure.

Rather than focusing on one isolated tournament, a tour can connect qualifying competitions, rankings, and championship-level events. This provides players with a clearer understanding of how individual performances relate to the broader competition.

For a game as familiar as Tongits, this structure is significant. The game itself does not need to be reinvented. Filipino players already know it. What organized competition changes is the way performance is presented and measured.

A formal tournament framework can provide:

  • More clearly defined competitive stages
  • Consistent tournament mechanics
  • Ranking systems
  • Documented results
  • Live championship rounds
  • A more visible pathway for tournament players

GameZone has indicated that additional TPCC legs will take place in 2026. Players should still wait for official announcements before relying on specific dates, venues, qualification requirements, or registration details.

This is particularly important because tournament information can change between stages. An earlier announcement should not automatically be treated as the final schedule for a later competition.

What to Know About GameZone Pusoy Events

Pusoy and Pusoy Dos occupy another part of GameZone’s card-game ecosystem. Both games are familiar to Filipino players, making them natural candidates for organized competitive formats.

However, readers interested in GameZone Pusoy tournaments should understand the difference between an officially listed game and a confirmed tournament.

  • The official tournament announcement
  • Confirmed dates
  • Registration periods
  • Eligibility requirements

No, it isn’t too late.

People in their 70s, 80s, and 90s get meaningfully stronger when they start strength training, and beginners tend to see the biggest response to training.

You also need far less than you’d guess: 30 to 60 minutes of lifting spread across a week is a great starting point.

Here’s the science, three ways to begin, and a client who started at 57 and is still going at 77.

A reader wrote me this week. Her whole email was one sentence:

“Is it too late if you’re a 71-year-old woman?”

That hits deep. That fear of: have I missed my window?

I want to share with you what I shared with her, because the science behind this is actually super cool and empowering.

Heck no, it isn’t too late! 

What happens when you start strength training later in life

Your body doesn’t have a cutoff age.

People in their 70s, 80s, and 90s still get stronger when they start lifting. And it meaningfully improves how they move through their lives.

And if you’re someone who hasn’t trained before, or hasn’t trained in a while, I have even better news.

As a beginner, your body is even more sensitive to training. Translation: you get even more reward for your effort!

In the first few weeks, most people feel stronger before they look any different.

That’s because a lot of early strength is actually your muscles and brain learning to coordinate. The movement gets smoother. Standing up from a chair feels a little easier. Etc.

Over a few months, muscle starts to build.

Your bones respond to the load too (strength training is one of the best-supported ways to support bone health as we age.)

Balance gets better.

You’ll start to feel some muscle in your arms that you didn’t notice there before, like this reader who’s been doing workouts 3x/week and kicking butt with it:

“Sometimes I’ll scratch my arm and be shocked because it doesn’t feel like my arm. My arm is flabby, what is this muscle that I’m feeling?”

Is every body different? Yes. Does it also depend on sleep, nutrition, and what else life is throwing at you? Also yes.

But the direction is the same for everyone. No matter your age, you can make a meaningful impact in your overall health, fitness, and quality of life!

How to start strength training after 70: three ideas to get you going

1) Don’t start with workouts. Start with practice.

Take a few minutes each day to learn and practice a couple of strength moves.

You’re not training yet. You’re getting acquainted.

Try some push-ups off of the wall or the countertop.
​
Sit down in a chair, slow and controlled, and practice keeping your weight evenly spread across your feet (that’s a squat right there!)

Grab a gallon jug, some light dumbbells or canned goods or whatever you have on hand, and practice a one-arm row. See if you can feel it in your upper back as you pull the weight up.

Work within whatever your ability level is, what feels safe and approachable to start.

You’re collecting information about what your body is ready for, and learning exercises like you’re learning a new skill.

2) It takes way less than you think.

30-60 minutes of strength training in TOTAL across the week goes a surprisingly long way.

You can break that up however works best for you. A short 5-10min session most days of the week, inserting some “movement snacks” throughout the day, or a couple of longer sessions 2x/week, etc.

It doesn’t require hours of your day to make serious progress.

3) It’s easier to level up than to cut down

Because your body is more sensitive to strength training, you’ll likely feel more sore in the beginning.

So in general, I recommend you err on the side of less is more.

If you do a few short sessions during the week, and wake up the next day feeling great with little to no soreness, great! You can add a bit more the next week.

Mentally, I’ve also just found it’s way easier to be “leveling up” than having to cut back, which really helps with building that early momentum.

Becky started at 57. She’s 77 now.

NF also does a great job in helping me modify workouts, especially when I’ve had injuries (I’ve had total knee replacement and physical therapy for knees and shoulders.)” ​ “Learning more about what makes me tick from NF has lead to success in my decision to keep moving as I age. You’re never too old to exercise.”

I couldn’t have said it any better myself, Becky.

No matter your age or where you’re starting from…YOU GOT THIS!

Max Greenfield Is That Dad

📅 Aug 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The first thing you may observe about the dad in Netflix’s upcoming teen drama Don’t Say Good Luck is that his hair looks perfect. That’s only the start of the aspirational cues coming from this character played by Max Greenfield. In this funny — and, yes, tear-jerking — family drama, Greenfield’s character juggles work, elementary-aged twins, a big-feeling teen, and his wife’s cancer diagnosis. While he is at times off-kilter from all that life throws his way in the film, this dad does not bumble his way through it. In every scene, he finds ways to be the emotionally available parent and partner.

Greenfield says that understanding the character was easy. “There’s so much of me that’s in that movie,” he tells Fatherly. “I felt so close to what we were doing.” It could be, perhaps, because his daughter, like the main character, is a high schooler; she has mutual friends to the lead actress, Sunny Sandler; and her experience — at least onstage, navigating high school life — just resonated.

We sat down to give Max Greenfield the Cool Dad questionnaire, and then talked about the movie, finding patience, and fighting parental anxiety — and why LA’s bagels are seriously underrated.

This interview has been edited for brevity and clarity.

I finished watching Don’t Say Good Luck today. I definitely did some crying.

They keep playing clips in these interviews that I’m doing for press, and they’ll play it before the interview, and I’m constantly like, “I need a minute.”

It’s intense. It’s a wonderful film. I’m wondering how close of a dad are you to who you play on screen in Don’t Say Good Luck. Are you that dad?

I mean, there’s so much of me that’s in that movie. I felt so close to what we were doing. It was really the reason I wanted to do it. My daughter, I think, is a year younger than Sunny [Sandler, the lead], and I was really aware that this was going to be a movie that Sunny was starring in. And from everything that I had heard, I had never done a Sandler movie before, but his sets are famously the best. And so I really wanted to just make sure that she had a comparable experience on this with somebody else. I felt like I knew how to do that.

“I didn’t know if I was going to be very good in the movie as an actor, but I was like, ‘I think I know how to be the dad.’”

My daughter and her have mutual friends, I know where she goes to school… just a lot of the stuff I just was like, “I know this world well.” And so talking to somebody like Sunny is not an issue for me. And trying to be light and supportive in this environment, it was something I felt like I was capable of doing. I didn’t know if I was going to be very good in the movie as an actor, but I was like, “I think I know how to be the dad.” And then there’s the two young boys in the movie, the twins, and they were so funny because they were just never acting. They just were running around.

They’re a little bit younger than my son, but I just coached my son’s basketball team, and wrangling all those kids and getting them going, getting them involved, it was very good prep work for dealing with these two boys. And it made the scenes and made the movie so much fun.

It felt very real.

Yeah. Everything was just like, “Oh, this is what my real life is.” And being there with Sunny, I understood that relationship. Being there with the boys, I was like, “I get this relationship.”

There was a more dramatically real scene later in the movie … I personally had a similar experience to the lead character. My mom had cancer when I was a sophomore in high school, and all I could think about was how I wanted you — your character — to be my dad for that time. For example, that scene when you were sitting in the car and Sunny asked, “Are you okay?” And you asked, “Are you okay?” And both of you said, “No.” It was just… right.

That was a really cool scene. Julia Hart, who had directed the movie and wrote the movie along with Laura Hankin and Jordan Horowitz … [had] a lot of conversations about that scene, and they were so generous in my input. It really felt like that scene was going to be the scene for this relationship. And as we started to see how the movie was playing out and what was happening before then, we had sent a bunch of drafts of that scene back and forth to one another, and we all collectively landed in that spot because it really needed to set up everything that comes after that, but it needed to honor everything that was built up before it, and it needed to feel real.

I didn’t want there to be an answer or a fix to what was happening, but I wanted them to feel it, both characters. When we shot that scene, it was nuts. It was really heavy, man.

In this movie, you run a bagel shop. So what’s your order?

Oh, wow. Great question. I’ve probably gotten older and more healthy than I need to be, more LA than I need to be. I’ll give you two. One’s an everything bagel [with] just cream cheese, onion, tomato, lox.

Netflix

Respect.

That can’t ever miss, it’s the best. The other one is … either a light cream cheese spread [or] the right type of hummus and then just veggies. But fresh, good veggies that are with the right type of dressing on it and salt and pepper. There’s a place in LA that has been hyped and hyped and I finally went there, I don’t know, a month or two ago. It’s called Courage Bagels. And the line is a mile long. And I was like, “I’m not waiting in line.” And then my daughter and I were like, “Let’s go wait in line.” So we went over there and they had one, I think it’s called the Garden or something. I don’t know. Holy sh*t. It was unbelievable.

One of the big themes of the movie I wanted to get your take on is disclosure to children. That is, what you tell a kid, what you don’t tell a kid, when you’re protecting them from the truth, and when that backfires. I was just wondering if you had thoughts on how you strike that personally.

It’s a really good question. I actually feel like I’ve done a good job with this, personally. There are certain things that I want them to discover on their own, and hopefully my job is to have created a space where they feel comfortable to then bring those things up to me. And then depending on how the question’s asked or how much they want to know, you ease them into certain topics of discussion and what you choose to discover based on what they’re asking. And you let that conversation be ongoing. And so I’ve had some real good success in some areas where those conversations have opened up and become more in-depth over time. And as a result, I see that my kids are way more comfortable with information that I was worried might be confusing or scary. And because they’ve sort of been able to take it in at their pace.

How has your outlook changed now that you’re past the baby and elementary parenting and you have a 16- and 11-year-old?

I’m making a lot more mistakes. That thing we talked about, responsibility versus what to let go, that line is thinner and thinner every day. And there’s so many times where, what are the things that I feel like I need to control to make me feel safer in this situation that is so scary? And being a fear-based parent is not a good zone to operate from.

“When I’m sitting in a car with my daughter and she’s driving, this is real fear. This is what real fear feels like.”

And so it’s like my daughter’s now driving and she gets her driver’s license and it’s just like, my wife and I are both like, “Well, so the next day, is she just driving to volleyball practice? Is that how that works?” And if you strip all the fear away from it, you’re like, “Yeah, she practiced and worked up to it and has now passed the test and she’s allowed to legally drive. So yes, why wouldn’t she be able to?” There’s a period of time when they’re small where you’re like, “Yeah, this is just going to keep getting better.” And then it shifts in the most terrifying way and you’re like, “Oh, this is all so much worse.”

When we were [learning to drive], I would sit in the car with her, we would drive, and I would go, “Oh, you know what? All the anxiety and fear I have about the future and life and work and all the nonsense, that’s not real.” When I’m sitting in a car with my daughter and she’s driving, this is real fear. This is what real fear feels like. It’s like, this is the shark in the water kind of fear. And it’s heavy and it’s tangible. And you almost hold it and you go, “This is what real fear feels like, and this is appropriate for the situation.” So outside of that, when I do feel any sort of anxiety about the future or whatever it is, I go, “Oh, this is not real.” And now I know the difference. So at least I have that.
