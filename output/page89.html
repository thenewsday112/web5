Holly Beck Exposes Surfing’s Toxic Past of Sexism and Abuse

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Surfing has never been short on machismo. But as more women describe what happened behind the scenes, the sport is confronting a much uglier side of its history.

Former professional surfer Holly Beck has added her voice to the growing conversation, sharing accounts of sexual harassment, sexism, and mistreatment she says she experienced during her career. She took to Instagram, to share her experiences.

See her post, linked here, and below.

View this post on Instagram

Beck, who competed professionally for more than a decade, has described how men with influence in the surf industry suggested they could help advance her career in exchange for sex. She says those encounters began when she was just 17, an age when sponsorships and access to powerful industry figures could shape a young surfer’s future.

The problems went beyond unwanted advances. Women of Beck’s generation were frequently marketed for their looks rather than their surfing. A 2002 newspaper profile described her early sponsorship as being “more as a model than a surfer.” Beck has also recalled being told her place in the lineup ranked below even the groms.

In one harrowing memory, when she was just 18 years old, Beck recalled being on the North Shore of Oahu when: “I came home late from a party, passed out in bed, and woke up to a surf shop owner on top of me trying to take my pants off…”

At 24 years old, terrified of losing her sponsors and derailing her livelihood, Beck recounted a surf photographer attempting to get a group of girls naked during a trip.

“You don’t want to know,” she was warned at the time.

She described an industry where young women were systematically commodified, treated as disposable sexual objects, and forced to tolerate foul misconduct simply to survive.

Her experiences speak to a culture in which women often had to fight for respect in the water while navigating the expectations of men who controlled opportunities outside it.

And the conversation is unfolding amid another, far more serious controversy.

As SURFER has reported, Mexican Olympian Alan Cleland Jr. is linked to a criminal investigation in French Polynesia concerning an alleged sexual assault of a 16-year-old Australian girl at a private gathering following August’s Tahiti Pro.

The World Surf League and International Surfing Association have issued provisional suspensions. No arrests or criminal charges have been publicly announced, and Cleland is presumed innocent.

Separately, his former girlfriend, professional surfer Noah Klapp, has publicly accused him of abuse during their past relationship. Those allegations have not been adjudicated.

Surfing’s history of sexism is hardly a revelation. What’s different now is the number of women speaking openly, demanding accountability, and questioning the systems that allowed troubling behavior to go unchallenged.

The question is whether this moment leads to meaningful change—or simply becomes another controversy the surf world leaves behind.

#761: 3 Million Americans Got Rich From Boring Businesses. Here’s How, with Owen Zidar & Eric Zwick

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Dick Portillo started his hot dog business with about $1,000 and a shack that had no running water. He washed the dishes in his family’s bathtub. Decades later, he sold the company for roughly a billion dollars, which makes him one of the starkest examples of how boring businesses quietly create American wealth.

He’s not an outlier. Economists Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick spent more than a decade in the Treasury Department matching anonymized tax returns to the private businesses behind them. They found roughly 3 million private business owners worth at least $5 million each, who together hold more than ten times the wealth of the entire Forbes 400.

These aren’t tech founders or Wall Street tycoons. They own beer distributors, car dealerships, HVAC companies, dental practices and trash-bag-liner manufacturers. These boring businesses typically have 10 to 50 employees and took decades to build.

Most of these owners didn’t start out rich, either. The majority of founders behind successful businesses grew up in families outside the top 10% of earners, and only about 23% of top-earning business owners inherited their companies.

Owen and Eric join the show to talk about their book, The Everywhere Millionaire, and what it means for anyone thinking about starting or buying one of these boring businesses. They cover how to find an edge, why retiring baby boomers are opening up opportunities, and how to run small experiments without betting the house.

Key Takeaways

The biggest ideas from the conversation

+
  • Private business owners hold more wealth than billionaires: Roughly 3 million private business owners have a net worth of at least $5 million, averaging about $25 million each. Individually they’re nowhere near Elon Musk, but as a group they hold more than ten times the combined wealth of the Forbes 400.
  • The richest Americans often run boring businesses: Beer distributors, car dealers, HVAC companies, mid-market manufacturers, dentists and lawyers fill the ranks of the top 1%. These boring businesses usually have 10 to 50 employees and $10 million to $50 million in revenue, and they drive more than half the rise in top 1% income share.
  • The edge comes from years of experience: Most owners spend years learning an industry first. Larry Miller, for example, worked in a Toyota parts department before he bought dealerships. The average founder of a star business is in their late 30s or early 40s, and the typical owner is in their late 50s or early 60s.
  • Retiring baby boomers are selling their businesses: A wave of baby boomer owners want to retire, and many of their kids don’t want the business. Buying one of these boring businesses means the idea is already tested, and seller financing can keep the previous owner invested in its success.
  • Fund experiments with profits, not debt: Successful owners focus on profitability early and reinvest cash flow instead of borrowing, so no single failed experiment wipes them out. Reinvesting through the business also reduces taxable profits, which makes growth cheaper than pulling money out and investing it elsewhere.
  • Concentration builds wealth; diversification protects it: Star business owners stay deeply concentrated in one business for years and carry a lot of idiosyncratic risk. Zidar and Zwick suggest diversifying by moving profits outside the business into retirement accounts, index funds or real estate, and keeping enough of a cushion to cover basic household needs.

Copycat Taco Bell Caramel Apple Empanadas

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 1-1/2 cups finely chopped peeled tart apples
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/8 teaspoon ground allspice
  • 1/3 cup thick caramel apple dip
  • 2 sheets refrigerated pie crust
  • 1 large egg yolk
  • 2 tablespoons water
  • 1/4 cup butter, melted
  • 1 cup cinnamon sugar
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. In a small saucepan, combine apples, sugar, cinnamon and allspice. Cook until apples are tender, 4-5 minutes. Drain; discard liquid. Transfer to a small bowl. Add caramel; stir to coat. Scoop by tablespoonfuls onto waxed paper. Freeze until firm, about 1 hour.
  2. Unroll pie crust; cut into 14 circles using a 4-in. circle cookie cutter (rerolling dough scraps as needed).
  3. In a small bowl, whisk together egg yolk and water. Brush edges of crusts with egg wash; place apple filling in the center. Fold crusts over filling and seal edges well with a fork. Place 2 in. apart on parchment-lined baking sheets. Freeze until firm, about 30 minutes.
  4. Brush remaining egg wash over tops. Cut 2 small slits on the top of each empanada.
  5. Bake at 425° for 12-14 minutes or until golden brown. Remove from oven and brush with melted butter. Carefully toss in cinnamon sugar. Serve warm.

Nutrition Facts

1 piece: 234 calories, 13g fat (6g saturated fat), 29mg cholesterol, 159mg sodium, 28g carbohydrate (14g sugars, 0 fiber), 2g protein.

Cherry Peach Pie with Salted Pecan Streusel

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 1-1/3 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1-1/2 teaspoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon pumpkin pie spice
  • 4 tablespoons shortening, cubed
  • 4 tablespoons cold butter, cubed
  • 3 to 4 tablespoons water
  • TOPPING:
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 cup packed brown sugar
  • 1/4 cup crushed gingersnap cookies
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup old-fashioned oats
  • 1/2 cup chopped pecans
  • 1/4 cup butter, melted
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • FILLING:
  • 2 cups sliced peeled peaches
  • 1 cup frozen pitted dark sweet cherries, thawed
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • 1/3 cup sugar
  • 1/3 cup packed brown sugar
  • 4 tablespoons potato starch
  • Pinch salt
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. In a large bowl, mix flour, sugar, salt and pie spice; cut in shortening and butter until crumbly. Gradually add ice water, tossing with a fork until dough holds together when pressed. Shape into a disk; wrap and refrigerate 1 hour or overnight.
  2. Preheat oven to 400°. On a lightly floured surface, roll dough to a 1/8-in.-thick circle; transfer to a 9-in. pie plate. Trim crust to 1/2 in. beyond rim of plate; flute edge. Refrigerate while preparing filling.
  3. For the topping, in a medium bowl, whisk together flour, sugars, crushed cookies and salt. Stir in oats and pecans. Add the butter and vanilla until small clumps form. Set aside.
  4. In a large bowl, toss peaches and cherries with lemon juice and vanilla. In a separate bowl, whisk together sugars, potato starch and salt. Add to fruit, toss until well coated.
  5. Transfer filling to prepared crust. Sprinkle with reserved topping; pressing down to adhere as needed. Bake for 20 minutes. Reduce oven setting to 350°, bake until crust and topping are golden brown and fruit is bubbly, 20-25 minutes longer. Cool completely on a wire rack.

Nutrition Facts

1 piece: 503 calories, 23g fat (9g saturated fat), 31mg cholesterol, 563mg sodium, 70g carbohydrate (38g sugars, 2g fiber), 5g protein.

Darkbloom, which allowed Mac users to rent out their GPUs for AI inference, warns of low payouts

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Declining token prices have prompted Darkbloom, a distributed inference network, to reassess its business plans.

Darkbloom, a project from Seattle-based tech biz Eigen Labs, has developed software that allows anyone with a sufficiently capable macOS device to rent their GPU through the Darkbloom network for interference jobs. In return, they get paid.

Through this network, 800 or so participants – including as of two days ago this reporter – have been able to collect a few cents to a few dollars per day by having their hardware handle AI model prompts submitted through OpenRouter, an AI model gateway.

In theory, someone running one of Apple's recently released Mac minis with an M5 Pro chip and 64 GB of RAM could earn as much as $192 per month handling incoming inference jobs, per the company's estimate.

That assumes full 24/7 utilization during this period on a sought-after model like Qwen3.6 35B A3B, which sells for $0.065/M input tokens, $0.75/M output tokens. But not every network participant has fully surrendered their Mac hardware to the network.

The median earnings of the 50 people on the leaderboard amount to about $5.26 per day, $159.86 per month, or $1,918.50 per year. So the best case estimate presented by Darkbloom looks plausible. But it may not be sustainable.

The price of tokens – the unit of AI work – has declined, reportedly down 54 percent since May. Ramp's AI Token Price Index, which tracks the price of tokens from Anthropic and OpenAI, shows similar slippage. For Anthropic, the price per million tokens went from $2.65 on May 1, 2026 to $0.62 on October 22, 2026, a decline of 77 percent.

In a message shared with Darkbloom participants on the company's Slack channel, Kydo, one of the Eigen Labs researchers working on the distributed inference network, celebrated the network's processing of its trillionth token while attempting to moderate expectations about the project's viability.

"Earnings have been low," said Kydo. "For most providers, I don't expect that it is going to recover soon."

Pointing to the computational prowess of DeekSeek's DeepInfer framework, he explained that structural changes in the inference market have affected the supply of tokens.

"Data centers have become cheaper," he said. "Output per NVIDIA cluster is up ~20x this year, and their margins are wide enough to cut prices 90 percent and still profit at 50 percent."

The flood of tokens in turn has washed away the price advantage Darkbloom had previously. "The market caught up to our price," Kydo explained. "We could go lower, but then most of you would earn even less nothing."

Finally, he said, the inference routing market is changing, with OpenRouter moving away from price-based routing and putting more emphasis on decode speed, quantization, and prefill speed.

For network participants with older or less powerful Mac hardware – those without top of the line Max or Ultra Apple Silicon – "inference won't be worth running," he said.

Kydo went on to outline how Darkbloom aims to adapt to the commoditized token market. This involves analyzing model demand to steer participants toward more sought-after models, and looking at ways to provide other kinds of compute work, like distributed CI/CD jobs.

For Mac hardware, the focus now appears to be on clusters of high-end Mac Studios (now available with up to 512 GB of RAM), using Apple's Remote Direct Memory Access (RDMA) over Thunderbolt.

The CI/CD work, based on the observation that AI agents now make pull requests faster than CI/CD pipelines can handle them, will require sandboxing — something the biz expects to begin testing soon. And Darkbloom also aims to improve the performance of its network (in terms of time to first token) and to look for other token buyers beyond OpenRouter.

"The next stretch will be a fight, and I expect many machines will leave and Slack will get quieter," Kydo concluded. "But those who stay will be first in line when new earning opportunities come along. We are a driven team and well capitalized. We will not give up." ®

Piolets d'Or Insider: How the Jury Works, Style Debates, and Future Plans

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The recent 2026 edition of the Piolets d'Or awards, held last week in a remote corner of the French Alps, felt like an organization trying to adapt to fast-changing times and attract larger audiences while keeping its prestige and spirit intact.

ExplorersWeb was there to meet old acquaintances and to put a face to some of the names we wrote about in the last 12 months. We also wanted to participate in the collective exercise of reflection about the future.

Views of Les Ecrins Massif from Saint-Christophe-en-Oisans. Photo: Angela Benavides

Alpine-style experts debate

Piolet d'Or recipients enjoyed a relaxed atmosphere at the tiny village of Saint-Christophe-en-Oisans and a ceremony at Burg d'Oisans, a 30-minute drive down the valley. Yet, the most interesting event took place the following day, as an elite group of climbers sat around a table in a small hall with large glass windows facing autumn-colored forests and the ragged peaks of the Ecrins Massif, covered in the first snow dust of the season.

Representing the awardees were Nicolas Jean (awarded for climbing Jannu East with Benjamin Vedrines); the complete teams from Ultar Shar, Aikache Chokk, and Jerupaja Grande; a hyper-energetic, widely smiling Peter Habeler, 84, who received a lifetime achievement Piolet d'Or; Simon Welfringer, who gave a talk; organizers Christian Trommsdorf and David Chambre; geographer and chronicler Rodolphe Popier, and two members of the jury: Jordi Corminas of Spain and Paul Ramsden of the UK. At a press conference, these experts debated some of the hot issues in today's mountaineering.

Simon Welfringer of France speaks at the post-Piolets d'Or experts' panel. Photo: Angela Benavides

How the jury works

Trommsdorf noted that the jury had an easy time selecting the awarded climbs this year. He explained how these expert jurists choose the best alpine-style activities of the year.

The members work remotely, choosing from a Big List of outstanding activities. This list is previously compiled by Lindsay Griffin, long-time editor of the American Alpine Journal, and the UK's Alpine Club. Jurors study the climbs and narrow it down to a short list of candidates, which is not made public. The awards go to those activities chosen by the majority of jury members. In this case, decisions were finalized on the first vote, with no need for a tie-break.

Left to right, Rodolphe Popier, Jordi Corominas, Paul Ramsden (speaking), and David Chambre. Photo: Piotr Drozdz

 

That led to the first topic of debate: It was noted that some climbing teams happily announce themselves as "Piolet d'Or nominates" on social media when their climbs appear on the Big List -- although organizers make it clear that not all activities on that list are suitable for a Piolet d'Or. Yet, no one supported the idea of making the short lists public. The general agreement was to keep the small "best of the year" finalists available only to the jury.

Drones

The second topic for debate was the presence of a relatively new piece of technology now ubiquitous on expeditions: drones. These have become essential on rescues, but climbing teams also use them to scout the route ahead. In the near future, drones could also carry supplies, emergency kits, or extra equipment. Commercial outfitters are already using them in this way.

Benjamin Vedrines and Nicolas Jean of France used drones to check the state of their planned route on Jannu. However, they explained that the drone was useless in relaying information about a section that they couldn't see from Base Camp. It had a different angle of view, and the drone lost connection when it tried to approach that area.

Drones piloted by their base camp film crew also hovered overhead as they traversed the long summit ridge. Finally, before the summit push, they used drones to clearly identify the highest point, to make sure they reached the true summit.

The Ultar Sar team also had a drone, they noted. All the awarded climbers agreed that drones are okay as long as the climbers are completely transparent about what they used them for. Jordi Corominas said the drones are a technological innovation that has come to stay, no different than InReach devices, satphones, or even ice axes and crampons.

Jordi Corominas, left, discusses past climbs with Benjamin Vedrines. Photo: Piotr Drozdz

 

Interestingly, one jurist stood against the tide: Paul Ramsden is an advocate for the pure alpine-style criteria of the UK climbing school, and with five Piolets d'Or, he is the overall record holder of these prestigious awards. In his opinion, drones spoil the adventure and uncertainty inherent in pure alpine style. Ramsden himself uses neither drones, trackers, nor even satphones.

Expanded Piolets

Finally, Trommsdor suggested possibly expanding the awards to create a special Piolet d'Or for the best rock climb and perhaps even the best ski descent. The idea expand on the new performance category introduced this year in the big list as a test. The category featured, among others, the two Everest ski descents in 2025. Both were heavily supported, and Jim Morrison's descent of the Hornbein Couloir was oxygen-assisted.

In the end, one of the performance ski descents received the female award: Typhaine Duperier's alpine-style climb+ski descent of Nanga Parbat's Schell route, with Boris Langenstein.

It is unclear whether the new additions will become official in the near future, but some attendees expressed concern about the context in which these take place. Would they award ski expeditions for their boldness or technical excellence without bearing in mind how the skiers reached the start of their descent (supported climbs, fixed routes, even helicopters)?

As for rock climbs: where to set the difference between a rock climb and an alpine climb on high, but dry, faces? The debate was left open. We will find out what, if anything, changes in the next edition of the Piolets d'Or.

Buried houses at the now abandoned village of La Berarde. Photo: Piotr Drozdz

No more ice axe

The physical award is no longer an actual piolet (ice axe). In the last few years, it has been a hand-crafted trophy that changes with the venue. This time, it was a plaque with a reproduction of Ecrins National Park's most distinctive peak, La Meige. It rested on a piece of rock from the remains of La Berarde, a nearby village destroyed in a flood two years ago.

At the Piolets d'Or ceremony in Burg d'Oisans. Photo: Angela Benavides

 

Next year, the Piolets d'Or will be staged in the same location, perhaps in late summer. The event will commemorate the 150th anniversary of the first ascent of La Meige. The long-term plan is to turn the Piolet d'Or ceremony into a bigger, international mountain festival.
